What a CDRL actually is

A Contract Data Requirements List (CDRL) entry is a single line that answers four questions: what is being delivered, who has to deliver it, how often, and what format it must take. Each CDRL usually points back to a Data Item Description (DID): a DoD-standard template that defines the required content and structure for that deliverable type. You almost never see 'DID' spelled out in a Task Order. Instead you'll see language like the deliverables table below, tied to specific SOW subtasks.

Acceptance & Rejection Timeline

This is the mechanical clock every CDRL runs on. Memorize this pattern. It repeats across nearly every federal services contract:

StepActionTimeframe
1Contractor submits deliverablePer Section F due date
2Government reviews and provides written acceptance, comments, or rejection15 workdays from receipt
3If comments/rejection issued, contractor incorporates changes and resubmits10 workdays from Government comments
4If non-conforming, contractor corrects deficiency or notifies Government of delay reason with a corrective action plan10 workdays from rejection notice

Unresolved non-conformance doesn't just sit there. It gets documented in the award fee determination report and directly reduces the fee the contractor earns for that period. This is the thread that connects CDRL performance to CPAF award fee scoring.

From Lucas The biggest mistake new PMs make with CDRLs: treating the 'Traces to SOW' column as decoration. It's not. If the Government ever asks why you're spending labor hours on something, your answer is always 'it maps to Deliverable #X, which traces to Task Y, Subtask Z.' Can't draw that line? Then you're probably doing unscoped work. And unscoped work on a cost-reimbursement contract is how PMs get fired.
