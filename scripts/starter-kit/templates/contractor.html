If your compliance matrix is from 2025, it is out of date

02 · Task orders vs. standalone contracts

Winning the vehicle is not winning the work

A standalone contract is one award for one defined requirement, with its own solicitation and evaluation. A task order is issued under an existing IDIQ (Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity) contract. The IDIQ sets the rules, the labor categories and a ceiling. The task order is where the actual work and the actual money live.

The one-sentence versionAn IDIQ is a hunting license. It lets you into the woods, but you still have to win each task order, and the guaranteed minimum on a very large IDIQ can be small enough to make the press release look silly.
Standalone contractSingle-award IDIQMultiple-award IDIQ
How orders are wonOne competition (full and open, or set aside)One competition for the vehicle, then orders go to the holderCompete for the vehicle, then compete again for each order
Revenue certaintyHigh once awardedHigh, but the vehicle is hard to winModerate: depends on your order win rate
Getting onModerate to hardVery hardModerate
BD after awardLowLowHigh and continuous
Typical length1 to 5 years5 to 10 years5 to 10 years
Common forUnique, well-defined workSpecialized capability, one qualified sourceBroad services and IT. The dominant model.

Fair opportunity, and its exceptions

On a multiple-award IDIQ, each holder must get a fair opportunity to compete for orders above the micro-purchase threshold. The traditional exceptions are narrow: an unusually urgent need, only one holder able to do specialized work, a logical follow-on to an earlier order, satisfying a minimum guarantee, a statute that expressly authorizes or requires a specific source, or a set-aside for small business.

Know them because they cut both ways. A competitor may get a sole-source order you expected to compete for, and your own firm may qualify for one in the right circumstances.

Check the versionThese rules sit in FAR 16.505 in the pre-overhaul numbering. Part 16 has been rewritten and is in the September 2026 proposed rules, and DoD already uses the rewritten text by deviation. The ideas are stable, but check the current cite before you put it in a protest or a proposal.

03 · When GSA buys for DoD

Some big DoD buys are run by a civilian agency

Assisted acquisition means one agency runs a procurement for another. GSA's Office of Assisted Acquisition Services (AAS) does this at scale, and DoD is a major customer. If you only watch DoD contracting offices, you will miss some of the biggest defense opportunities.

How it works

  • The DoD office signs an interagency agreement with GSA and transfers the funds. DoD must show that using another agency is in its interest.
  • GSA contracting officers run the solicitation, the source selection and the award, often on GSA vehicles such as OASIS+ or Alliant 3.
  • The DoD program office still owns the requirement and provides technical evaluators.
  • GSA charges the customer agency a fee for the service.

Why DoD uses it

  • Capacity: the program office lacks contracting staff for a large, complex buy.
  • Speed: a team that runs these every day can move faster than a local office doing its first one.
  • Complexity: big, multi-stakeholder IT and professional services buys with heavy documentation.
  • Defensibility: disciplined, well-documented source selections hold up better if protested.

The three AAS delivery models

ModelWhat it handles
FEDSIMThe Federal Systems Integration and Management Center. Large, complex, noncommercial buys on cost-type contracts, often for defense customers, with project management through performance.
FLEXFixed-price, time-and-materials and labor-hour buys of IT and professional services. AAS's highest-volume model.
INNOVATEResearch and development buys, including partnering with SBA to award SBIR Phase III contracts.

GSA reorganized its Federal Acquisition Service in May 2026. Assisted acquisition kept its mission under the new Office of Assisted Acquisition Services.

What it means for your pipeline

  • Search SAM.gov for GSA and FEDSIM postings, not only DoD offices. The customer's name is usually in the description.
  • Get on the GSA vehicles your customers use. For many of these buys, no vehicle means no bid.
  • Expect structured, documentation-heavy source selections. Write proposals that are easy to trace to the evaluation criteria.
  • Build relationships on both sides: the GSA contracting team runs the process, but the DoD program office defines "good".
  • Do not assume the incumbent wins. These competitions are designed to test current value and risk.

04 · Who's actually buying

"We want to sell to the Air Force" is not a strategy

No service is one buyer with one credit card. Contracting is split into layers with different authorities, contract sizes and mission focus. Aim at the wrong layer and a great proposal bounces off. The Air Force is the example here; the Army and Navy have the same three layers under different names.

Layer 1

Base-level contracting

  • A contracting squadron or office at each installation.
  • Day-to-day needs: facilities, local IT, services.
  • High volume, mostly simplified buys and BPAs.
  • The best place for a new firm to earn past performance.
Layer 2

Enterprise sourcing

  • For the Air Force, the Air Force Installation Contracting Center (AFICC), part of AFIMSC and headquartered at Wright-Patterson AFB.
  • Eight enterprise sourcing squadrons aligned to category management.
  • Consolidated, multi-year, multi-base buys with formal source selections.
Layer 3

Program and command

  • Program offices under PEOs, now becoming PAEs, and major command contracting.
  • Weapon systems, mission support, operational needs.
  • Often classified, cleared or overseas. Lower competition, higher barriers.
Base levelEnterprise sourcingProgram / command
Typical sizeMostly under $350K, some into the millions$1M to $100M+Millions to billions
How they buySimplified procedures, BPAs, small business set-asidesFormal source selections, orals, written proposalsFormal source selections, OTs, sole source where justified

Sizes are a rough guide, not a rule. Check real award history before you commit bid money.

Find your buyer in 30 minutesOn USAspending.gov, search your NAICS code and filter by awarding office to see who buys what you sell, from whom, and at what price. Then check SAM.gov for that office's sources sought notices and RFIs. Responding to those is how you shape a requirement before the RFP exists. If the office has paid $4M a year for work you cannot do under $7M, walk away before you spend on a proposal.

06 · OTs, CSOs and CMMC now

The fastest-growing doors in, and the one rule that did not pause

Other Transactions (OTs)

Agreements for prototypes, research and follow-on production that are not subject to the FAR or DFARS, though some statutes still apply. Faster and more flexible, with fewer standard clauses and more negotiation.

FY2024 OT obligationsOver $18B
FY2016, inflation-adjusted$1.8B
Share for prototypes, FY2021 to FY2024About 90%

GAO-25-107546, September 2025.

Many prototype OTs run through consortia, and a successful prototype can lead to a follow-on production award without a new competition. Join the consortia in your field and read their notices every week.

Commercial Solutions Openings (CSOs)

A way to buy innovative commercial products and services by asking for solutions to a problem, usually starting with a short white paper or pitch instead of a full proposal.

  • Default solicitation method for the software pathway since the March 2025 memo, along with OTs.
  • Used heavily by DIU and the service innovation units.
  • Good fit for firms with a working product and no long federal past performance record.

Commercial first is now the default expectation across DoD buys, not just software.

08 · Analogies for the hard parts

The ideas that trip up new contractors, in plain English

Good for onboarding new BD and proposal staff, and for explaining to your CFO why the government does what it does.

FAR and DFARS

The FAR is the league rulebook every federal agency plays by. The DFARS is the extra rulebook one team, DoD, follows on top, because its games carry higher stakes. And this season the league is rewriting the rulebook mid-game.

FFP vs. cost-type vs. T&M

FFP is a fixed quote for a kitchen remodel: $40,000 whether it takes three weeks or three months, the contractor's problem. Cost-type is paying the mechanic for time and parts with no firm total: the customer's risk. T&M is a freelancer by the hour with no end date: nothing stops the clock but the customer.

IDIQ

A hotel's corporate agreement. It sets the rates, but it does not put you in a room. The task order is the actual reservation, for specific dates, with money attached.

Undefinitized contract action

A restaurant with no prices on the menu. The customer is starving, orders everything, and then tries to negotiate the bill after the meal. If you are the restaurant, keep excellent records of every course.

DCAA vs. DCMA

DCAA is the accountants who check the money math. DCMA is the contract managers who check you deliver what you promised. Number problem, DCAA. Delivery, schedule or quality problem, DCMA.

TINA

Showing your receipts. Above the threshold ($10M on DoD contracts) you certify that the cost data behind your price is current, accurate and complete. If a receipt later turns out to be wrong, the government can take the difference back.

The authority ladder

The CO signs and changes the contract. The PM manages the program. The COR watches the work. Only one of them can commit the government's money, and it is not the one you talk to every day.

CMMC right now

The gym paused the personal trainer who checks your form. The workout is still mandatory, and you are still signing a form saying you did it.

Wrap rate

A restaurant steak. The price on the menu pays for the steak, plus the cook, the rent, the manager and a little profit. A wrap rate is that menu price divided by what the steak cost. At one large prime, a services business unit ran about 1.7 times while a systems division under the same parent ran about 2.9 times.

09 · Five mistakes contractors make

Avoid these before they cost you the contract

Not obscure edge cases. They show up at firms of every size, year after year, and each one is fixable once somebody names it.

1

Chasing instead of planning

Reacting to whatever shows up on SAM.gov burns proposal money and wins at low rates. By the time the final RFP posts, the firms that shaped it at the RFI stage are already ahead.

Do instead: pick target customers 12 to 18 months out, map the vehicles you need, respond to their market research, and score each pursuit's pWin before you commit bid money.
2

Treating the COR as a checkbox

The COR is the government's day-to-day technical eyes and gives input to your CPARS. Firms that only manage the CO relationship miss their best source of feedback.

Do instead: plan COR engagement from kickoff and put issues in writing. The COR cannot change the contract: anything that changes scope, price or schedule goes to the CO, or you may end up doing it for free.
3

Answering a PWS like it was a SOW

A SOW says what tasks to do. A PWS says what outcome to deliver and leaves the method to you. Answering a PWS with a list of activities tells evaluators you missed the point.

Do instead: for a PWS, lead with your management approach, the measures you will report and how you will hit the outcomes in the QASP.
4

Finding accounting gaps at the worst possible time

Firms moving into cost-type work often find the gaps after award: timekeeping that doesn't track by contract, unallowable costs in indirect pools, no written policies.

Do instead: build an adequate accounting system before you bid cost-type work, with a CPA who knows government contracts. Know where your deals sit against the TINA line ($10M on DoD contracts) and the EVM lines ($50M and $100M).
5

No recompete plan

Firms neglect the most winnable deal they have: the contract they already hold. Incumbents lose more often than most people assume.

Do instead: start the recompete plan at award. Track wins, CPARS narratives, staffing continuity and challengers, and put recompete risks on your R&O with a likelihood and a weighted dollar value, so leadership sees a number, not just a worry.

10 · Before you bid, and where to go next

A pre-bid checklist that catches most of the above

  • We know the buying office, its award history and what it has paid for this work.
  • We have the vehicle, or a teaming partner who does.
  • We answered the RFI or sources sought, and it shows in the requirement.
  • Our pWin is written down with the reasons, and leadership agreed to it.
  • We know which FAR version and clause numbers this solicitation uses.
  • Our SPRS entry and CMMC self-assessment are current and accurate.
  • Our accounting system fits the contract type we are bidding.
  • Someone checked our terms: PWS vs SOW, CO vs COR.
