02 · How the system works now

Three systems that have to agree before anything gets bought

People call it "Big A" acquisition. A program only moves when three separate systems line up: someone has to need it, someone has to pay for it, and someone has to buy it. Most program drama happens where two of them disagree.

Do we need it?

Requirements

  • Each service validates its own requirements (since Aug 2025).
  • The JROC ranks the joint force's Key Operational Problems.
  • The RRAB lines requirements up with money.
  • MEIA runs mission engineering and experiments.
  • Output: a validated requirement. Many offices still call it a CDD.
Can we pay for it?

Resources (PPBE)

  • Planning, Programming, Budgeting, Execution.
  • Services build the POM. OSD adjusts it with Resource Management Decisions (RMDs, once called PBDs).
  • The President's Budget goes to Congress, which authorizes (NDAA) and appropriates.
  • The money you spend today was planned about two years ago.
How do we buy it?

Acquisition

  • DoDI 5000.02, the Adaptive Acquisition Framework: six pathways (page 4).
  • Run by program managers under PEOs, which are becoming PAEs.
  • A Milestone Decision Authority (MDA) approves the big steps.
  • Contracts are awarded and changed only by a warranted contracting officer.

Who does what

RoleWhat they actually do
Milestone Decision Authority (MDA)Approves pathway, milestones and the program baseline. For the biggest programs, USD(A&S) or a service acquisition executive.
Service / Component Acquisition Executive (SAE, CAE)The senior acquisition official in each service. The Department of the Air Force has a separate one for space.
PEO, becoming PAERuns a portfolio of related programs. PAEs get more authority to move money and people across that portfolio.
Program Manager (PM)Owns cost, schedule and performance for one program. The person everyone calls when something slips.
Contracting Officer (CO or KO)The only person who can sign, change or end a contract on the government's behalf.
Contracting Officer's Representative (COR)Watches technical performance day to day. Cannot change the contract, however helpful that would be.
DCMA and DCAADCMA oversees contractor performance and administers contracts. DCAA audits contractor costs and accounting.

03 · The six pathways

Pick the right lane before you pick up speed

The Adaptive Acquisition Framework replaced one-size-fits-all with six pathways. The pathway sets your paperwork, your oversight and how fast you can move. The MDA makes the call, but a good PM walks in with a recommendation and a reason.

05 · Nunn-McCurdy and the big picture

How close is your program to a breach?

Nunn-McCurdy is the law that tracks unit cost growth on major programs against their baseline. Think of it as a credit limit with Congress on speed dial.

BreachUnit cost growthWhat happens
Significant15% over the current baseline, or 30% over the originalThe service reports the breach to Congress with an explanation.
Critical25% over the current baseline, or 50% over the originalThe program is terminated unless the Department certifies, after a root-cause analysis, that it is essential, has no cheaper alternative and has credible new estimates. Then it is restructured.
Know your distance in dollarsPercentages feel abstract. Work out how many dollars of unit cost growth separate your program from each line, keep it on your risk and opportunity register (R&O) with a likelihood and a weighted value, and brief it every quarter. Nobody has ever been thanked for a surprise breach.

08 · Analogies for the hard parts

The concepts new PMs trip on, in plain English

Use these to learn a concept, and to explain it to the next new person on your team. Teaching it is how you find out whether you really know it.

Requirements, after JCIDS

It used to be one bouncer at the door for the whole club. Now each service checks its own guest list, and the Joint Staff decides which parties matter most for the whole building.

PPBE

Planning a wedding two years out. You book the venue long before you know the guest count, and by the big day the money is committed. The only question left is whether you spend it well.

Color of money

Envelopes of cash, each legally sealed to one purpose. The rent envelope cannot buy groceries, even when it is full. RDT&E builds something new, procurement buys the finished thing, O&M keeps the lights on. Mixing them up can be an Antideficiency Act violation, and those are personal.

Continuing resolution

Running the household on last year's budget. You can keep paying the bills you already had, but you cannot sign up for anything new, and the new car waits.

The six pathways

A highway with six lanes to the same destination: a capability in users' hands. Major Capability is the heavy-truck lane, slow and inspected. Software is the express lane with frequent exits. The wrong lane for your cargo is how programs sit in traffic they never needed.

Nunn-McCurdy

A credit limit with Congress on speed dial. Go 15% over and they get a call. Go 25% over and the card gets cut up unless someone very senior vouches for you.

Undefinitized contract action

A restaurant with no prices on the menu. You are starving (the mission is urgent), so you order everything, and then you try to negotiate the bill after you have eaten. That is why these need tight limits and a fast push to a final price.

DCAA vs. DCMA

DCAA is the accountants who check the money math. DCMA is the contract managers who check the contractor delivers what was promised. Number problem, call DCAA. Delivery, schedule or quality problem, call DCMA.

TINA

Showing your receipts. On big sole-source deals (above $10M on DoD contracts) the contractor certifies that the cost data behind its price is current, accurate and complete. If a receipt turns out to be wrong, the price comes down.

Earned value

A road trip where you watch the fuel gauge and the map at the same time. Fuel burned tells you one thing, miles covered another. Earned value compares both with where you planned to be, so you hear about the detour before you run out of gas.

09 · Your career roadmap

How acquisition careers are built, and your first 90 days

Your acquisition career is governed by DAWIA and supported by WarU. Since the "Back-to-Basics" reforms, certification is simpler: a core certification in your functional area, then credentials for specific skills, then continuous learning to stay current.

The rules that follow you

Continuous learning80 CLPs every 2 years. One CLP is one hour of training.
PM certificationPractitioner at about 4 years of experience, Advanced at about 8, per WarU's program management fact sheet.
Where it is trackedYour component's learning system. Training outside WarU can be self-reported as external training. Your supervisor decides whether it counts.
Where to lookWarU (waru.edu), your component's acquisition career manager, your supervisor.

Skills that get people promoted

  • Money: read a budget, know your color of money, explain your EAC.
  • Contracts: know what your contract actually says and who can change it.
  • Risk: keep a live R&O and brief it as dollars, not adjectives.
  • Data: read an IPMDAR without waiting for someone to summarize it.
  • People: keep your users, sponsor, CO and contractor PM in the loop before there is a problem.

Your first 90 days on a program

  • Read the APB and write down your distance to each Nunn-McCurdy line in dollars.
  • Read the contract, not the summary of the contract. Note the type, the CLINs and the CDRLs.
  • Meet your contracting officer, COR, cost lead and financial manager in your first two weeks.
  • Find out which FAR overhaul deviations your contracting office has adopted.
  • Get access to the IPMDAR or your program's earned value tool.
  • Learn your program's colors of money and when each one expires.
  • Ask where your program sits in the current POM, and what the CR means for it.
  • Walk the IMS critical path with your schedule lead.
  • Open the R&O. If it has no dollar values, that is your first project.
  • Meet the users. They are the reason any of this exists.

10 · Five mistakes new PMs make

Learn these the cheap way

None of these is exotic. They show up in program after program, because the job teaches them by pain unless someone warns you first. Consider yourself warned.

1

Treating the schedule as a report instead of a tool

A schedule that shows green while the program is amber doesn't protect you. It moves the bad news to the milestone review, where it is more expensive and more public.

Do instead: hold a weekly schedule scrub with your IPT leads, work the critical path, and never let the IMS drift more than one reporting cycle from reality.
2

Not knowing your breach distance, or your real EAC

Many new PMs have never worked out their Nunn-McCurdy lines in dollars, and brief one EAC number as if it were a fact.

Do instead: keep an R&O with a likelihood and weighted value on every item, and brief the EAC as a range: worst, likely and best. Opportunities can be netted against risks in the forecast, but only a risk that is actually retired comes off the list.
3

Reading earned value one number at a time

A CPI of 0.92 trending up for three months may be fine. The same CPI trending down is a program in trouble.

Do instead: read trends, and compare the to-complete index (TCPI) with the CPI the team is really getting. EVM applies from $50M and a validated system from $100M. Below that, ask how the numbers are produced before you trust them.
4

Writing requirements nobody can test

"Highly reliable" and "user friendly" become arguments, then change orders, then claims. With JCIDS gone, your own service validates the requirement, so there is less excuse for leaving it fuzzy.

Do instead: give every key performance parameter a test method, a threshold and an objective value. If nobody can write the test, rewrite the requirement before it reaches the contract.
5

Waiting for a problem before talking to stakeholders

Your stakeholders include your PEO or PAE, the users, your service's requirements owner, CAPE, the comptroller, congressional staff and the contractor's PM.

Do instead: build a stakeholder plan in week one and keep it as current as your schedule. People support what they saw coming.
