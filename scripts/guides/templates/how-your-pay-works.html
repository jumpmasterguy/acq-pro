01 · The short version

Your hour has a price tag. Your salary is what's left of it.

Somewhere in your company's contract there is a price list with your job on it. It says what the government pays, per hour, for someone like you. Most people on a government program have never seen it, which is strange, because it explains almost everything about your paycheck. If you read nothing else, read this page.

  1. Your job has a price. Before your company bid on this contract, it listed every job category it would need and priced each one. Your title maps to one of them.
  2. That price covers a lot more than you. The hourly rate for your category pays for your salary, your benefits, your company's overhead and its profit. The government pays that rate.
  3. Your salary is what's left. Once the company has covered everything else, the rest is room for your pay. The rate sets what's possible, not what you get.
  4. Raises come through three doors. The contract gets recompeted and the rates get repriced, you move up to a higher category, or your company negotiates a change to the contract (a modification).
  5. The recompete matters more than your annual review. If your company wins it with higher rates, you might see a raise. If it loses, you might be job hunting, or wearing the new contractor's badge by Monday.

02 · Your job has a price tag

Your offer letter says "Data Scientist." The contract says "Senior Analyst, $95 an hour."

Welcome to labor categories. On a government contract, every person is assigned to one. You will hear it called a labor category, or LCAT (people say "el-cat"). It sounds technical. It just means a job title with a price the government agreed to pay.

Before your company ever won this contract, it built a price list of job titles: Junior Analyst, Senior Engineer, Program Manager and so on. Each title got an hourly rate, based on market salary data, the company's own cost structure, and how hard it had to sharpen its pencil to beat the other bidders.

Your title at work may not match. "Data Scientist" at your company might be billed as "Senior Analyst." "Staff Engineer" might show up as "Senior Systems Engineer." Someone made that mapping while writing the proposal. It is internal, it is sometimes approximate, and nobody mentions it at onboarding, because there is no natural moment to say "by the way, the government thinks you're a Senior Analyst."

The analogy

A labor category works like a seat on an airplane. The airline sells "Business, $1,200" long before it knows who will sit there. The government bought "Senior Analyst, $95 an hour" the same way. You are the passenger. The price came with the seat.

Worked example: the Senior Analyst category

WhatAmount

Fringe, overhead, G&A (general and administrative) and profitAbout $45 an hour, roughly 47% of the rate (page 4 breaks it down)

Illustrative numbers. Real rates vary widely by contract, agency and company.

Look at the last two rows. The rate is a ceiling, not a salary. Your company priced the category, not you, and it decides where you sit under that ceiling. Any raises it gives you over the life of the contract usually have to fit under the same rate, too.

Why doesn't my job title match what's in the contract?Because companies map their internal titles to contract categories when they write the proposal. A "Senior Analyst" on the contract might be what your company calls a "Lead Researcher." If you're curious about your mapping, ask your program manager. It's a normal question, not an interrogation.

03 · Where the rest of your rate goes

Where the other half of your hour goes

This is the moment most people do the math and get a little annoyed. The government pays $95 an hour for your category, and your paycheck says nowhere near $95 an hour. Before you draft the angry email, here is where the money goes.

The analogy

Ever paid a plumber $150 an hour? The plumber does not take home $150. The van, the insurance, the person answering the phone, the tools and the owner's profit all ride on that hour. Your billed hour works the same way. You are the plumber.

One illustrative $100 hour

Wrap rate = billed rate ÷ your salary rate  $100 ÷ $50 = 2.0
Every $1 of your salary costs the government $2 here. Across contractors it typically runs from about $1.70 to $2.20; at the lean end, salary is closer to 60% of the rate. A wrap rate of 1.8 means every $1 of salary bills at $1.80.
PieceWhat it pays forTypical share of the rate
Your salaryWhat shows up on your paycheck, before taxes45 to 55%
Fringe benefitsHealth insurance, your 401(k) match, paid leave, payroll taxes18 to 25%
OverheadManagers and supervisors who aren't billed to a contract, facilities, computers and networks, the security office15 to 20%
G&A (general and administrative)The corporate layer: executives, legal, finance, and the business development team that won this work in the first place8 to 12%
Profit or feeWhat the company actually earns. On cost-type contracts it is called fee.5 to 10%

Typical ranges, not one company's books, so they won't add up to exactly 100% for any single contract.

In plain English: if the government pays $100 an hour for your category, roughly $45 to $55 of it is your salary. Your benefits come out of the next slice. The rest keeps the company running and profitable.

Is my company ripping me off?Almost certainly not. This structure is standard, boring and regulated, which is the most reassuring thing about it. The rates are laid out in the company's proposal, and on cost-type contracts the Defense Contract Audit Agency (DCAA) audits whether the costs are real and allowable. A company caught padding its overhead has a much bigger problem than your raise. Every government contractor builds its rates this way.

04 · Why raises are harder

Why your manager makes the face when you say "raise"

You did good work, you asked for a raise, and your manager made the face. It usually isn't about you. The money has a ceiling, it was set before you walked in, and your manager can't raise it by being persuaded.

The constraintYour company agreed to bill a set rate for your category. If a raise pushes your cost above what that rate can support, the company loses money on you every hour, for the rest of the contract. Every dollar of raise has to come from somewhere.
The analogy

Asking for a raise mid-contract is like haggling with a vending machine. You can be very persuasive. The price was still set before you walked up.

The three moments the rate behind your pay can move

1

The contract is recompeted (the best time to ask)

When the work comes up for a new competition, or a follow-on contract, your company reprices every category. That's when rates can go up, and when companies often give raises to keep good people heading into the new period. If you've been performing well, this is your best leverage.

Option years are different. An option year is a pre-priced extension the government can choose to buy. When it does, it pays the prices already written into the contract, often with a built-in yearly increase (escalation). Ask what escalation your category already has baked in.
2

You move up to a higher category

If you've grown into a more senior role, your company can start billing you under a higher-rate category. It takes paperwork, because they have to formally reclassify you on the contract, but your billed rate and your salary ceiling both go up.

3

The contract is modified

If the scope of work changes significantly, companies sometimes negotiate new rates as part of the contract modification. Less common, but it happens.

The hard truthOn a cost-plus contract, the government pays actual costs, so there is more room for raises without the company eating them. On a fixed-price contract, every raise comes straight out of profit. That's why fixed-price programs tend to run tighter pay. Page 6 covers contract types.
Can I just ask my manager for a raise?Yes. Just know the box they're working in. The raise conversations that work on government programs cover three things: which category you're billed under now, whether a higher category exists on the contract, and when the next recompete or option year lands. Bring those and you're not asking for a favor, you're proposing a plan. And if the answer is no, it is often a timing problem, not a verdict on your work.

05 · How the contract type shapes your pay

Same title, same company, very different raise odds

Not every government contract works the same way. The type your program runs on decides how much room your company has to pay you, and how hard it is squeezing to keep costs down. If you don't know which type you're on, that's worth ten minutes of finding out.

Price set up front

Firm-Fixed-Price (FFP)

  • Your company agreed to do X for $Y.
  • Come in under budget and it keeps the difference. Go over and that's its problem.
  • Every incentive to keep costs lean, salaries included. Efficiency is rewarded.
  • Overspending comes directly out of profit.
Actual costs, plus a fee

Cost-Plus (CPFF, CPIF)

  • Cost-Plus-Fixed-Fee or Cost-Plus-Incentive-Fee.
  • The government pays actual costs plus a fee, so there is less pressure to cut, within limits.
  • Government auditors check that the costs are real and allowable.
  • More room for raises, less direct pressure on headcount.
Hours at set rates

Time and Materials (T&M)

  • A fixed hourly rate for each job category, and materials reimbursed.
  • The most flexible type for the company day to day.
  • Less stable for you: the government buys hours, not a commitment, and it can buy fewer of them.

What this means for your pay

Contract typeRaise flexibilityYour risk level
Fixed-priceLow: raises come out of profitModerate: lean staffing preferred
Cost-plusHigher: costs pass through to the governmentLower: costs are reimbursed
Time and materialsModerate: tied to the category ratesHigher: hourly funding, less stability

So who actually pays for your raise?

Contract typeWho covers it
Fixed-priceYour company, out of profit. The price the government pays doesn't move.
Cost-plusMostly the government, as long as the pay is reasonable (auditors check) and the contract's funding stretches to cover it.
Time and materialsYour company. Your category's hourly rate doesn't move, so the raise comes out of its margin on every hour you bill.
The analogy: three ways to hire movers

Fixed-price is a flat quote. If the move runs long, the movers eat it. Cost-plus is paying their actual costs plus an agreed fee, and checking the receipts. Time and materials is paying by the hour, which is great for the movers right up until you decide to carry the lamps yourself.

How to find out which one you're onAsk your program manager. It's question 4 on page 9. Bigger programs sometimes mix types, with some work fixed-price and some cost-plus, so ask about the work you actually charge your hours to.

06 · What the recompete means for you

Every contract has an expiration date. So does your seat on it.

This is the section most people don't see coming. When a contract expires, the government usually competes the work again, and your company has to bid against everyone else who wants it. That's the recompete.

The analogy

A recompete is like your landlord selling the building. You probably still have somewhere to live. The rent, the rules and the person you call when the heat breaks may all change.

Three ways it can go

Your company wins

Usually business as usual. Sometimes with updated rates, which is good for raises. Sometimes with tighter rates, if the competition was fierce and your company sharpened its pencil to win.

A competitor wins

The new company may offer you a job, because you bring the knowledge and the clearance. In most cases it doesn't have to match your current pay. It will offer what its own rate structure allows.

The work goes away

Programs get restructured, defunded or absorbed into something else. The contract ends, and the role ends with it.

The clearance factorIf you hold a security clearance, you are worth more to the incoming contractor. Cleared people are expensive to replace: clearing a new person can take 12 to 18 months and cost $15,000 to $50,000. That is real leverage in a transition.
Should I negotiate with the new contractor?Yes. They need you more than they'll admit. Your institutional knowledge, your relationships with the government customer and your active clearance are all worth negotiating for. Don't assume they'll match your current package automatically. Ask, specifically.

Before the recompete, know these

  • When the contract ends or recompetes (question 3 on page 9). Six months out is when to start paying attention.
  • Your labor category and, if you can find out, its rate (question 1).
  • Your clearance level, and whether it is active. It travels with you, so look after it.
  • What you would ask a new contractor for: salary, title, start date, leave. Decide before they call, not during the call.

07 · Life at a subcontractor

Same work, different deal

If your badge says one company and your customer's org chart says another, this page is for you. A subcontractor is a smaller company supporting the prime contractor, the company that holds the contract with the government. Working for the sub adds one more layer between the government's money and your paycheck.

Your company's rates are negotiated with the prime, not with the government. The prime marks up your company's rates before billing the government, which is sometimes called a pass-through. So your company has a ceiling set by the prime, and the prime has a ceiling set by the government.

The analogy

Working for a sub is like subletting an apartment. You pay the tenant, the tenant pays the landlord, and the tenant is not doing this out of the goodness of their heart.

LayerWho pays whomWhat gets taken
The governmentPays the prime contractorNothing. This is where the money starts.
Prime contractorBills the government, pays your companyThe prime's overhead, plus its markup on your company's rates
Your company (the sub)Gets paid by the prime, pays youYour company's fringe, overhead, G&A and profit
YouReceive your salaryNothing. Your salary is what's left.

What this means for you

  • Two layers of overhead sit between the government's money and your potential pay.
  • Your company's room to raise your salary depends on what the prime will pay it.
  • When the prime wins a recompete or renegotiates rates, your company may or may not see any benefit. It depends on the subcontract terms.

This is also why subcontractor employees often get hired by the prime at some point. It simplifies the contract structure, and it can improve pay for the individual, because there is one layer of overhead instead of two.

Should I try to get hired by the prime?It depends. Primes often pay more, because there is one less layer of markup. But subcontractors sometimes offer faster promotions, more responsibility and closer-knit teams. Weigh the whole picture, not just the salary line.

08 · Five questions most people never ask

What the contract thinks of you

Annual reviews tell you what your manager thinks of you. These five questions tell you what the contract thinks of you, which is the part that sets your pay. None of them is confrontational. All of them are normal things to ask a program manager. Most people never ask them anyway.

1

"What labor category am I billed under on this contract?"

Your company should be able to tell you. If the answer is vague, that's a signal. Knowing your category tells you what the government pays for your hour, and how close you are to the ceiling for your level.

2

"What's the next category up, and what does it require?"

If a higher category exists on the contract, that's your promotion ladder, already written down and already priced. Ask what the qualifications are and how close you are to meeting them.

3

"When does this contract hit its next option year or recompete?"

That's the best time to talk about a raise or a reclassification. If it's six months out, start the conversation now.

4

"Is this a fixed-price or a cost-type contract?"

It tells you how much flexibility exists before you ask for anything, and how much pressure your company is under to keep costs down.

5

"What happens to my position if we lose the recompete?"

A good manager gives you a straight answer. If they dodge, assume the answer is "you'd negotiate with the new contractor." That's the standard answer and not necessarily bad, but plan for it. A dodge is also an answer.

Do the napkin math

Once question 1 gets you your category's rate, work out the ceiling for your salary in two lines.

Low end: billed rate $ ÷ 2.2 = $ an hour
High end: billed rate $ ÷ 1.7 = $ an hour
Multiply each by 2,080 hours: $ to $ a year

Example: $95 ÷ 2.2 is about $43, and $95 ÷ 1.7 is about $56, so roughly $90,000 to $116,000 a year. Near the bottom of your range or under it? Question 2 is your friend. Near the top? Question 3 matters more. No rate yet? The General Services Administration's (GSA) free CALC tool (Contract-Awarded Labor Category) shows hourly rates awarded on GSA schedule contracts. Not your contract, but it shows you the neighborhood.
