01 · Why this matters

Osmosis is not an onboarding plan

We've all watched it happen. A sharp new hire joins a GovCon (government contracting) team, sits in meetings they only half follow, reads contracts they can't quite parse, and slowly pieces together how the Department of Defense (DoD) buys things. It works, eventually. So does learning to swim by falling off the dock.

Osmosis is slow. It's inconsistent, because it depends on who the new hire happens to sit next to. And it's expensive, though the time is the cheap part of the bill.

The expensive part: avoidable mistakes

Money

The wrong color of money

A funding document that cites the wrong appropriation. Research, development, test and evaluation (RDT&E) money, operation and maintenance (O&M) money and procurement money each buy different things, and the law cares which is which.

Contracts

Vehicle or task order?

Mixing up the IDIQ (indefinite delivery, indefinite quantity) vehicle with the task order under it, then quoting the wrong scope, ceiling or deliverable to the customer.

Authority

The wrong person said yes

Starting new work because someone without contractual authority asked for it. That is how unauthorized commitments happen, and how invoices end up in a dispute.

Each one chips away at customer trust and creates real compliance risk. None of them needs a bad hire. They only need nobody to have explained the rules early.

What a structured 30 days changes
  • Fewer mistakes in the first 90 days.
  • Better proposal quality from day one.
  • Faster contribution to program execution.
  • A repeatable process, not a one-time lucky hire.

Who this is for

Team leads, program directors and hiring managers who want one repeatable way to bring new program managers (PMs), business development (BD) staff, capture managers and analysts up to speed on DoD acquisition, quickly and the same way every time.

How it works

You run a week-by-week framework. The new hire takes Acqlerate's short, self-paced lessons alongside it. The goal is practical fluency, meaning they can use what they learned on a real program, not just compliance awareness that evaporates after the training certificate is filed.

Your part as the lead fits on a sticky note: a program briefing on day 1, a check-in at day 14, a debrief at day 21 and a sign-off at day 30.

03 · Weeks 1 and 2: Foundation

Days 1 to 14: understand the landscape

The first two weeks build a shared vocabulary and a baseline. Nobody needs to quote the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) by day 14. They do need to stop nodding politely every time someone says "task order" or "color of money" and hoping nobody asks a follow-up question. Four topics get them there.

Topic 1

What is DoD acquisition?

  • The acquisition lifecycle, from requirement to award to execution.
  • The key players: the PM, the contracting officer (CO), the contracting officer's representative (COR) and the Defense Contract Management Agency (DCMA).
  • Common contract types: firm fixed price (FFP), cost plus fixed fee (CPFF), time and materials (T&M), and IDIQ.
Topic 2

How does the government buy?

  • Standalone contracts vs. task orders.
  • IDIQ vehicles and the fair opportunity process for competing orders.
  • The FAR, which is being rewritten under the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul (RFO). Same name, new text, some new clause numbers.
Topic 3

What is color of money?

  • Appropriation categories: RDT&E, O&M and procurement.
  • Fiscal year rules: O&M can be put on contract for 1 year, RDT&E for 2, procurement for 3.
  • Why Antideficiency Act (ADA) violations matter.
Topic 4

The three career tracks

  • Government PM: owns cost, schedule and performance for the program.
  • Contractor PM: delivers the work, and the margin.
  • Contracting officer: the only one who can sign or change the contract.
  • Different roles, different authorities, different incentives.
Recommended modulesDoD Acquisitions Foundations (Module 1, free) plus Defense Contracting Fundamentals. About 6.5 hours of lessons in total, so roughly 40 minutes a workday across the two weeks. Module 1 includes the lesson on working while the FAR is being rewritten.

Day 1 checklist

  • Create a free Acqlerate account at acqlerate.com.
  • Finish the first Foundations lesson.
  • Read the Acquisition Starter Kit (Contractor or Government edition, also free).
  • Program context briefing with the PM or program lead.
  • Review the contract or task order they'll work under. The real one, not the slide that summarizes it.

By day 14

  • Foundations module complete and Starter Kit read (aim for day 7).
  • Defense Contracting Fundamentals complete.
  • Contract reviewed, with a written list of questions.
  • Day 14 check-in with you: what's clear, what's still fog.

04 · Week 3: Application

Days 15 to 21: apply it to your program

Week 3 is where it stops being a course. The vocabulary from weeks 1 and 2 meets your actual contract, your actual customer, and that one deliverable that is somehow always late. The new hire shifts from learning to doing.

Four things to do this week

  1. Shadow one real activity. A contract review, a program review or a proposal session. Watching a concept land in a live meeting beats reading about it three times.
  2. Complete the module that fits the role. BD and capture staff take Capture Management & Business Development. PMs take Defense Finance & Budgeting or Data Analytics for Program Managers.
  3. Name one concept they still can't explain, and fix it. The AI Study Assistant inside each Acqlerate lesson will explain it more simply or show how it applies on the job. Then have them explain it to you in two minutes. If they can teach it, they know it.
  4. Review their first CDRL (Contract Data Requirements List) or deliverable requirement. The CDRL says what is due, in what format, to whom and how often. Somebody on the customer side is counting on it.

Role-specific guidance

Contractor PM

Know the contract cold

  • Focus on Defense Contracting Fundamentals.
  • Learn EVM (earned value management) basics: planned work, work done and what it cost.
  • Know your task order SOW inside and out.
BD / Capture

Write to the scorecard

  • Complete Capture Management & Business Development.
  • Understand Section L (instructions to offerors) vs. Section M (evaluation criteria).
  • L tells you how to write it. M tells you how it gets scored. Evaluators score against M.
Proposal analyst

Learn how proposals are judged

  • Work through your pricing volume checklist.
  • Study your past performance template.
  • Learn source selection basics: how the government evaluates and picks a winner.
Make the shadow seat countSpend five minutes before the meeting on what it's for, who's who and what to listen for. Spend five minutes after on what surprised them. With a briefing and a debrief, a meeting is a lesson. Without them it's just another meeting, and new hires already have plenty of those.
Day 21 milestoneRole-specific module done and first deliverable reviewed. Hold the day 21 debrief: what they saw in the shadow seat, what the CDRL asks for, and which concept they fixed this week. Anything still foggy goes on the list for week 4.

05 · Week 4: Integration

Days 22 to 30: operate independently

By the end of week 4 the new hire works with minimal hand-holding. Not zero. Nobody is fully independent at day 30, including a few people who've been on the program for ten years. Here is what "ready" looks like.

Ready means they can

  • Describe the acquisition lifecycle in plain English, from requirements to award to execution.
  • Explain what type of contract their program uses, and why it matters for risk and billing.
  • Name the CO, COR and DCMA representative on their program, and say what each one controls.
  • Say what color of money funds their program and the fiscal year limits that come with it.
  • Identify two or three risks on their program and how each is being mitigated. Bonus points for putting a likelihood and a dollar value on each, the way a risk and opportunity register (R&O) does.
The assessmentRun the ten-question Day 30 check on the next page. It covers the acquisition lifecycle, contract types, color of money, and roles and authorities. If they pass, they're ready to contribute independently. If not, each miss points to a specific lesson to revisit, so you're fixing gaps, not repeating the whole month.

06 · The Day 30 check

Ten questions, one coffee, no slides

Sit down with the new hire for about twenty minutes, notes closed. Ask every question about your program, not in the abstract. You're not grading trivia. You're checking whether they can hold their own when you're not in the room.

#Ask themA good answer includes
1Walk me through how our program got from a need to a contract.
Lifecycle		A requirement, money in a budget, a solicitation, an award, then execution. In that order, in plain English.
2Where is our program in its lifecycle today, and what's the next big event?
Lifecycle		The current phase, plus the next option exercise, milestone, review or recompete, and roughly when.
3What type of contract are we on?
Contract type		The type (FFP, CPFF, T&M or a mix) and whether it's a standalone contract or a task order under an IDIQ.
4Who carries the cost risk on our contract, and how do we bill?
Contract type		FFP: the contractor carries it and bills the agreed price. Cost-type: allowable costs reimbursed, plus fee. T&M: hours at fixed rates, plus materials.
5What's the difference between the IDIQ and our task order?
Contract type		The vehicle sets the ceiling and general terms. The order sets our scope, funding and deliverables, and it governs our work.
6What color of money funds our work, and when does it expire?
Money		The appropriation by name and how long it can be put on contract: O&M 1 year, RDT&E 2, procurement 3.
7Why can't the customer just use leftover O&M money for our development work?
Money		Each appropriation buys only what it was appropriated for. Using the wrong one can become an Antideficiency Act violation.
8Who are our CO, COR and DCMA representative, and what does each one control?
Roles		Names, plus: the CO signs and changes the contract, the COR monitors performance, DCMA oversees contractor performance and administers contracts.
9Someone on the customer side asks for work you don't see in the SOW. What do you do?
Roles		"Let me check with the CO," routed through our contracts lead. No work starts on a handshake.
10What are the top two or three risks on our program, and who's working each one?
Risk		Specific risks, a mitigation and an owner for each. Strong answers add a likelihood and a dollar value.
Scoring itEight or more solid answers: they're ready, so sign them off. Fewer than eight: each miss points to a lesson. Assign it, then ask only those questions again a week later.
A second data pointEvery Acqlerate module ends with an assessment that unlocks the next depth level. Passed assessments plus a good Day 30 conversation is a sign-off you can defend.

07 · Five mistakes new hires make

Head these off in the first 30 days

These five come up again and again with new GovCon hires. Catch them in the first month and you save months of course correction, and at least one awkward call with the customer.

1

Skipping the contract review

The SOW tells you what's actually authorized. Everything you do on a government contract traces back to it: scope, deliverables, period of performance and funding. If you haven't read it, you're guessing. Confidently, and in front of the customer.

Do instead: read it before week 1 ends, and keep a one-page note of scope, deliverables, period of performance and funding where you can find it in a meeting.
2

Confusing the IDIQ with the task order

The vehicle gets you in the door. The task order is your actual contract. Think of the IDIQ as the restaurant menu: it sets the ceiling and the general terms. The task order is what was actually ordered, and it's the only thing anyone is paying for. It defines your specific scope, funding and deliverables.

Do instead: know which one governs your work. When someone quotes a ceiling or a requirement, ask "the vehicle's or the order's?"
3

Ignoring color of money

Appropriations work like gift cards: the coffee shop card doesn't work at the hardware store, however much is left on it. Using O&M money for work that should be RDT&E can become an Antideficiency Act violation. These categories aren't bureaucratic trivia. They're legal limits, and the consequences are personal for the officials involved.

Do instead: know the difference, know which color funds your work, and know when it expires.
4

Treating the COR like a boss

The COR monitors performance. Only the CO can change your contract. The COR checks your work against the blueprint, but only the CO can change the blueprint. Work started because someone without that authority asked for it rests on an unauthorized commitment, and you may never get paid for it.

Do instead: when anyone other than the CO asks for a change, however reasonable and however senior, the answer is "Let me check with the CO."
5

Assuming WarU training equals operational fluency

Compliance training and practical fluency are different things. Courses from WarU (Warfighting Acquisition University, called Defense Acquisition University or DAU until November 2025) cover policy and regulation. Knowing the rules of the road and merging onto the highway at rush hour are related skills. They are not the same skill.

Do instead: build both. Use WarU for the policy and regulation, and Acqlerate for the practical judgment to apply it in real contract and program situations.
