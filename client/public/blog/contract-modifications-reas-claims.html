Contract Modifications, REAs, and Claims: When Defense Contracts Change

Things change on defense contracts. Here's how to navigate bilateral and unilateral modifications, pursue an REA, convert it to a CDA claim, and protect your rights through appeals.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 10 min read · For: Contractor

No defense contract survives contact with reality unchanged. Requirements shift. The government directs new work. Unforeseen site conditions blow up your cost estimate. The contracting officer issues a stop-work order for six months. Whatever the cause, when the terms of your contract change — formally or informally — knowing how to respond is not optional. The difference between recovering your costs and eating a loss often comes down to whether you took the right procedural steps at the right time.

This guide covers the full spectrum: routine contract modifications, the Changes clause, constructive changes, the Request for Equitable Adjustment (REA) process, converting an REA to a formal claim under the Contract Disputes Act (CDA), and what happens at the Armed Services Board of Contract Appeals (ASBCA) or the Court of Federal Claims (COFC). For foundational context on contract types and the FAR framework, see our Federal Contracting 101 guide.

Contract Modifications: The Formal Mechanism for Change

A contract modification is any written change to the terms of a contract. Modifications are executed on Standard Form 30 (SF-30) and come in two flavors that carry very different legal implications.

Bilateral Modifications (Supplemental Agreements)

A bilateral modification is signed by both the Contracting Officer (CO) and the contractor. Both parties agree to the change. Bilateral modifications are used for:

  • Changes that are within scope but require negotiated price adjustments
  • Definitizing letter contracts (converting a preliminary authorization into a final contract)
  • Negotiated equitable adjustments following a change order
  • Voluntary, no-cost modifications to administrative terms
  • Exercise of options (though some options can be unilateral)

The critical trap with bilateral modifications: Standard bilateral modification language often includes release language — the contractor agrees that the modification constitutes full settlement of all issues related to the change. If you sign a bilateral mod and later discover additional costs you didn't account for, you may have signed away your right to recover them.

Before you sign any bilateral modification, ask: does this compensation make me whole? If you have unresolved claims or additional costs you haven't quantified yet, include a reservation of rights — explicit language stating that you reserve the right to pursue additional compensation for specific issues not addressed in this modification. Get it in writing before your signature goes on the form. Courts have held that failure to include a reservation of rights can constitute a waiver, even when the contractor later argues the modification didn't address a specific element of the change.

Unilateral Modifications

A unilateral modification is signed only by the CO. The government acts alone. Contractors must comply with unilateral modifications even if they disagree with them — subject to their right to seek equitable adjustment. Unilateral modifications are used for:

  • Administrative changes (issuing a new CO, changing the payment address)
  • Exercising options previously agreed to in the contract
  • Issuing change orders under the Changes clause
  • Termination for convenience
  • Issuing stop-work orders

When you receive a unilateral modification directing new or changed work, your clock starts. The Changes clause requires timely notice and an assertion of your right to equitable adjustment.

The Changes Clause: Your Legal Foundation

FAR 52.243-1 (for supplies) and FAR 52.243-4 (for construction) — the Changes clause — is one of the most important clauses in any government contract. It authorizes the CO to order changes to the contract unilaterally, within the general scope of the contract, and it preserves the contractor's right to an equitable adjustment in price and schedule if that change increases the contractor's cost or time.

Key elements of the Changes clause:

  • The CO can direct changes to the statement of work, specifications, place of performance, time of performance, and method of shipment/packing.
  • Changes must be within the general scope of the contract — the government cannot unilaterally direct work that is fundamentally different from what was contracted.
  • If the change causes an increase or decrease in cost or time, the contractor is entitled to an equitable adjustment.
  • The contractor must assert its right to an equitable adjustment within 30 days of receiving a change order (the contract may specify a different period). Failure to provide timely notice can waive your right to adjustment — though courts sometimes apply exceptions where the government had actual knowledge.

Notice is not optional. If you receive a change order and say nothing about cost impact for six months while you absorb the expense, you may have waived your right to recover. The moment a CO directive has cost implications for you, communicate it in writing — even a brief letter stating that you are analyzing the cost and schedule impacts and will provide a Request for Equitable Adjustment within a specified timeframe preserves your rights and starts the dialogue.

Constructive Changes: When Direction Comes Without a Formal Mod

A constructive change occurs when the government — through action or inaction — causes the contractor to perform work beyond the contract scope without issuing a formal change order. The contractor's costs increase, but there's no modification. Common constructive change scenarios:

  • Government-furnished property (GFP) delays: The government promised to deliver equipment needed for your work by a certain date. It's six weeks late. Your schedule and costs are affected.
  • Defective specifications: You followed the government's specifications exactly, and the product doesn't work. The specs were wrong. You're directed to fix it — but fixing it is extra work.
  • Informal technical direction: A COR (acting outside their authority) tells you to use a different approach, add a feature, or change a process. You comply. No modification is issued.
  • Overly strict inspection: The government applies a more demanding inspection standard than the contract requires, causing you to rework acceptable work.
  • Interference or failure to cooperate: Government failure to provide timely approvals, access, or decisions that delay your performance.

Constructive changes are recoverable, but they require documentation. You need to show: (1) the government's action or inaction; (2) that it went beyond the contract's requirements; (3) that you incurred additional costs as a result; and (4) the quantum (amount) of your damages. Contemporaneous documentation — emails, meeting minutes, technical direction letters, cost records — is everything.

The REA Process: Your First Path to Recovery

A Request for Equitable Adjustment (REA) is an informal written request to the CO for an adjustment to contract price, schedule, or terms based on a government-caused change or constructive change. "Informal" here means there's no required format, no certification requirement (below $100K), and no strict timeline on the government's response. It's the contractor saying: here's what happened, here's what it cost me, here's the adjustment we need.

Step-by-Step: How to Prepare and Submit an REA

Step 1: Identify the triggering event. What specifically did the government do (or fail to do) that caused extra cost or delay? Be precise. "The government was slow" is not a basis for an REA. A properly framed REA identifies the exact directive, date, and resulting cost impact.

Step 2: Tie the event to a contract clause. Your REA must cite a legal basis. Usually this is the Changes clause (FAR 52.243), but it might also be the Differing Site Conditions clause (FAR 52.236-2), the Government Delay of Work clause (FAR 52.242-14), or the Stop Work Order clause (FAR 52.242-15). No clause, no claim — or at least, a much weaker claim.

Step 3: Quantify your damages. This is the hard part. You need to show additional labor hours (by category, by week), additional material costs (receipts, vendor quotes), extended overhead and G&A costs for time-based impacts, and any other recoverable costs. The quantum must be defensible — don't inflate it, as this destroys your credibility with the CO and in any subsequent litigation.

Step 4: Prepare a clear, organized REA package. The REA should include: an executive summary of the issue; the factual background; the legal basis (contract clause); the quantum (with supporting documentation); and a proposed equitable adjustment (price, schedule, or both). A well-organized REA moves faster. A disorganized one gets buried.

Step 5: Submit to the CO and engage in good faith. Submit the REA in writing to the CO (copy the COR). Follow up, engage in discussions, be willing to provide additional backup. The CO is not required to respond on any specific timeline to an REA (unlike a formal claim), which is both a feature (no pressure) and a bug (it can drag forever). If negotiations stall, you have the option to convert.

Converting an REA to a Formal CDA Claim

When REA negotiations break down — or when you need to stop the clock on a six-year statute of limitations — it's time to consider a formal claim under the Contract Disputes Act (CDA), 41 U.S.C. sections 7101-7109.

A CDA claim has specific requirements that distinguish it from an REA:

  • Must be in writing
  • Must request a sum certain — a specific dollar amount (or other specific relief)
  • Must demand a written final decision from the CO (the Contracting Officer's Final Decision, or COFD)
  • If the claim exceeds $100,000, it must be certified — a written statement that the claim is made in good faith, the supporting data is accurate, and the amount accurately reflects the contract adjustment for which the contractor believes the government is liable. Certification by an unauthorized person or a certification that omits required language can sink a claim.
  • Must be submitted within six years of the accrual date — the date when all events giving rise to the claim were known or should have been known. This is the statute of limitations; missing it is fatal.

Once you submit a properly certified CDA claim, the CO has 60 days to issue a COFD. The CO may request additional time if the claim is complex, but must notify you of the expected timeline. If the CO doesn't issue a COFD within 60 days (without requesting an extension), the failure to decide is treated as a "deemed denial" — you can appeal immediately.

Key strategic decision: REA first vs. claim first? An REA preserves the relationship and avoids the formality (and cost) of litigation. But REA negotiations can drag for years without resolution, eating your statute of limitations if you're not careful. Many contractors file an REA to start negotiations and convert to a claim only if negotiations stall — but track your accrual date and the six-year window carefully from day one.

The COFD and Appeals: What Happens When the Government Says No

If the CO denies your claim (or partially denies it), you receive a Contracting Officer's Final Decision (COFD). The COFD must state the CO's decision with supporting rationale and advise you of your appeal rights. You have two options:

Armed Services Board of Contract Appeals (ASBCA)

The ASBCA is the specialized administrative tribunal for DoD contract disputes. It's composed of experienced contract law judges. To appeal to the ASBCA, you must file within 90 days of receiving the COFD. ASBCA proceedings are less formal than federal court but still involve discovery, briefing, and hearings. The ASBCA has small claims procedures (expedited/accelerated) for claims under $100K/$250K respectively — faster, cheaper, but binding without appeal.

Court of Federal Claims (COFC)

The COFC is a federal court with nationwide jurisdiction over claims against the United States. You can appeal a COFD to the COFC within 12 months (one year) of receiving the COFD. COFC proceedings are more formal, more expensive, and more appropriate for large or legally complex disputes. COFC decisions can be appealed to the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals and ultimately to the Supreme Court.

Note: You can also go to COFC directly from a CDA claim, bypassing the ASBCA entirely, within the 12-month window.

Practical Tips for Contractors

Document Everything in Real Time

Claims are won and lost on contemporaneous documentation. An email written the day the government directed extra work is worth ten times more than a reconstruction six months later. Build the habit: when something happens that could have cost implications, document it that day. Email the CO and COR summarizing what was discussed and what direction was given. Keep your own copies.

Know the Difference Between In-Scope and Out-of-Scope Changes

The government can unilaterally direct changes within the general scope of the contract using the Changes clause. But if the government directs work that is fundamentally different from what was contracted — outside the scope — it's a cardinal change, which may entitle the contractor to greater remedies. The line between in-scope and out-of-scope changes is fact-specific and often litigated. When in doubt, flag it to the CO and to counsel.

Read Every Modification Before You Sign

Bilateral modifications often contain broad release language. Before you sign, ask: is there anything I'm giving up that I haven't been compensated for? If yes, negotiate the release language or include a reservation of rights.

Engage Counsel Early

REAs and claims involve contract law, regulatory requirements, and litigation strategy. Government contracting law is a specialized field. The investment in GovCon counsel at the REA stage — not just when you're filing an ASBCA appeal — can be the difference between a negotiated resolution and years of litigation. Counsel can also help you spot claims you may not have recognized as such.

Track Your Statute of Limitations

The six-year CDA statute of limitations runs from accrual — not from when the REA was denied. If you filed an REA in Year 1 and the government hasn't responded by Year 5, your statute of limitations is approaching. Don't let negotiations lull you into missing it. Calendar your accrual dates and set alerts well before the deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between an REA and a CDA claim?

An REA (Request for Equitable Adjustment) is an informal request for contract adjustment — no required format, no certification requirement (under $100K), and no deadline on the government's response. It's used to initiate negotiations with the contracting officer. A CDA (Contract Disputes Act) claim is a formal legal demand that requires a written COFD within 60 days, must be certified if over $100K, and triggers access to the ASBCA or COFC for appeals if denied. REAs are typically the first step; claims are the formal escalation path.

What is the Eichleay formula and when does it apply?

The Eichleay formula is a method for calculating unabsorbed home office overhead — the overhead costs a contractor continues to incur during a government-caused delay or suspension of work when the contractor can't take on other work. It applies when: there was a government-caused delay; the contractor was on standby during the delay; and the contractor couldn't replace the delayed work with other work. The formula calculates a daily overhead rate based on the contract's proportional share of home office overhead, then multiplies it by the number of delay days. Eichleay claims are common in construction contracts and are heavily litigated.

Can the government terminate a contract unilaterally?

Yes. The government has the right to terminate contracts for convenience under FAR 52.249-1 through 52.249-8 (depending on contract type). This isn't a breach — it's a contractual right that the government reserves. When terminated for convenience, the contractor is entitled to recover its reasonable costs incurred up to termination, profit on work performed, and reasonable settlement costs — but not anticipated profits on unperformed work. The contractor must submit a termination settlement proposal within one year of the termination notice. Terminations for default (when the government asserts the contractor breached) carry far more significant consequences.

What does "sum certain" mean for a CDA claim?

A CDA claim must request a specific dollar amount — a sum certain — or other specific, definite relief (such as a specific number of days of schedule extension). Courts and boards have held that vague requests like "such damages as may be proven at hearing" or open-ended requests are not CDA claims because they don't meet the sum certain requirement. You need to do the work to quantify your damages before filing — not a rough estimate, but a supportable specific number. You can reserve the right to update the amount if additional costs develop, but the initial claim must state a specific amount.

How long do ASBCA proceedings typically take?

It varies significantly by complexity and docket. Simple cases under the ASBCA's small claims (Expedited Procedures) or accelerated procedures can be decided in 6-12 months. Complex major system disputes can take 3-7 years or more from filing to final decision. The ASBCA has made efforts to manage its docket and move cases faster, but large programs with extensive discovery and multiple hearings take time. This is one reason why contractors strongly prefer to resolve disputes at the CO level or through negotiations rather than litigation — the time cost of ASBCA proceedings is itself substantial.
