The PPBE Cycle Explained: How the Pentagon Builds Its Budget

A phase-by-phase walkthrough of Planning, Programming, Budgeting, and Execution — with key documents, owners, and timelines for every step.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG Personnel

The DoD budget is the largest discretionary budget in the federal government — and it takes roughly two years to build. The process is called PPBE: Planning, Programming, Budgeting, and Execution. If you work in any part of the defense acquisition or budgeting enterprise, understanding PPBE isn't optional. It's how your program gets funded.

This guide breaks down each phase, the key documents produced, who owns the decisions, and the reform effort currently underway to modernize the process.

For broader context on how PPBE fits into defense finance — including color of money, the Anti-Deficiency Act, and EVM — see our pillar guide: Defense Finance for Program Managers: The Complete Guide.

The Four Phases of PPBE

PPBE is often described as a cycle, but in practice it's more of a two-year relay race. Work on Fiscal Year 26 funding starts in FY24. By the time money arrives in your program office, it has passed through four distinct phases — each with its own documents, decisions, and bureaucratic battles.

Phase 1: Planning — Strategy Drives Resources

The PPBE process begins with strategy, not spreadsheets. The National Defense Strategy (NDS) — a document published roughly every two to four years — sets the strategic priorities that the entire defense enterprise is supposed to resource. Below the NDS, the Defense Planning Guidance (DPG) translates those strategic goals into specific programmatic direction for each military department and defense agency.

The DPG tells the services: here are the capability gaps we need to close, here are the threat scenarios we're planning for, and here's roughly how much money you'll have to work with. It's the starting gun for the programming phase.

Who owns it? The Office of the Secretary of Defense (OSD), specifically the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy (USD(P)) and the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff (CJCS), drive the planning phase. Service chiefs and combatant commanders provide input through the Joint Requirements process.

So what for program managers: If your program doesn't map to a stated priority in the DPG or NDS, it will struggle in the programming phase. Program advocates spend months before POM season building the strategy-to-program linkage that makes budget requests credible.

Phase 2: Programming — The Resource Fight

This is where the budget actually gets built — and where the real competition happens. Each military department and major defense agency develops a Program Objective Memorandum (POM): a detailed 5-year spending plan covering all their programs and activities.

The POM covers what's called the FYDP window — the Future Years Defense Program, typically 5 years of planned spending. The first year of the POM (the budget year) will eventually become the formal appropriations request. Years 2–5 are planning projections — important for program continuity, but not appropriated law.

Building the POM is an intensely political process inside each service. Program managers and their advocates compete for limited resources. Decisions are made through a series of internal reviews, with Issue Papers documenting the tradeoffs and debates. Unfunded requirements — programs that didn't make it into the POM — are often captured in separate lists sent to Congress.

When it happens: POM development typically runs from roughly February through May of Year N-2 (two years before execution). The POM is submitted to OSD in the spring/early summer.

OSD review: After submission, OSD analysts review the POMs and may issue Program Decision Memoranda (PDMs) — formal decisions that add, reduce, or redirect resources. This OSD scrub is where DoD-wide priorities override service-specific preferences.

Phase 3: Budgeting — Translating Plans into Budget Lines

Once the POM is finalized, it transitions into the budgeting phase. Each department submits a Budget Estimate Submission (BES) to the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller) — known as OUSD(C). The BES is the formal budget request, broken down into the specific appropriation accounts (O&M, Procurement, RDT&E, etc.) that will eventually appear in the President's Budget.

OUSD(C) and the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) conduct detailed reviews, called Program Budget Reviews (PBRs). Decisions here are documented in Program Budget Decisions (PBDs). By December/January, the budget is finalized.

In February of Year N-1, the President's Budget is submitted to Congress. This is the formal legislative request — a detailed document that specifies exactly how much money the administration is requesting for each program, by appropriation account.

Key document: The President's Budget includes the DoD Budget Request Overview and hundreds of R-forms (for RDT&E) and P-forms (for Procurement) that detail each program's funding and justification. These are public documents — you can read exactly what was requested for any major program.

Congressional Action: Authorizations and Appropriations

After the President's Budget is submitted, Congress takes over. Two parallel processes run simultaneously:

  • Authorization: The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), passed annually by the Armed Services Committees, authorizes programs and sets policy. It doesn't actually appropriate money — it gives DoD permission to spend it.
  • Appropriations: The Defense Appropriations Subcommittees actually fund the programs. Congress may appropriate more, less, or the same amount as the President requested, and may add restrictions (called fences or earmarks).

The ideal outcome: both bills signed by October 1, the start of the new fiscal year. The reality: most years, Congress passes a Continuing Resolution (CR) that funds the government at roughly prior-year rates while the full appropriations bills are negotiated. This creates serious execution headaches for program offices.

Phase 4: Execution — Turning Appropriations into Action

Once appropriations are enacted, OMB apportions the funds — releasing them to the agencies in chunks (typically quarterly) to prevent spending everything in October. The services then allot the funds to budget activities and sub-allot to program offices and commands.

In execution, the key activities are:

  • Obligation: Program offices and contracting officers execute contracts, grants, and other agreements that legally commit the appropriated funds.
  • Expenditure: Contractors and activities deliver work; invoices are paid through the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS).
  • Reporting: Program offices track and report obligations and expenditures against their budget authority. Unobligated balances at fiscal year-end must be returned or deobligated.

The execution phase also involves significant oversight. The Congressional Budget Justification documents submitted the prior year become benchmarks — Congress can and does ask why programs didn't execute as planned.

PPBE Reform: What's Changing in 2025–2026

The PPBE process has been criticized for decades as too slow, too rigid, and misaligned with the speed of modern threats — especially in areas like software, artificial intelligence, and cyber. In 2022, Congress established the Commission on PPBE Reform to study the problem and recommend changes.

The Commission's 2024 final report made more than 100 recommendations, including:

  • Renaming the process to PPBE+ or a successor framework with faster programming cycles for technology insertion.
  • Creating a new "fast lane" for digital and software programs that can't wait two years to receive funding.
  • Strengthening execution-year flexibility — giving program offices more agility to adjust to real-world conditions without requiring elaborate reprogramming actions.
  • Improving the integration between requirements (JCIDS) and budgeting so that new requirements get resourced faster.

As of early 2026, DoD and Congress are still working through which recommendations to implement and how. The core four-phase structure remains in place, but expect incremental changes to timelines and flexibility rules over the next several years.

Why this matters now: If you're a program manager working on software-intensive or AI-enabled programs, the PPBE reform conversation directly affects how your program gets resourced. Watch for new budget exhibit types and modified appropriation guidance in the FY27 and FY28 budget cycles.

Why PPBE Matters for Contractors

Defense contractors often feel like they're on the outside of PPBE looking in — but the process directly shapes their business:

  • POM season is pipeline planning: What gets funded in the POM becomes contracts 18–24 months later. Contractors with strong government relationships know what's in the POM before it's public.
  • Budget exhibits are market intelligence: The R-forms and P-forms in the President's Budget tell you exactly what DoD plans to buy, when, and at what quantities.
  • CR risk is contract risk: Every year that Congress fails to pass appropriations on time, contract awards slip, and contractors face uncertain start dates and cash flow disruptions.
  • Reprogramming actions matter: When OSD or Congress moves money mid-year through a reprogramming or transfer, program offices scramble — and contract priorities shift.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does PPBE stand for?

PPBE stands for Planning, Programming, Budgeting, and Execution. It is the DoD's primary resource management process, used to develop the annual and five-year defense budget. The process runs on a roughly two-year lead time, meaning work on a given fiscal year's budget begins about two years before that fiscal year starts.

What is a POM in DoD?

A Program Objective Memorandum (POM) is the document each military department submits to OSD during the programming phase of PPBE. It's a 5-year spending plan that details how the service proposes to allocate its resources across all programs and activities. The POM is the primary vehicle through which programs fight for funding — if your program isn't in the POM, it won't be in the budget.

What is the difference between the NDAA and the Defense Appropriations Act?

The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) authorizes programs and sets DoD policy — it gives legal permission for programs to exist and establishes rules. The Defense Appropriations Act actually provides the money. Both are required for a program to operate. The NDAA can authorize a program that Congress never funds through appropriations.

How does PPBE reform affect software programs?

The PPBE reform commission highlighted that software and technology programs can't wait two years for funding decisions. Recommendations include creating faster programming pathways, new budget exhibit types for software, and more execution-year flexibility. As of 2026, implementation is ongoing — watch for changes in DoD budget guidance and NDAA provisions over the next few budget cycles.

What happens to unobligated funds at the end of the fiscal year?

It depends on the appropriation type. Most appropriations expire for new obligations at the end of their availability period (1 year for O&M, 2 years for RDT&E, 3 years for Procurement). Unobligated balances must be returned to the Treasury after a 5-year "expired" period. Already-obligated funds can remain available for expenditure longer, depending on the appropriation. Letting funds expire unobligated reflects poorly on program execution and can result in reduced future funding.
