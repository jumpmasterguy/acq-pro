OTA vs. FAR-Based Contracts: When to Use Each and Why It Matters

Other Transaction Authority is the fastest-growing contracting vehicle in DoD. Here's the legal basis, who qualifies, the non-traditional contractor requirement, and when OTA is actually the right call versus a standard FAR contract.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 10 min read · For: Contractor

In 2023, DoD obligated over $20 billion through Other Transaction Authority agreements. A decade ago, that number was under $3 billion. OTA has gone from a niche tool to a mainstream acquisition vehicle — and if you're selling technology to the defense sector and you don't understand OTAs, you're leaving significant opportunity on the table.

But OTAs aren't magic. They have specific qualifying criteria, legal constraints, and pitfalls that catch unprepared companies off guard. This guide breaks down everything you need to know: the legal basis, who qualifies, how OTA compares to FAR contracts, and the pathway from prototype OTA to a real production contract.

For context on where OTAs fit in the broader acquisition system, see The Complete Guide to DoD Defense Acquisition.

What Is Other Transaction Authority (OTA)?

Other Transaction Authority (OTA) is the legal authority that allows DoD to enter into agreements that are not procurement contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements — and therefore are not subject to the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) or the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS). The name "other transactions" comes directly from the statutory language.

There are two distinct OTA statutes:

  • 10 U.S.C. § 4021 — Research OTs: authority to conduct basic, applied, and advanced research through agreements that are not contracts or grants.
  • 10 U.S.C. § 4022 — Prototype OTs: authority to carry out prototype projects directly relevant to enhancing the mission effectiveness of military personnel or for weapon systems and platforms.

Most of the action — and most of the dollar volume — is under prototype OTs (§ 4022). That's what this guide focuses on. Research OTs are a smaller, older tool primarily used by defense research agencies.

Key Insight: Prototype OTAs are not a loophole to avoid oversight — they're a deliberate policy choice by Congress to let DoD work faster with a broader vendor base. The FY2016 NDAA, FY2017 NDAA, and subsequent legislation systematically expanded OTA authority because the traditional FAR-based system was too slow to keep pace with commercial technology development. In April 2025, President Trump directed DoD to expand OTA use as part of broader acquisition reform.

Why DoD Uses OTAs

The core problem OTA solves: the FAR was designed for commodity procurement, not for engaging with cutting-edge commercial technology companies. Companies like SpaceX, Palantir, Anduril, and hundreds of smaller startups built their defense businesses substantially through OTA vehicles — precisely because the FAR made it difficult for them to participate.

Key advantages of OTA over FAR-based contracts:

  • Speed — OTA agreements can be awarded in weeks; FAR Part 15 acquisitions often take 12–24 months from solicitation to award.
  • Flexibility — Parties can negotiate terms not permissible under FAR: IP provisions, data rights, payment structures.
  • Fewer compliance requirements — Cost accounting standards (CAS), certified cost or pricing data, DFARS business system requirements, and many other compliance obligations do not automatically apply to OTAs.
  • Non-traditional access — OTA is specifically designed to bring in vendors who don't normally work with DoD.
  • Prototype-to-production pathway — A successful prototype OTA can lead to a sole-source follow-on production contract without a full competitive recompete.

The Non-Traditional Contractor Requirement

This is the most important eligibility concept for OTA. Prototype OTs under § 4022 are only available when at least one of the following conditions is met:

  1. A non-traditional defense contractor is participating to a significant extent in the prototype project.
  2. At least one-third of the total cost of the prototype project is provided by sources other than the Federal Government (i.e., cost-sharing).
  3. The USD(A&S) or a service acquisition executive determines exceptional circumstances exist that merit use of an OTA (rare).

A non-traditional defense contractor is defined in statute as an entity that is not currently performing and has not performed, for at least one year prior to the solicitation, any contract or subcontract for DoD subject to full coverage under Cost Accounting Standards (CAS). In practice, this means companies that don't currently have large cost-reimbursement DoD contracts.

A company can participate as a subcontractor or team member — not necessarily the prime — and satisfy this requirement for the overall effort. This is why many OTA consortia include both traditional defense primes and non-traditional commercial companies.

For Contractors: If you're a pure commercial company without current CAS-covered DoD contracts, you likely qualify as non-traditional. That's a competitive advantage in the OTA market, not a disqualifier. Lead with it. If you're a large prime, you can satisfy the requirement by teaming with a qualifying non-traditional company or through the cost-share option.

The Prototype-to-Production Pathway

One of OTA's most powerful features — and one of the most misunderstood — is the statutory prototype-to-production pathway under 10 U.S.C. § 4022(f).

If a prototype project is successfully completed under a competitive OTA, DoD may award a follow-on production contract or OTA for that prototype — on a sole-source basis, without additional competition — under two conditions:

  1. The prototype OTA was awarded in a competitive manner (which DoD interprets broadly — it doesn't have to be a formal FAR-based competition), AND
  2. The follow-on production is provided for in the original prototype OTA solicitation.

This is the crown jewel of the OTA framework. A company that wins a competitive prototype OTA and performs well can proceed directly to production without facing a new competition from the incumbent defense prime establishment. It's a genuine path to a production contract for non-traditional companies.

Critical Point: The production follow-on must be contemplated in the original solicitation. Companies that win prototype OTAs where follow-on production wasn't mentioned in the solicitation cannot invoke this authority. Read OTA solicitations carefully for follow-on provisions before deciding to bid.

Major OTA Consortium Vehicles

Rather than running individual OTA competitions for every requirement, DoD has established OTA consortium vehicles — pre-competed agreements with groups of companies. Government customers can access these consortia quickly without a full competition.

Defense Innovation Unit (DIU)

DIU is OSD's commercial technology bridge. It uses Prototype OTAs to rapidly prototype and field commercial technologies for DoD. DIU has specific focus areas including autonomy, cyber, AI/ML, human systems, and space. Their solicitation and award timelines are typically 60–90 days — lightning-fast by defense standards. Companies that have gone through DIU include SpaceX, Joby Aviation, and dozens of AI-focused startups.

AFWERX

The Air Force's innovation arm. AFWERX operates several OTA vehicles including AFWERX OPEN, focused on commercializing emerging technologies. Also manages the Air Force SBIR/STTR programs and has a specific emphasis on dual-use technology.

NIWC Pacific / NIWC Atlantic (formerly SPAWAR)

The Navy's primary OTA vehicles for C5ISR (Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Cyber, Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance) and cybersecurity technologies. NIWC Atlantic operates the CyberWorx OTA consortium.

Army Applications Laboratory (AAL)

The Army's tech accelerator in Austin, Texas. Uses OTA to rapidly test and field commercial solutions for Army requirements. Known for being particularly accessible to startups and small businesses.

SOCOM's SOFWERX

Special Operations Command's OTA vehicle, focused on special operations-relevant technologies. Known for short timelines and openness to unconventional solutions.

When OTA Is the Right Choice

OTA isn't always the right vehicle. Here's a decision framework:

Use OTA when:

  • You're a non-traditional company and FAR compliance would be prohibitive
  • The capability involves novel commercial technology with uncertain government technical requirements
  • Speed to award is critical (operational urgency)
  • IP is a significant concern and you want to negotiate better data rights than DFARS defaults
  • The prototype-to-production pathway is strategically important to your business case
  • Cost-sharing is possible and strengthens your proposal

FAR is probably the right vehicle when:

  • The requirement is well-defined and there's a mature supply base (use FAR Part 12 for commercial items)
  • The program is at ACAT I/II scale and congressional reporting requirements apply
  • The government needs the full contractor accountability framework (DCMA oversight, CAS, etc.)
  • Legal protections of formal bid protest rights are important to the competitive landscape
  • You're an established prime that already has DCAA-approved accounting systems and can compete on FAR terms

OTA Risks and Watchouts

  • No GAO protest rights — If you lose an OTA award and believe the process was flawed, your protest remedies are limited. The Court of Federal Claims (COFC) has jurisdiction over some OTA disputes, but the legal framework is unsettled.
  • Follow-on isn't guaranteed — Even with the § 4022(f) pathway, the government isn't required to pursue follow-on production. OTA prototypes are real investments with real risk of not leading to production.
  • Inspector General scrutiny — DoD IG and GAO have repeatedly flagged improper use of OTA, particularly cases where programs use OTA to avoid competition requirements or acquire commercial items that could be bought under FAR Part 12. Use it legitimately.
  • IP trap — While OTA allows better IP negotiation, companies sometimes negotiate away rights they shouldn't, or fail to understand what happens to their IP if the prototype succeeds and DoD wants to sole-source production to a second manufacturer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the legal authority for DoD OTAs?

DoD OTA authority comes from two statutes in Title 10 of the U.S. Code: 10 U.S.C. § 4021 (research other transactions) and 10 U.S.C. § 4022 (prototype other transactions). The prototype OTA authority under § 4022 is the primary vehicle used for most modern OTA agreements. This authority has been expanded significantly through successive National Defense Authorization Acts, most recently in the FY2023 and FY2024 NDAAs.

Does my company need to be a non-traditional defense contractor to win an OTA?

Not necessarily, but a non-traditional contractor must be involved to a significant extent in the prototype effort — either as the prime or as a subcontractor/team member. Traditional defense contractors can lead an OTA effort as long as a qualifying non-traditional company is meaningfully involved, or the program meets the one-third cost-share alternative. Check the specific OTA solicitation for how the government defines significant participation.

Can an OTA lead to a production contract without competition?

Yes — this is one of OTA's most powerful features. Under 10 U.S.C. § 4022(f), if a prototype OTA was awarded competitively and the original solicitation contemplated a production follow-on, DoD may award a sole-source follow-on production contract or OTA. The production award doesn't require a new competition. This is why companies fight hard to win prototype OTAs even at cost-share or below-cost pricing — the production follow-on is the real prize.

Are OTA agreements subject to GAO bid protests?

Generally no. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has declined jurisdiction over most OTA protests, holding that OTAs are not "contracts" under the Competition in Contracting Act. The Court of Federal Claims (COFC) has asserted limited jurisdiction in some cases. This reduced protest risk is one reason some government customers prefer OTA — but it also means competitors have less recourse if they believe an award was improper.

What is a consortium OTA and how does it work?

A consortium OTA is a master agreement between DoD and a group of pre-screened companies (the consortium). DoD issues solicitations to consortium members for specific projects, and members respond with proposals. This avoids the need to establish a new OTA for every requirement, dramatically speeding up the process. Major examples include DIU, AFWERX, NIWC Pacific, and the Army Applications Laboratory (AAL). Joining a consortium as a member is often a company's first step into the OTA market.
