OTA vs. FAR-Based Contracts: When to Use Each and Why It Matters

DoD's use of Other Transaction Authority has grown about tenfold since 2016. Here's the legal basis, who qualifies, the non-traditional contractor requirement, and when OTA is actually the right call versus a standard FAR contract.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 10 min read · For: Contractor

TL;DR. Prototype OTAs sit outside the FAR, so they can move faster, with negotiable IP terms. It's the rare corner of DoD where having no big CAS-covered contracts on your resume counts as a qualification.

  • New to this? You usually need a non-traditional contractor participating significantly, a team made up only of small businesses or non-traditional firms, or non-federal sources paying a third of the cost.
  • Commercial company? No fully CAS-covered DoD contract in the past year likely makes you non-traditional. Lead with it.
  • Bidding a prototype? Confirm the solicitation and the agreement provide for follow-on production, or there's no sole-source path later.
  • Established prime? FAR often fits well-defined needs better. For an OTA, team with a non-traditional firm, and guard your IP.

In fiscal year 2024, DoD obligated more than $18 billion through Other Transaction Authority agreements. In fiscal 2016 it was $1.8 billion (in inflation-adjusted dollars), according to GAO. OTA has gone from a niche tool to a mainstream acquisition vehicle. If you're selling technology to the defense sector and you don't understand OTAs, you're leaving money on the table.

But OTAs aren't magic. They have specific qualifying criteria, legal constraints, and pitfalls that catch unprepared companies off guard. This guide breaks down everything you need to know: the legal basis, who qualifies, how OTA compares to FAR contracts, and the pathway from prototype OTA to a real production contract.

For context on where OTAs fit in the broader acquisition system, see The Complete Guide to DoD Defense Acquisition.

What Is Other Transaction Authority (OTA)?

Other Transaction Authority (OTA) is the legal authority that allows DoD to enter into agreements that are not procurement contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements, and therefore are not subject to the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) or the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS). The name "other transactions" comes directly from the statutory language.

There are two distinct OTA statutes:

  • 10 U.S.C. § 4021, Research OTs: authority to conduct basic, applied, and advanced research through agreements that are not contracts or grants.
  • 10 U.S.C. § 4022, Prototype OTs: authority to carry out prototype projects directly relevant to enhancing the mission effectiveness of military personnel or for weapon systems and platforms.

Most of the action, and most of the dollar volume, is under prototype OTs (§ 4022). That's what this guide focuses on. Research OTs are a smaller, older tool primarily used by defense research agencies.

Key Insight: Prototype OTAs are not a loophole to avoid oversight. They're a deliberate policy choice by Congress to let DoD work faster with a broader vendor base. The FY2016 NDAA (which made prototype authority permanent), the FY2018 NDAA, and later laws systematically expanded OTA authority because the traditional FAR-based system was too slow to keep pace with commercial technology development. In April 2025, Executive Order 14265 directed DoD to give OTs a "general preference" as part of broader acquisition reform.

Why DoD Uses OTAs

The core problem OTA solves: the FAR was designed for commodity procurement, not for engaging with cutting-edge commercial technology companies. Newer defense companies have used OTs to get in the door. Drone-boat maker Saronic, for example, signed a Navy production OT worth more than $392 million in 2025 (DefenseScoop), and GAO counted about 450 prototype OTs from the Defense Innovation Unit alone between FY2016 and FY2023.

Key advantages of OTA over FAR-based contracts:

  • Speed: OTA agreements can be awarded in weeks or a few months (DIU says as few as 60–90 days), while a major FAR Part 15 source selection can take far longer.
  • Flexibility: Parties can negotiate terms not permissible under FAR: IP provisions, data rights, payment structures.
  • Fewer compliance requirements: Cost accounting standards (CAS), certified cost or pricing data, DFARS business system requirements, and many other compliance obligations do not automatically apply to OTAs.
  • Non-traditional access: OTA is specifically designed to bring in vendors who don't normally work with DoD.
  • Prototype-to-production pathway: A successful prototype OTA can lead to a sole-source follow-on production contract without a full competitive recompete.

The Non-Traditional Contractor Requirement

This is the most important eligibility concept for OTA. Prototype OTs under § 4022(d) are only available when at least one of the following conditions is met:

  1. A non-traditional defense contractor (or a nonprofit research institution) is participating to a significant extent in the prototype project.
  2. All significant participants other than the government are small businesses or non-traditional defense contractors.
  3. At least one-third of the total cost of the prototype project is paid by sources other than the Federal Government (i.e., cost-sharing).
  4. The agency's senior procurement executive determines in writing that exceptional circumstances justify an OT (rare).

A non-traditional defense contractor is defined in statute (10 U.S.C. § 3014) as an entity that is not currently performing and has not performed, for at least one year prior to the solicitation, any contract or subcontract for DoD subject to full coverage under Cost Accounting Standards (CAS). In practice, this means companies that don't currently have large cost-reimbursement DoD contracts.

A company can participate as a subcontractor or team member (not necessarily the prime) and satisfy this requirement for the overall effort. This is why many OTA consortia include both traditional defense primes and non-traditional commercial companies.

For Contractors: If you're a pure commercial company without current CAS-covered DoD contracts, you likely qualify as non-traditional. That's a competitive advantage in the OTA market, not a disqualifier. Lead with it. If you're a large prime, you can satisfy the requirement by teaming with a qualifying non-traditional company or through the cost-share option.

The Prototype-to-Production Pathway

One of OTA's most powerful features, and one of the most misunderstood, is the statutory prototype-to-production pathway under 10 U.S.C. § 4022(f).

DoD may award a follow-on production contract or OTA for that prototype, on a sole-source basis, without additional competition, when these conditions are met:

  1. Competitive procedures were used to select the prototype participants (which DoD interprets broadly; it doesn't have to be a formal FAR-based competition), AND
  2. The participants successfully completed the prototype project, AND
  3. The possibility of follow-on production was stated in both the prototype solicitation and the prototype agreement, as DoD's Other Transactions Guide requires (MITRE AiDA summary).

This is the crown jewel of the OTA framework. A company that wins a competitive prototype OTA and performs well can proceed directly to production without facing a new competition from the incumbent defense prime establishment. It's a genuine path to a production contract for non-traditional companies.

Critical Point: The production follow-on must be contemplated in the original solicitation and the agreement. Companies that win prototype OTAs where follow-on production wasn't mentioned cannot invoke this authority. Read OTA solicitations carefully for follow-on provisions before deciding to bid.

Where to Find OTA Work: Consortia and Innovation Offices

Rather than running individual OTA competitions for every requirement, DoD has established OTA consortium vehicles: pre-competed agreements with groups of companies. Government customers can access these consortia quickly without a full competition. MITRE's AiDA site lists more than 50, including the National Armaments Consortium, the Navy's Information Warfare Research Project, and the Space Force's Space Enterprise Consortium. Innovation offices are the other front door: some award OTs directly, and others help you find the right buyer.

Defense Innovation Unit (DIU)

DIU is OSD's commercial technology bridge. It uses Commercial Solutions Openings and prototype OTs to prototype and field commercial technologies for DoD. Its focus areas include AI/ML, autonomy, cyber and telecom, emerging technology, energy, human systems, and space. DIU says it can award prototype agreements in as few as 60–90 days, lightning-fast by defense standards. GAO counted about 450 DIU prototype OTs from FY2016 through FY2023.

AFWERX

The Department of the Air Force's innovation arm, powered by the Air Force Research Laboratory. AFWERX runs the Air Force SBIR/STTR program, including its Open Topic solicitations, and its site describes Commercial Solutions Openings as a route to rapid prototypes through OT authority. It has a specific emphasis on dual-use technology.

NIWC Pacific / NIWC Atlantic

The Navy's information warfare centers, focused on C5ISR (Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Cyber, Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance) and cybersecurity technologies. A key OT door is the Information Warfare Research Project (IWRP), an OT consortium managed by Advanced Technology International.

Army Applications Laboratory (AAL)

The Army's tech accelerator, based at Capital Factory in Austin, Texas. AAL connects the Army with commercial technology and talent, often funding work through SBIR awards and broad agency announcements.

SOCOM's SOFWERX

Not a contract vehicle itself. SOFWERX is an innovation hub in Tampa set up in 2015 under a partnership intermediary agreement with Special Operations Command. It runs events and assessments that connect companies with SOCOM needs. Known for short timelines and openness to unconventional solutions.

When OTA Is the Right Choice

OTA isn't always the right vehicle. Here's a decision framework:

Use OTA when:

  • You're a non-traditional company and FAR compliance would be prohibitive
  • The capability involves novel commercial technology with uncertain government technical requirements
  • Speed to award is critical (operational urgency)
  • IP is a significant concern and you want to negotiate better data rights than DFARS defaults
  • The prototype-to-production pathway is strategically important to your business case
  • Cost-sharing is possible and strengthens your proposal

FAR is probably the right vehicle when:

  • The requirement is well-defined and there's a mature supply base (use FAR Part 12 for commercial items)
  • The program is at ACAT I/II scale and congressional reporting requirements apply
  • The government needs the full contractor accountability framework (DCMA oversight, CAS, etc.)
  • Formal bid protest protections matter for a high-stakes competition
  • You're an established prime that already has DCAA-approved accounting systems and can compete on FAR terms

OTA Risks and Watchouts

  • Very limited GAO protest rights: If you lose an OTA award and believe the process was flawed, your protest remedies are limited. GAO generally won't review OT awards, except claims that an agency misused OT authority to buy something that needed a procurement contract. The Court of Federal Claims (COFC) has jurisdiction over some OTA disputes, but the legal framework is unsettled.
  • Follow-on isn't guaranteed: Even with the § 4022(f) pathway, the government isn't required to pursue follow-on production. OTA prototypes are real investments with real risk of not leading to production.
  • Inspector General and GAO scrutiny: In 2022 the DoD Inspector General found follow-on production and experimental OTs misreported as prototype awards, and in 2025 GAO found DoD couldn't track which prototypes reached production. Expect questions about whether an OT was the right tool, and use it legitimately.
  • IP trap: While OTA allows better IP negotiation, companies sometimes negotiate away rights they shouldn't, or fail to understand what happens to their IP if the prototype succeeds and DoD wants to sole-source production to a second manufacturer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the legal authority for DoD OTAs?

DoD OTA authority comes from two statutes in Title 10 of the U.S. Code: 10 U.S.C. § 4021 (research other transactions) and 10 U.S.C. § 4022 (prototype other transactions). The prototype OTA authority under § 4022 is the primary vehicle used for most modern OTA agreements. This authority has been expanded significantly through successive National Defense Authorization Acts, most recently the FY2025 NDAA, which changed approval levels and clarified follow-on production (sections 816 and 817).

Does my company need to be a non-traditional defense contractor to win an OTA?

Not necessarily. The most common route is having a non-traditional contractor involved to a significant extent in the prototype effort, either as the prime or as a subcontractor/team member. Traditional defense contractors can lead an OTA effort as long as a qualifying non-traditional company is meaningfully involved, or the program meets the one-third cost-share alternative. (Teams made up entirely of small businesses and non-traditional firms also qualify.) Check the specific OTA solicitation for how the government defines significant participation.

Can an OTA lead to a production contract without competition?

Yes. This is one of OTA's most powerful features. Under 10 U.S.C. § 4022(f), if a prototype OTA was awarded competitively, the prototype was successfully completed, and the solicitation and agreement contemplated a production follow-on, DoD may award a sole-source follow-on production contract or OTA. The production award doesn't require a new competition. This is why companies fight hard to win prototype OTAs even at cost-share or below-cost pricing. The production follow-on is the real prize.

Are OTA agreements subject to GAO bid protests?

Generally no. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) says it does not review awards made under OT authority, because OTs aren't procurement contracts. It will review a protest alleging that an agency is improperly using an OT to buy goods or services that belong under a procurement contract. The Court of Federal Claims (COFC) has asserted limited jurisdiction in some cases. This reduced protest risk is one reason some government customers prefer OTA, but it also means competitors have less recourse if they believe an award was improper.

What is a consortium OTA and how does it work?

A consortium OTA is a master agreement between DoD and a group of pre-screened companies (the consortium). DoD issues solicitations to consortium members for specific projects, and members respond with proposals. This avoids the need to establish a new OTA for every requirement, dramatically speeding up the process. Examples include the National Armaments Consortium, the Navy's Information Warfare Research Project, and the Space Enterprise Consortium. Innovation offices such as DIU and AFWERX are separate doors that also lead to OT work. Joining a consortium as a member is often a company's first step into the OTA market.
