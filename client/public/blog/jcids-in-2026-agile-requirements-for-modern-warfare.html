JCIDS in 2026: Agile Requirements for Modern Warfare

Understanding how the DoD defines needs is more dynamic than ever, shaping every acquisition decision from concept to capability.

By Acqlerate · June 2026 · 7 min read · For: USG Personnel

The Foundation: Why Requirements Still Reign (Even When They Evolve)

As USG personnel, you know that before a single dollar is spent or a line of code is written, the Department of Defense (DoD) must first define what it needs. This critical initial step is the domain of the Joint Capabilities Integration and Development System (JCIDS). For years, JCIDS has been the bedrock, ensuring our warfighters get capabilities that are rigorously vetted, jointly interoperable, and cost-effective. It's the process designed to prevent costly redundancies and ensure every new system truly addresses a capability gap.

But the world doesn't stand still. In 2026, the landscape of defense acquisition is fundamentally different than even a few years ago. Great Power Competition (GPC), the exponential growth of emerging technologies, the dominance of software, and urgent operational needs are demanding a new level of speed and agility. While JCIDS remains the foundational process, its application is rapidly evolving. Understanding this evolution isn't just academic; it's essential for every acquisition professional to successfully deliver capabilities to the warfighter.

The "So What": Requirements, even in their evolving forms, are the foundation. Misunderstand them, and you risk building the wrong thing, too slowly, or too expensively. Get them right, and you empower our warfighters with decisive advantages.

The New Reality: Pressure Points on Traditional JCIDS (2025-2026)

The traditional, often lengthy, waterfall-like approach of JCIDS is under unprecedented pressure. Here’s why the old ways are no longer enough:

  • Great Power Competition (GPC): Adversaries are innovating rapidly. Our multi-year JCIDS cycles can struggle to keep pace, putting us at a disadvantage. We need to field capabilities faster to maintain overmatch.
  • Emerging Technologies: AI, hypersonics, directed energy—these aren't future concepts; they're here now and evolving at warp speed. Defining stable, long-term requirements for technologies that change every six months is a fool's errand.
  • Software-Defined Everything: Modern systems are software-intensive. Agile development, DevSecOps, and continuous delivery are the norm in the commercial world, but they clash with static, document-centric requirements.
  • Urgent Operational Needs: Global instability means immediate, critical needs arise constantly. Waiting years for a new program to cycle through full JCIDS validation is simply not an option for these situations.

These pressures have highlighted critical challenges like the "Valley of Death" for innovative solutions and the perennial struggle with "requirements creep" versus the need for a Minimum Viable Product (MVP). The focus has shifted from perfect, immutable requirements to rapidly fielding effective, iterative capabilities.

The "So What": Don't assume requirements are static documents. The environment demands adaptability. If you're still thinking of requirements as fixed at Milestone B, you're already behind.

JCIDS Adapts: A Family of Requirements Processes Under AAF

The DoD isn't abandoning JCIDS; it's adapting it. We're moving towards a "family of requirements processes" integrated within the Adaptive Acquisition Framework (AAF). This framework, maturing rapidly in 2025-2026, explicitly encourages tailoring and alternative approaches, acknowledging that one size does not fit all.

Here’s a look at how JCIDS is evolving:

Feature Traditional JCIDS Approach Adaptive/Agile Requirements Approach (AAF Pathways)
Requirements Stability Generally static, defined early (CDD/CPD), changes require formal revalidation. Dynamic, evolving through iteration, user feedback, and continuous learning.
Development Cycle Linear, waterfall-like, milestone-driven, often multi-year. Iterative, rapid prototyping/fielding (within 5 years for MTA), continuous integration.
Documentation Focus Comprehensive, detailed documents (CDDs, CPDs) as primary artifacts. Top-level operational needs statements, user stories, product backlogs, digital models.
Primary Goal Achieve full, pre-defined capability; prevent redundancies; ensure interoperability. Rapidly field Minimum Viable Product (MVP); iterate and adapt; maintain decisive advantage.
Risk Tolerance Lower tolerance for change, emphasis on upfront planning to mitigate risk. Higher tolerance for iterative learning, managing risk through frequent feedback loops.

Key mechanisms driving this evolution include:

  • Middle Tier of Acquisition (MTA) Pathway: This pathway is a game-changer for rapid prototyping and rapid fielding. MTA programs often leverage existing validated requirements or develop "top-level" operational needs statements, allowing for quick iteration and user feedback. You'll see many urgent needs, particularly in counter-UAS or initial increments of hypersonics, using MTA.
  • Software Acquisition Pathway (SWP): For software-intensive systems, requirements are managed through backlogs and user stories, evolving continuously rather than being fixed in a Capabilities Development Document (CDD). JADC2 components and warfighting applications are prime examples.
  • Joint Requirements Oversight Council (JROC) Modernization: The JROC is streamlining its processes, focusing on strategic priorities and delegating more authority for lower-level requirements. The goal is faster validation, emphasizing operational context over bureaucratic hurdles.
  • Digital Engineering & Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE): Requirements are increasingly defined, managed, and traced within digital models, not just static documents. This allows for earlier identification of conflicts, better traceability, and more rapid iteration. Programs like Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) are heavily reliant on this.

The "So What": Your pathway choice dictates your requirements approach. Don't default to traditional JCIDS. Understand the AAF pathways and how they enable a faster, more flexible requirements process for your program.

Real-World Impact: Where Agile Requirements are Making a Difference

These policy shifts aren't theoretical; they're actively shaping major DoD initiatives and budget priorities. The FY25/FY26 budget requests, for instance, continue to prioritize funding for programs leveraging MTA and Software Pathways, especially in areas like AI/ML integration, hypersonics, JADC2, and cyber resilience. The **FY25 budget request for R&D is approximately $143.2 billion**, with significant portions allocated to these rapid-development areas.

  • JADC2 Implementation: The continuous integration and development of JADC2 capabilities are prime examples of evolving requirements. Instead of a single CDD, JADC2 "requirements" are being continuously defined and refined through operational experimentation, data analysis, and agile software development cycles across the services. The goal is to declare **Initial Operational Capability (IOC) for key JADC2 components by 2026-2027**.
  • Hypersonic Development: Programs like the Air Force's Hypersonic Attack Cruise Missile (HACM) or the Army's Long-Range Hypersonic Weapon (LRHW) are using rapid prototyping and fielding authorities (often MTA) to accelerate development. Requirements are refined iteratively based on flight tests and technological maturity, with a goal of **fielding initial capabilities in the 2025-2027 timeframe.** The **FY24 budget included over $14 billion for MTA programs**, a trend expected to continue.
  • Innovation Pathways: Organizations like DIU, AFWERX, and Army Futures Command continue to serve as alternative avenues for rapidly identifying and prototyping solutions. DIU's commercial solutions openings (CSOs) are growing, with hundreds of projects annually transitioning to program records within 12-24 months, demonstrating the viability of rapid, non-traditional requirements generation.

The "So What": Look at the budget and major programs—they reflect the shift. If your program isn't leveraging these agile approaches where appropriate, you're likely missing opportunities for speed and efficiency that are now standard practice.

Your Role: Navigating the New Requirements Reality as USG Personnel

For Program Managers and other USG personnel, adapting to this new requirements reality is paramount. It requires a shift in mindset and practical application:

  • Master the AAF: Don't just know it exists; understand all six pathways and proactively select the most appropriate one for your program. Don't default to the traditional pathway out of habit. Your choice of pathway fundamentally changes how you'll manage requirements.
  • Embrace Agility: Especially for software and MTA programs, requirements will be dynamic. Adopt agile management techniques, continuous user feedback loops, and accept that initial requirements will evolve. This means managing requirements as a living artifact, not a static document.
  • Focus on MVPs, Not Gold-Plating: Prioritize fielding minimum viable capabilities quickly to warfighters, then iterate based on their feedback. The goal is to get 80% of the solution into their hands now, rather than 100% in five years.
  • Become Digitally Fluent: Leverage digital engineering and MBSE tools to define, manage, and trace requirements. This enables faster analysis, better collaboration, and more rapid adaptation throughout the lifecycle. If you're still relying solely on static documents, you're missing out on powerful efficiencies.
  • Prioritize Collaboration: Work closely with warfighters, operational commands, and test organizations from the earliest stages. Requirements should be validated through hands-on experience and continuous feedback, not just documentation reviews.

The future of defense acquisition is less about perfectly defined, static requirements and more about rapidly evolving capabilities that meet urgent needs. Your ability to adapt and leverage these new approaches will directly impact our nation's competitive edge.
