ACAT Levels: Your Program's Blueprint for Oversight and Management

Understanding your program's Acquisition Category (ACAT) is fundamental; it dictates the rules of engagement, from who makes key decisions to how your program is managed.

By Acqlerate · July 2026 · 7 min read · For: USG Personnel

TL;DR. Your ACAT level, set by estimated cost, decides who your Milestone Decision Authority is and how hard they look. If old slides show a lower ACAT I line, the program did not get cheaper; the ruler got longer.

  • New to this? ACAT I is more than $1B RDT&E or $4.5B procurement (FY2024 dollars). ACAT II is more than $200M RDT&E or $920M procurement. ACAT III is below that.
  • Government PM? Don't default to Major Capability Acquisition. Pick the pathway that fits and be ready to justify it.
  • Prepping a review? Expect questions on open systems (MOSA), digital engineering, software metrics and supply chain risk.

The ACAT Foundation: Tiers of Oversight

If you're managing a defense acquisition program, you know that not all programs are created equal. Some are massive, multi-billion-dollar endeavors critical to national security, while others are smaller, more focused efforts. The DoD's Acquisition Category (ACAT) system is the foundational mechanism that recognizes this difference, providing a structured framework for oversight based on a program's estimated cost and criticality.

Think of your ACAT level as your program's "tier." This tier determines your Milestone Decision Authority (MDA), the individual empowered to make critical decisions at program milestones, authorizing progression, committing resources, and establishing baselines for cost, schedule, and performance. In 2025-2026, while the Adaptive Acquisition Framework (AAF) provides flexibility, these core ACAT distinctions remain profoundly influential.

ACAT Level Cost Thresholds Typical MDA "So What" for Your Program
ACAT I (MDAPs) RDT&E > $1B OR Procurement > $4.5B (FY2024 dollars) USD(A&S) or delegated SAE Highest level of scrutiny, extensive documentation, formal milestone reviews. These are the "crown jewels" requiring top-level strategic alignment and oversight. Expect intense focus on cost growth, schedule adherence, and technical risks.
ACAT II RDT&E > $200M OR Procurement > $920M (FY2020 dollars) Component Acquisition Executive (the SAE) or a designee Significant oversight at the Service level. While rigorous, there's often more tailoring potential than ACAT I. Focus remains on delivering capability efficiently within Service priorities.
ACAT III Below ACAT II Thresholds Designated by the CAE, often a PEO Most delegated oversight. Allows for greater agility and streamlined decision-making, often closer to the operational user. Emphasis on rapid problem-solving and meeting specific user needs.

Heads up if your old slides disagree: Congress raised the ACAT I line in December 2025, from about $525M RDT&E or $3.1B procurement to $1B or $4.5B (10 U.S.C. 4201). Plenty of briefings still show the old numbers. The program did not get cheaper; the ruler got longer.

So what? Your ACAT level defines your primary audience and the depth of detail they'll demand. An ACAT I program manager will spend considerable time preparing for OSD-level reviews, while an ACAT III PM focuses more on stakeholder engagement within their PEO or command. Misunderstanding your ACAT's implications can lead to wasted effort, delayed approvals, or worse, program failure.

ACATs in the Adaptive Acquisition Framework Era (2025-2026)

While the ACAT system provides the fundamental categorization, the DoD's Adaptive Acquisition Framework (AAF), in place since January 2020, has profoundly reshaped how programs within each ACAT are managed and overseen. The AAF's six tailored pathways (Urgent Capability Acquisition, Middle Tier of Acquisition, Major Capability Acquisition, Software Acquisition, Defense Business Systems, and Acquisition of Services) don't replace ACATs, but rather provide different lanes for them to operate within.

For example, an ACAT I program typically follows the Major Capability Acquisition pathway, adhering to traditional milestones and rigorous documentation. However, an ACAT I-level capability might use the MTA pathway for rapid prototyping, pushing for speed over traditional baseline stability. In such cases, the MDA might delegate decision authority lower, but still maintains strategic oversight to ensure the chosen pathway's principles are met. MDAs are now "pathway enablers," demanding clear justification for pathway selection and consistent execution.

Key Insight: The AAF allows for significant tailoring and delegation, but the underlying ACAT level still sets the initial expectation for MDA involvement. Choosing the right pathway is critical, but so is understanding your ACAT's inherent oversight demands.

One more layer arrived in November 2025. Secretary Hegseth's Warfighting Acquisition System memo moves programs under Portfolio Acquisition Executives (PAEs) over about two years, makes commercial solutions the default, and replaces analyses of alternatives with competitive prototyping where it can. The ACAT I line still comes from statute, so your category still matters. What's changing is who manages the portfolio around you.

Beyond the AAF pathways, other critical policies are shaping MDA oversight across all ACATs:

  • Mandatory Modular Open Systems Approach (MOSA): Required by law. Major programs approved at Milestone A or B after 1 January 2019 must be designed with MOSA to the maximum extent practicable, and other programs are told to do the same (10 U.S.C. 4401). Expect your MDA to ask how your design lets components be added, removed or replaced over time. A weak MOSA plan invites conditions at a milestone.
  • Digital Engineering (DE) as an Expectation: DoD policy (DoDI 5000.88) pushes programs to use digital models and data analytics. This means less paper, more authoritative digital threads at reviews. PMs must present digital artifacts, demonstrating how DE reduces risk and optimizes design, testing, and sustainment.
  • Software Acquisition Pathway (SWP) Maturity: For software-intensive programs, regardless of their hardware ACAT, the SWP emphasizes rapid, iterative development. MDAs focus on metrics like software velocity, defect rates, and user feedback, rather than traditional hardware milestones.
  • Supply Chain Resiliency and Security: Post-COVID and in a volatile geopolitical climate, MDAs demand detailed supply chain risk assessments, diversification strategies, and strong cybersecurity. Programs must identify foreign dependencies and single points of failure, a critical factor for any program approval.

So what? Your ACAT level still determines who your MDA is, but the AAF and other policies dictate what that MDA will focus on. You can't just follow the old rulebook; you must integrate these modern imperatives into your program's strategy, regardless of its ACAT.

Real-World Impact: ACATs in Action (2025-2026)

Let's look at how ACATs and their associated oversight play out in actual programs you might encounter:

  • LGM-35A Sentinel (formerly GBSD): A Classic ACAT I MDAP.

    This intercontinental ballistic missile program is the epitome of an ACAT I MDAP, and it shows what that oversight looks like when things go wrong. After a critical Nunn-McCurdy unit cost breach in 2024, the Air Force now estimates the program will cost at least $141 billion, and the USD(A&S) rescinded Sentinel's Milestone B approval and sent it back for a restructure (GAO, February 2026). As of September 2026, the Air Force still expects to regain Milestone B by the end of 2026, with initial capability in the early 2030s (Air Force Times). For truly massive, strategic programs, the Major Capability Acquisition pathway and its stringent oversight remain paramount.

    So what? For programs like Sentinel, prepare for deep dives into every aspect of cost, schedule, and performance. Data-driven decision-making and meticulous adherence to baselines are non-negotiable. Your stakeholders extend far beyond the DoD, including Congress and the public.

  • F-47 (Next Generation Air Dominance): Big, Fast-Moving, and Mostly Classified.

    On 21 March 2025 the Air Force picked Boeing for the F-47, its NGAD fighter, with an engineering and manufacturing development contract (overview). Many details of the program remain classified, including how its oversight is tailored, so treat confident public claims about its pathway or MDA with caution.

    So what? Even for top-tier capabilities, the AAF allows tailoring. PMs on fast-moving programs must be comfortable accepting more risk in exchange for speed, demonstrating quick iteration and learning from prototypes. Expect MDA reviews on those programs to focus less on fixed baselines and more on progress toward capability delivery.

  • F-35 Technology Refresh 3 (TR-3) & Block 4 Delays: Sustaining an ACAT I MDAP.

    The F-35, a cornerstone ACAT I MDAP, shows that mature programs still get intense scrutiny. GAO reported in September 2025 that Block 4 modernization is at least five years behind its original schedule and more than $6 billion over its original estimate, that the $1.9 billion Technology Refresh 3 (TR-3) upgrade drove late deliveries (all 110 jets delivered in 2024 were late, by 238 days on average), and that total life-cycle costs exceed $2 trillion (GAO-25-107632). Software integration, sustainment, and timely delivery of promised upgrades are where the oversight bites.

    So what? For sustainment and upgrade programs, your ACAT status means continued, rigorous oversight. Software integration, supply chain resilience for parts, and lifecycle cost management become paramount. MDAs will demand transparency and solid plans for overcoming unforeseen challenges.

Navigating Your ACAT: Key Takeaways for USG Personnel

Understanding your program's ACAT level isn't just an administrative detail; it's your strategic roadmap for success. Here's what you need to internalize:

  • Master the AAF, Don't Default: Don't automatically slot into Major Capability Acquisition. Explore MTA, Software Acquisition, or other pathways if they better suit your program's needs, but always be prepared to justify your choice to the MDA based on your ACAT's inherent oversight expectations.
  • MOSA and Digital Engineering are Table Stakes: These aren't just buzzwords; they're non-negotiable requirements for demonstrating technical maturity and future adaptability at any ACAT level. Your plans and execution for open architectures and a comprehensive digital thread will be scrutinized.
  • Software Demands a Different Mindset: For software-intensive components, shift from fixed milestones to continuous delivery and user feedback. MDAs will expect different metrics and review cycles, focusing on velocity, security, and demonstrated capability.
  • Proactive Supply Chain Management: Your MDA will demand transparency on foreign dependencies, single points of failure, and solid mitigation plans. Integrate supply chain resilience into your program planning from day one.
  • Data-Driven Decisions are King: Every justification to your MDA must be backed by solid, verifiable data. Use digital engineering tools to provide comprehensive insights into cost, schedule, performance, and risk.
  • Know Your Audience: Your ACAT level determines your primary MDA, but also the broader network of stakeholders (OSD staff, Congress, operational users) who influence decisions. Understand their priorities and anticipate their questions.

So what? Your ACAT level is the foundation, but the modern acquisition environment layers on specific expectations for speed, agility, and technical rigor. Success now means not just knowing your ACAT, but also mastering how its oversight is shaped by the AAF and evolving DoD policies.
