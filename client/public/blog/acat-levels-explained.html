ACAT Levels Explained: Why Your Program's Category Determines Everything

The dollar thresholds, decision authorities, and oversight requirements that make ACAT I programs operate completely differently from ACAT III — and what it means for how your program runs.

By Acqlerate · January 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG Personnel

Ask a program manager what their ACAT level is and you'll immediately know something important about their day-to-day reality. An ACAT ID program manager is swimming in statutory reporting requirements, briefing OSD leadership, and coordinating with CAPE on independent cost estimates. An ACAT III program manager might be moving in weeks where the ACAT I manager moves in years.

ACAT — Acquisition Category — is one of the first things established for a new defense program, and it shapes nearly everything about how that program is managed. This guide covers every level, the dollar thresholds that define them, who makes decisions at each level, and what changes in practice.

This article is part of our series on the DoD acquisition system. For the full context, start with The Complete Guide to DoD Defense Acquisition.

What Is an Acquisition Category?

An Acquisition Category (ACAT) is a classification assigned to defense acquisition programs that determines the level of oversight, reporting, and decision authority they require. The classification is based primarily on estimated program cost — specifically, estimated Research, Development, Test & Evaluation (RDT&E) costs and total procurement costs.

The governing authority is DoDI 5000.85 (Major Capability Acquisition) and DoDD 5000.01 (the Defense Acquisition System). Dollar thresholds are expressed in FY2020 constant dollars and are adjusted periodically.

Key Insight: ACAT level isn't permanent. If a program's costs grow significantly, the Milestone Decision Authority (MDA) is required to consider recategorization — typically when cost growth gets within 10% of the next higher ACAT threshold. Cost growth that triggers recategorization is a serious event, often accompanied by a Nunn-McCurdy breach notification to Congress.

ACAT I: Major Defense Acquisition Programs

ACAT I programs are the biggest, most complex, most expensive programs in DoD's portfolio — formally called Major Defense Acquisition Programs (MDAPs). These are defined in 10 U.S.C. § 4201 (formerly § 2430) and are triggered when a program's total estimated cost crosses the $525M RDT&E or $3.065B procurement thresholds (in FY2020 constant dollars).

Real Examples of ACAT I Programs

  • F-35 Lightning II — The most expensive defense acquisition program in history, with lifetime costs projected to exceed $1.7 trillion.
  • B-21 Raider — Next-generation strategic bomber; ACAT ID with DAE as MDA.
  • DDG-51 Arleigh Burke-class destroyer — Ongoing production with ACAT I oversight.
  • Sentinel ICBM (formerly GBSD) — Replacement for the aging Minuteman III missile; faced significant cost restructuring.

What ACAT ID Means in Practice

At ACAT ID, the Defense Acquisition Executive — the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment (USD(A&S)) — is the Milestone Decision Authority for all major milestone decisions (A, B, and C). Every milestone requires:

  • An independent cost estimate from CAPE (Cost Assessment and Program Evaluation)
  • A Selected Acquisition Report (SAR) submitted to Congress annually and at each milestone
  • An Acquisition Program Baseline (APB) with approved cost, schedule, and performance parameters
  • An Independent Operational Test & Evaluation (IOT&E) before Full-Rate Production
  • Nunn-McCurdy reporting to Congress if cost growth exceeds 15% (significant) or 25% (critical) above the APB baseline

The FY2016 NDAA introduced ACAT IB, which made the Service Acquisition Executive the default MDA for MDAPs reaching Milestone A after October 1, 2016, unless the SecDef designates otherwise. This was intended to push oversight back to the services and reduce OSD bottlenecks.

ACAT II: Major Systems

ACAT II programs are major systems — significant enough to warrant elevated oversight, but not at the MDAP threshold. They're governed by 10 U.S.C. § 3041 (formerly § 2302d) and defined by RDT&E costs exceeding $200M or procurement costs exceeding $920M in FY2020 constant dollars.

The Milestone Decision Authority for ACAT II is the Component Acquisition Executive (CAE) — typically the Service Acquisition Executive (SAE) or a delegated senior official within the military department.

What Changes at ACAT II

Compared to ACAT I, ACAT II programs have less OSD-level involvement. Key differences:

  • No SAR requirement (Congress doesn't receive the same level of standardized reporting)
  • No mandatory CAPE independent cost estimate (though services may conduct their own)
  • Milestone decisions made at the Service level, not OSD
  • Still requires an Acquisition Program Baseline and formal milestone reviews
  • Can move faster than ACAT I because fewer OSD touchpoints are required

With roughly $144.4 billion in DoD FY2019 budget designated for ACAT II and III programs combined, these programs collectively represent the largest share of DoD acquisition activity by program count — even if individual programs are smaller than MDAPs.

ACAT III: The Majority of Programs by Count

ACAT III is the entry-level classification — programs below the major system dollar thresholds and not specifically designated by the MDA as a higher ACAT. Most programs in DoD's acquisition portfolio fall here by count.

The MDA for ACAT III programs is designated by the Component Acquisition Executive. In practice, that often means a PEO or a senior program official. Documentation requirements are streamlined significantly compared to ACAT I and II, and milestone reviews may be informal or waived entirely depending on program characteristics.

Why ACAT III Still Matters

Don't mistake "lower oversight" for "unimportant." Many ACAT III programs are operationally critical. And because ACAT III represents a massive share of contract dollars in aggregate, contractors spend more time working on ACAT III programs than on flagship MDAPs. For a company trying to break into DoD, ACAT III programs are often the entry point.

For Contractors: Don't spend all your time chasing ACAT I programs. The majority of DoD contract awards — by count — sit at ACAT II and III levels. These programs move faster, have less competition from the defense prime establishment, and are often where innovative small businesses can compete effectively.

ACAT IV: The Army's Additional Category

ACAT IV is Army-specific and applies to programs below ACAT III thresholds. The Army Acquisition Executive may delegate MDA authority for ACAT IV programs to a PEO, who may further delegate to a project manager at the Colonel or civilian equivalent level. The thresholds are roughly <$100M in RDT&E and <$400M in procurement (in FY2014 constant dollars) — though these are guidelines, not hard mandates.

For all practical purposes, ACAT IV programs operate with the maximum flexibility and minimum documentation burden in the Army's acquisition system. They're often the proving ground for innovative capabilities and non-traditional contractors.

How ACAT Designation Works in Practice

When a new program is being initiated, the acquiring organization submits a program identification and the appropriate authority assigns an ACAT level based on cost estimates. If costs change significantly, recategorization is required. Programs that breach ACAT I thresholds from a lower level must go through a formal recategorization process — and the MDA, documentation requirements, and oversight structure all change accordingly.

One nuance: the Secretary of Defense can designate any program as a "Special Interest" ACAT I regardless of cost. This is typically done for programs with significant technological complexity, high congressional interest, or national security criticality. Programs that already meet MDAP thresholds cannot receive the Special Interest designation.

Common ACAT Mistakes to Avoid

  • Underestimating initial cost to avoid ACAT I thresholds — this almost always catches up with a program later, leading to costly recategorization mid-stream.
  • Assuming ACAT III means no oversight — the MDA still has authority to impose requirements, and contract oversight (DCMA/DCAA) applies regardless of ACAT level.
  • Ignoring PPBE implications of ACAT level — ACAT I programs have visibility in the President's Budget; ACAT III programs may not, making them more vulnerable to cuts.
  • Confusing MDA authority — just because MDA is delegated to the SAE doesn't mean OSD has no visibility. Political and policy interest can bring OSD attention to any program.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between ACAT I and ACAT II?

ACAT I programs — called Major Defense Acquisition Programs — exceed $525M in RDT&E or $3.065B in procurement costs (FY2020 dollars). They require OSD-level (or Component Head) Milestone Decision Authority, annual Selected Acquisition Reports to Congress, and independent CAPE cost estimates. ACAT II programs are "major systems" below those thresholds (RDT&E >$200M or procurement >$920M) and have Service-level MDA with less OSD involvement and no SAR requirement.

Who is the Milestone Decision Authority for an ACAT ID program?

The Defense Acquisition Executive — currently the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment (USD(A&S)) — is the MDA for ACAT ID programs. Post-FY2016 NDAA, ACAT IB programs (MDAPs reaching Milestone A after October 2016) use the Service Acquisition Executive as the default MDA unless the SecDef designates otherwise.

Can an ACAT level change after a program starts?

Yes. ACAT levels can be revised — up or down — based on updated cost estimates. The MDA is required to consider recategorization when cost growth approaches 10% of the next higher ACAT threshold. Being recategorized to ACAT I mid-program is a significant event: it triggers new documentation requirements, elevates the MDA, and often requires a congressional notification if the program reaches Nunn-McCurdy breach levels.

What is a Nunn-McCurdy breach?

The Nunn-McCurdy Act (now codified in 10 U.S.C. § 4371) requires DoD to notify Congress when an ACAT I program's unit cost increases significantly (15%) or critically (25%) above the baseline established at Milestone B. A critical breach requires the Secretary of Defense to certify the program or terminate it. Several major programs — including the F-35 — have experienced Nunn-McCurdy breaches.

Does ACAT level apply to all AAF pathways?

ACAT levels primarily apply to the Major Capability Acquisition (MCA) pathway. Programs under the Software Acquisition Pathway, MTA, UCA, or Acquisition of Services pathways generally don't carry formal ACAT designations — though MTA programs still cannot exceed ACAT I cost thresholds. The ACAT framework was designed for the traditional hardware acquisition lifecycle and is most relevant to MCA programs.
