Why Most Defense Proposals Lose Before Anyone Writes a Word

The real battle isn't your prose, it's whether you mapped Section L to Section M before you opened a blank document.

By Acqlerate · September 2026 · 6 min read · For: Contractor

The Proposal Was Dead on Arrival, and Nobody Noticed

Here's an uncomfortable truth that took me years to accept: most losing proposals aren't bad. The writing is fine. The graphics are clean. The past performance citations are relevant. And they lose anyway, because somewhere between reading the solicitation and drafting Volume I, the team answered a question the government never asked.

This isn't a writing problem. It's a discipline problem. Specifically, it's a failure to separate two documents that live right next to each other in every RFP but do completely different jobs: Section L and Section M.

If you've been in this business a while, you already know the terms. But knowing the terms and living by them are two different things. So let's slow down and actually look at why so many capable teams still get this wrong.

Section L Tells You What to Turn In. Section M Tells You How You'll Be Judged.

Think of Section L as the packing list for a trip and Section M as the actual itinerary the customs officer is going to check against. Section L (Instructions to Offerors) says: submit a technical volume, no more than 40 pages, address these five sub-factors, use this font, don't exceed this page count. It's procedural. It's about form and content requirements.

Section M (Evaluation Factors for Award) is a completely different animal. It tells you how the government will score what you submit, what matters more than what, and often what "good" actually looks like in the evaluator's head. Section M is where the real scoring logic lives.

Here's the trap. Because Section L tells you exactly what to write about, teams treat it like a to-do list, check every box, and assume they're done. But Section M is the actual scorecard. You can be 100 percent compliant with Section L and still score poorly, because compliance and persuasion are not the same thing.

Where Proposals Actually Die

Most proposal failures I've seen trace back to one of three quiet mistakes, none of which show up until the debrief:

  • Writing to the outline, not the criteria. The team faithfully follows the Section L structure (heading by heading, sub-factor by sub-factor) but never asks, "what is the evaluator actually going to give us credit for here?" That question only gets answered in Section M.
  • Burying the discriminator. Section M usually tells you, directly or indirectly, what the customer values most (technical risk, past performance recency, small business utilization, whatever it is). Teams often spend equal ink on every sub-factor instead of weighting their content the way the government weights its evaluation.
  • Assuming compliance equals persuasion. Technically responsive is not the same as competitively compelling. You can answer every Section L instruction and still leave an evaluator with nothing to actually score you well on.

The fix isn't more writing talent. It's discipline, applied early, before the outline exists.

Here's the mental model that fixes most of this: Section L is your table of contents. Section M is your grading rubric. If you only outline from L and never cross-check against M, you're building a beautifully organized document that answers the wrong question really well.

The Bridge: Building a Compliance and Scoring Matrix

The single highest-leverage thing a capture or proposal manager can do, before a single sentence is drafted, is build a crosswalk that connects every Section L requirement to its corresponding Section M evaluation criterion, and then to where in your proposal you'll actually address it. This is sometimes called a compliance matrix, sometimes a proposal outline, sometimes just "the map." Whatever you call it, skipping it is how good writers produce losing proposals.

The matrix does three things at once. It proves you're compliant (satisfying Section L). It shows you where the scoring weight actually sits (from Section M). And it forces an honest conversation, early, about whether your content plan matches what will actually earn points.

Below is a simple version you can build for any solicitation in about an hour. Copy it into a spreadsheet before you write anything else.

Notice what this table forces you to do: it makes you rank your own effort by what's actually worth points, not by what's easiest to write or what you're proudest of. That "Weighting Notes" column is where most teams skip the hard conversation. Don't skip it.

What to Actually Do Differently, Starting With Your Next RFP

Here's the practical version of everything above, stripped down to actions:

  • Read Section M before you outline anything, even before you finish reading Section L. Know how you'll be judged before you plan what you'll say.
  • Build the crosswalk table on day one of the proposal effort, not after the first draft. It should live in front of every writer, every day.
  • Rank your own writing time by evaluation weight, not by page count or personal comfort. If risk mitigation is 40 percent of the technical score, it shouldn't get the same word count as your corporate overview.
  • Ask, section by section, "if I were the evaluator with this scoring criterion in hand, would this paragraph earn me points, or just prove I read the instructions?"
  • Run a pink team or color review specifically against Section M language, not just against Section L compliance. Compliance reviews catch missing pages. They don't catch missing persuasion.

None of this requires more talent, more hours, or a bigger team. It requires deciding, early and deliberately, that Section M runs the show and Section L just tells you the shape of the container. Get that order right, and you've already out-executed a good chunk of your competition, before you've written a single sentence they'll ever read.
