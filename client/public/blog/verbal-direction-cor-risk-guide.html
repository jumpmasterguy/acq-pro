Your COR Said "Just Start." Congrats, You May Now Own a Contract Dispute.

Two words that feel helpful and can quietly become a REA. Here's how to say yes to your COR without saying yikes to your contracts department.

By Acqlerate · September 2026 · 7 min read · For: Contractor

The Moment That Feels Routine (And Isn't)

Here's a scene that plays out on defense programs every week. Your COR calls or emails and says something like, "go ahead and get started on that," or "we need this done, can your team handle it." It sounds so harmless it could double as small talk. You say yes, your team starts working, and everyone moves on with their day, feeling productive and helpful.

Except your COR almost never has the authority to tell you to do new work. Only your Contracting Officer (the CO) can direct changes to your contract. The COR's job is to watch the game, not call the plays. That gap between "sounds like a normal Tuesday" and "is actually a legally loaded request" is where a lot of contract disputes quietly get born, usually without anyone meaning any harm at all.

Why This Isn't Just a Technicality

When a contractor performs work based on informal direction from someone without contracting authority, it's called a constructive change. You did something outside your original scope because someone with government-sounding authority told you to. The FAR built an entire system to prevent unauthorized people from committing the government to extra costs, and a COR giving verbal direction walks right up to that tripwire wearing a blindfold.

Two things can happen from here, and neither one involves confetti:

  • The government refuses to pay for it. Since the direction didn't come through the CO, the government can argue it never actually authorized the extra work or cost, leaving you to eat it.
  • You end up filing a claim to get paid anyway. You can pursue a Request for Equitable Adjustment (REA) or a formal claim under the Contract Disputes Act, but that means proving the direction happened, proving it changed your scope, and waiting months (sometimes years) for a decision.

The core rule to remember: a COR can tell you what your contract already says. Only a CO can tell you something new. If what you're hearing sounds like new scope, new cost, or new schedule, that's a Contracting Officer conversation, not a COR one, no matter how nicely it was asked.

What a COR Can and Can't Do

CORs aren't villains here, and most of them are genuinely trying to keep your program moving. The issue isn't intent, it's authority. Think of it like a very competent stage manager telling an actor to hit their mark. They can absolutely tell you where to stand. They cannot rewrite the play.

What To Actually Do When It Happens To You

You don't need to be difficult about this, and flatly refusing can damage a working relationship you'll need for years. The fix is procedural, not personal, and it takes about five minutes.

  • Get it in writing, even informally. Reply to the verbal or email direction with something like, "Confirming your request that we [do X]. This appears outside our current SOW, we'll proceed once we have direction from the Contracting Officer." That single email is often the difference between getting paid and eating the cost.
  • Loop in the CO immediately, not after the work is done. A quick heads-up now is a five-minute email. Doing it after three weeks of unauthorized work is a claim, and a considerably less fun email to write.
  • Know your COR's actual delegation. Every COR is appointed with a letter that spells out exactly what they can and can't approve. If you've never seen it, ask for it. It's not adversarial, it's basic contract hygiene, like checking the expiration date before you cook with it.
  • Document everything, always. Even when direction comes through proper channels, keep a paper trail. Constructive changes usually aren't malicious, they're just informal habits that catch up with someone eventually. Don't let it be you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this apply even if the COR outranks me or seems very senior?
Rank doesn't grant contracting authority. Authority comes from the appointment letter and the FAR, not from seniority or how convincing someone sounds on a call. A general or a GS-15 COR still can't direct new scope without the CO.

What if refusing the request would delay a mission-critical need?
You can still move fast, just document it as you go. A same-day email to the CO copying the COR usually gets a quick answer, and it protects you either way.

Can a constructive change ever work in the contractor's favor?
Yes, if you document it properly. Contractors have successfully recovered costs through REAs and claims when they can show the government directed the work, even informally. The paperwork is what turns "extra work we ate" into "extra work we got paid for."

Is this covered under a specific FAR clause?
The general concept lives under the Changes clause (FAR 52.243) and the government's broader authority rules in FAR Part 1 and Part 43. Your specific contract will reference which Changes clause applies to your contract type.
