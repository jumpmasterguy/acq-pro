DCAA vs. DCMA: What's the Difference and Why You Need to Know

Both agencies oversee defense contractors — but they have completely different missions, tools, and triggers. Here's how to tell them apart and what each one means for your program or business.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG & Contractor

If you're a defense contractor, two agencies will have more impact on your day-to-day operations than almost any other part of the government: DCAA and DCMA. They're often mentioned in the same breath. They're frequently confused. And they have the authority to delay payments, flag findings to your contracting officer, and — in serious cases — put your contract at risk.

They also do entirely different things.

Understanding the distinction isn't just useful knowledge. It shapes how you set up your accounting system, how you prepare for reviews, and what you should do when either agency shows up. This guide covers both agencies, what triggers their involvement, and what contractors and program managers need to do to stay on the right side of each.

For the broader defense finance context — PPBE, color of money, and EVM — see our pillar guide: Defense Finance for Program Managers: The Complete Guide.

The Core Distinction in One Sentence

DCAA audits whether your costs are legitimate. DCMA ensures your performance is on track.

DCAA is an accounting and audit agency. Its job is to verify that what contractors are billing the government is allowable under the FAR/DFARS cost principles, allocable to the contract, and reasonable in amount.

DCMA is a contract administration agency. Its job is to ensure contractors are actually performing what the contract requires — on cost, on schedule, and at the required quality level.

DCAA Deep Dive: The Audit Agency

The Defense Contract Audit Agency was established in 1965 to provide a single, centralized audit function for DoD contract costs. DCAA operates under the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller) and has roughly 5,000 auditors assigned to field offices near major defense contractor locations.

What DCAA Audits

DCAA conducts several distinct types of audits, each targeting a different aspect of contractor finance:

  • Accounting System Audits: Before a contractor can perform cost-reimbursable work, DCAA reviews their accounting system to ensure it can properly segregate contract costs, identify allowable vs. unallowable costs, and produce reliable billing data. An inadequate accounting system can disqualify a contractor from cost-type contracts.
  • Forward Pricing Rate Audits: When contractors negotiate Forward Pricing Rate Agreements (FPRAs) — pre-negotiated rates for indirect costs like overhead and G&A — DCAA reviews the supporting data. These rates become the basis for cost estimates in future proposals.
  • Proposal Audits: For proposals with a total cost over $2 million that require submission of certified cost or pricing data (under the Truth in Negotiations Act / TINA), DCAA audits whether the proposed costs are realistic and compliant.
  • Incurred Cost Audits: At the end of the contract (or annually, for cost-type contracts), contractors submit an Incurred Cost Submission (ICS). DCAA audits actual costs claimed against the contract to verify allowability and allocability. This can happen years after the work was performed.
  • Floor Checks: Periodic unannounced visits to verify that timekeeping practices match what's being billed — employees are actually working on the jobs they're charging.

The Allowability-Allocability-Reasonableness Framework

Every dollar a contractor bills the government must pass three tests under FAR Part 31:

  • Allowable: The cost type must not be explicitly prohibited by FAR (entertainment, lobbying, and certain advertising costs are classic examples of unallowable costs).
  • Allocable: The cost must be reasonably assignable to the contract — you can't charge personal travel to a government contract.
  • Reasonable: The amount must be what a prudent businessperson would pay in similar circumstances. Paying $10,000 for a chair that's commercially available for $500 is not reasonable, regardless of whether it's technically allocable.

Common DCAA finding: Unallowable costs buried in overhead pools. If a contractor includes prohibited costs (like executive bonuses above statutory caps, or country club memberships) in indirect cost pools that are then applied to government contracts, DCAA will question those costs. The government doesn't owe them — and the contractor may have to return money already paid.

What Triggers a DCAA Audit

DCAA prioritizes its audit work based on risk. Higher-risk situations that typically trigger DCAA attention include:

  • New contractor seeking to perform cost-reimbursable work (pre-award accounting system audit)
  • Pricing proposals over $2 million requiring certified cost or pricing data
  • Annual Incurred Cost Submissions (ICS) — all cost-type contractors must submit these
  • Prior audit findings with unresolved questioned costs
  • Anomalous billing patterns flagged by DFAS or the contracting officer
  • Contracting officer request (COs can request a DCAA audit at any time)

DCAA Compliance: What Contractors Must Do

Staying DCAA-compliant is not just about having the right software. The key requirements are:

  • Adequate accounting system: The system must meet the 18 criteria in DFARS 252.242-7006. This includes proper segregation of direct vs. indirect costs, accurate timekeeping, and the ability to produce reliable contract-level cost data.
  • Timekeeping discipline: Every employee who charges time to government contracts must record their time accurately and daily (or at least contemporaneously). After-the-fact reconstruction of timesheets is a serious deficiency.
  • Unallowable cost identification: The accounting system must separately identify and exclude unallowable costs from billings. Commingling allowable and unallowable costs in the same accounts is a finding.
  • Incurred Cost Submission deadlines: ICS must be submitted no later than 6 months after fiscal year-end. Late submissions can trigger audit holds on final billing.

DCMA Deep Dive: The Contract Administration Agency

The Defense Contract Management Agency was established in 2000, consolidating contract administration functions that had previously been scattered across the military services. DCMA employs roughly 12,000 government civilians and provides contract administration services to DoD and other federal agencies.

What DCMA Does

  • Contract surveillance: DCMA assigns a Contract Management Office (CMO) and an Administrative Contracting Officer (ACO) to significant defense contracts. The ACO acts on behalf of the Procuring Contracting Officer (PCO) for day-to-day contract administration.
  • Quality assurance: DCMA quality assurance representatives (QARs) conduct surveillance of contractor manufacturing and quality management systems, verifying that products meet contract requirements before acceptance.
  • EVM surveillance: On EVM-required contracts, DCMA analysts review monthly Contract Performance Reports (CPRs), conduct Integrated Baseline Reviews (IBRs), and flag performance concerns to program managers. (See our companion guide: EVM for Beginners: Earned Value Management in Plain English.)
  • Property administration: DCMA tracks government property in the custody of contractors — a significant responsibility given that billions of dollars of government-owned equipment sits in contractor facilities.
  • CPARS input: DCMA is often the source of performance input for Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System (CPARS) ratings — the official record that follows contractors into future source selections.

What Triggers DCMA Involvement

DCMA is automatically assigned to most significant DoD contracts. Specific triggers for heightened DCMA attention include:

  • EVM-required contracts (DCMA is the designated EVM surveillance agency)
  • Cost-type contracts requiring property administration
  • Contracts with quality control requirements (manufacturing, testing)
  • Programs with cost or schedule performance concerns flagged by the program office
  • Contracts where the buying command has delegated specific ACO functions to DCMA

DCMA Findings and Their Impact on Programs

DCMA findings carry real consequences for both programs and contractors:

  • EVM corrective actions: If DCMA determines that a contractor's EVMS is not compliant or that the contractor is manipulating EV data, they can issue corrective action requests. Repeated or unresolved findings can lead to EVMS decertification — which can affect the contractor's ability to win future EVM-required contracts.
  • Quality nonconformances: DCMA can reject nonconforming products, which triggers corrective action costs that are typically unallowable and borne by the contractor.
  • CPARS ratings: DCMA surveillance data feeds into CPARS assessments. A pattern of DCMA findings translates into lower past performance ratings that affect future competitive bids.
  • Show-cause and cure notices: For serious performance failures, the ACO can issue a show-cause letter (asking why the contract shouldn't be terminated for default) or a cure notice (demanding correction of a specific deficiency within a defined period).

For program managers: DCMA is your representative in the contractor's facility. Build a relationship with your DCMA ACO. If they're seeing problems you don't know about, that's a process failure — not just a contractor problem. Regular communication with your DCMA team is a sign of a well-run program office.

How DCAA and DCMA Interact

DCAA and DCMA are separate agencies with distinct missions, but they interact on a regular basis:

  • Both report findings to the cognizant contracting officer (often the DCMA ACO for ongoing contracts).
  • DCAA may request DCMA assistance for floor checks or property verification during audits.
  • DCMA ACOs can request DCAA audits when they identify billing irregularities or cost concerns during contract administration.
  • Both agencies' findings can affect payment — DCAA through direct questioned costs and payment holds, DCMA through withholding of final payments pending quality acceptance or EVM compliance.

For contractors, the practical lesson is that DCAA and DCMA are both present in your program, and a finding by either agency will reach your contracting officer. Managing both relationships proactively is essential.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between DCAA and DCMA?

DCAA (Defense Contract Audit Agency) is an audit organization that reviews contractor accounting systems, cost proposals, incurred costs, and billing practices. DCMA (Defense Contract Management Agency) is a contract administration organization that monitors contractor performance — cost, schedule, quality, and EVM compliance. DCAA audits your books; DCMA watches your work.

Does every defense contractor deal with DCAA?

Not every contractor, but any contractor performing cost-reimbursable (cost-plus) work or submitting pricing proposals over $2 million requiring certified cost data will encounter DCAA. All cost-type contractors must submit annual Incurred Cost Submissions (ICS) that are subject to DCAA audit. Contractors who exclusively perform firm-fixed-price commercial work generally have less DCAA exposure, though they may still be subject to accounting system reviews if they pursue cost-type contracts.

What happens if DCAA questions my costs?

DCAA issues an audit report with "questioned costs" — amounts the auditor believes are not allowable, allocable, or reasonable. The contracting officer (not DCAA) makes the final determination on whether to disallow the costs. If costs are disallowed, the contractor must either repay amounts already paid or accept reduced final billings. Repeated or significant questioned costs can also affect the contractor's ability to win future contracts.

What does DCMA do during an Integrated Baseline Review (IBR)?

An IBR is a joint government-contractor review of the Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB) on an EVM-required contract, typically conducted within 6 months of contract award. DCMA facilitates the IBR on behalf of the program office, reviewing whether the baseline accurately represents the contracted scope, is technically achievable, and is properly resourced. IBR findings that the baseline is unrealistic are an early warning of future cost or schedule problems.

Can DCMA hold up payments?

Yes. DCMA Administrative Contracting Officers have authority over certain contract administration decisions that affect payment, including acceptance of deliverables, property accounting, and — on cost-type contracts — certain aspects of voucher approval. DCMA quality nonconformances can delay product acceptance and therefore delay final payment. On EVM-required contracts, significant EVMS deficiencies can lead to withholds on interim payments until corrective actions are taken.
