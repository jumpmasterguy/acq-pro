Best Tools for GovCon Proposal Managers in 2026

From RFP response software to AI research tools, here's the stack serious proposal managers are actually using — and what's worth your budget.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 11 min read · For: Contractor

Proposal management in GovCon is brutal without the right tools. You're juggling multiple contributors, tight RFP deadlines, page limits, compliance matrices, and evaluation criteria — all while trying to write something that actually wins. The good news is that the software landscape has improved significantly, and AI tools in particular have changed what's possible for small and mid-size teams that can't afford a 30-person proposal center.

This guide breaks down the tools worth knowing across three categories: proposal writing and management, program/project tracking, and AI. For each one, we've included real-world notes on price, fit, and where it falls short. At the end, there's a recommended stack for small, mid-size, and large GovCon teams.

Proposal Writing & Management Tools

Loopio

Loopio is one of the strongest RFP response platforms on the market. It's built around a content library where your team stores approved, reusable answers to common proposal questions — and then uses automation and AI to pull those answers into new RFP responses. For teams responding to a high volume of RFPs, this dramatically cuts the time spent on standard sections like corporate experience, past performance templates, and technical boilerplate.

The platform supports multi-contributor workflows with reviews, assignments, and version tracking — which matters a lot when you have subject matter experts, capture managers, and proposal writers all touching the same document under deadline pressure. Integrations with Salesforce, Microsoft, and Google Workspace are solid.

The price point is real: Loopio is designed for mid-to-large teams and the cost reflects that. Small shops doing occasional proposals may not get the ROI. It's also not built specifically for GovCon — the FAR/DFARS compliance matrix workflow isn't native — but teams adapt it effectively with custom templates.

Pros:

  • Excellent RFP content library and reuse features
  • Strong multi-contributor workflow and version control
  • AI assist for auto-populating standard responses
  • Integrates with major CRMs and productivity suites

Cons:

  • Expensive — best value for teams with high RFP volume
  • Not purpose-built for GovCon compliance matrix workflows
  • Learning curve for initial library setup

Qvidian / Upland

Qvidian (now part of the Upland Software family) is an enterprise-grade RFP and proposal automation platform targeting large organizations with complex, repetitive proposal needs. Like Loopio, it's built around a content library model — but at a more enterprise scale with deeper workflow automation, compliance tracking, and content governance features that matter to organizations responding to dozens of RFPs simultaneously.

For most GovCon teams below the enterprise tier, Qvidian is overkill. Pricing is custom and typically enterprise-level, which means dedicated procurement conversations and multi-year commitments. The strength is scale and governance — if you're a large defense contractor with a 20-person proposal center, it's worth evaluating. If you're a $50M GovCon shop trying to win your next IDIQ, Loopio or even a well-structured Microsoft 365 setup will serve you better at a fraction of the cost.

Pros:

  • Enterprise-grade content governance and automation
  • Strong compliance tracking features
  • Scales well for very high RFP volume

Cons:

  • Custom enterprise pricing — expensive and less transparent
  • Not built specifically for GovCon workflows
  • Heavy implementation overhead
  • Overkill for most small-to-mid GovCon teams

Google Workspace / Microsoft 365

Here's the honest truth: most GovCon proposals are still written in Microsoft Word and tracked in Excel. And for small teams, that's not crazy. Google Docs and Microsoft 365 are universally understood, integrate with everything, and cost almost nothing relative to dedicated proposal software. For a team that does 5–10 proposals a year, a disciplined folder structure and well-maintained templates in SharePoint or Google Drive can be perfectly workable.

The problem emerges at scale and complexity. Managing multiple SME contributions, version control, and compliance tracking in Word documents breaks down fast. Comments pile up, version names get confusing, and someone inevitably submits last week's draft. The tools work — but they require significant process discipline to avoid chaos on a competitive federal proposal.

Pros:

  • Everyone already knows how to use them
  • Near-zero marginal cost for proposal writing
  • Flexible — adapt to any proposal structure
  • Microsoft 365 is DoD/FedRAMP compatible

Cons:

  • Version control is entirely manual
  • No native compliance matrix or RFP workflow features
  • Multi-contributor coordination is painful at scale
  • No content library or reuse features

PM & Program Tracking Tools

Microsoft Project

If you're delivering on a DoD contract that requires an Integrated Master Schedule (IMS), Microsoft Project is effectively the industry standard. Contracting officers expect it, program offices are built around it, and most DoD-facing project managers know how to read a Gantt chart in MS Project format. The desktop version is powerful for critical path analysis, resource loading, and EVM baseline tracking.

The learning curve is real, and the collaboration features are dated compared to cloud-native competitors. Project Online (the cloud version) has improved but still feels clunky next to Smartsheet for day-to-day team coordination. For anything requiring formal IMS deliverables on a DoD program, though, MS Project is where you live.

Pros:

  • DoD IMS standard — program offices expect it
  • Robust critical path and resource management
  • EVM integration capabilities
  • Part of Microsoft 365 ecosystem

Cons:

  • Steep learning curve for new users
  • Collaboration features lag behind cloud-native tools
  • Expensive compared to alternatives for simple tracking needs

Smartsheet

Smartsheet hits a sweet spot that a lot of GovCon teams have discovered: it's collaborative, visually intuitive, and works well for task order tracking, milestone dashboards, action item registers, and cross-team coordination — all the work management that happens around a DoD contract but doesn't require the formality of a full IMS. The sheet-based interface is familiar enough that people actually use it, unlike more complex PM tools that get abandoned after the kickoff meeting.

Government customers increasingly accept Smartsheet deliverables for CDRLs and status reporting. It's cloud-native, integrates with Microsoft and Google, and has solid permission controls. For the day-to-day operational layer of running a program — especially task order management under an IDIQ — Smartsheet is hard to beat at its price point.

Pros:

  • Intuitive — high adoption across teams
  • Excellent for task order and milestone tracking
  • Strong collaboration and commenting features
  • Affordable for small-to-mid teams

Cons:

  • Not a substitute for formal MS Project IMS when required
  • Can get unwieldy as sheet complexity grows
  • Limited EVM/critical path analysis natively

Jira

Jira is the right tool if you're delivering software under a DoD contract — particularly in an Agile or DevSecOps context. Sprint planning, backlog management, story tracking, and release velocity are where Jira excels. For software-intensive programs under DODI 5000.87 or Agile software acquisition pathways, Jira fits naturally into how those teams already work.

Outside of software delivery, Jira is a poor fit for defense acquisition work. It's not built for IMS management, CDRL tracking, or the kind of milestone-based program governance most DoD contracts require. Use it where it fits; don't force it into a traditional program management context where MS Project or Smartsheet will serve you better.

Pros:

  • Best-in-class for agile software delivery
  • Strong integration with DevSecOps toolchains
  • Familiar to most software development teams
  • Affordable per-user pricing

Cons:

  • Poor fit for non-software DoD program management
  • Not suited to IMS, EVM, or traditional CDRL tracking
  • Requires customization for GovCon workflows

AI Tools

Perplexity

For GovCon proposal managers, Perplexity is genuinely one of the most useful tools available right now — and it's underused. It's an AI-powered research engine that pulls from current web sources and cites them, which makes it phenomenal for competitive intelligence: researching an incumbent contractor, pulling FPDS data context, understanding a government customer's recent contract history, or quickly getting up to speed on a technology area you're writing about in a proposal.

The ability to ask follow-up questions and get cited, synthesized answers in seconds is a legitimate productivity multiplier for proposal managers who need to quickly understand what's happening in a market, who the competition is, or what a government agency's stated priorities are going into a re-compete. Perplexity Pro ($20/mo) adds more powerful models and more daily searches — for proposal work, it's worth the upgrade.

High-value use case: Ask Perplexity to research a specific agency's recent contract awards in your domain, then use that intel to tailor your understanding of their customer and sharpen your proposal's win themes. Takes five minutes instead of three hours on USASpending.gov.

Pros:

  • Cited, current research — not hallucinated summaries
  • Outstanding for competitive intel and market research
  • Fast — turns multi-hour research into minutes
  • Genuinely useful for GovCon-specific queries

Cons:

  • Not a proposal writing tool — research only
  • Pro tier needed for heavy use

ChatGPT / Claude

ChatGPT (OpenAI) and Claude (Anthropic) are the workhorses for AI-assisted drafting. Proposal managers are using them to generate first drafts of technical approach sections, rewrite dense boilerplate into readable prose, summarize lengthy RFPs, draft executive summaries, and brainstorm discriminators. At $20/month for the Pro tiers, they're accessible to every proposal team.

The limitation is context and accuracy: these models don't know your company's past performance, your specific technical approach, or the nuanced requirements in an RFP unless you feed that material in. And they can hallucinate regulatory details — DFARS clauses, FAR thresholds, compliance requirements — with confident-sounding wrongness. They're best used as drafting assistants that get you to a workable first draft faster, with a human doing the substantive review and regulatory accuracy check.

Pros:

  • Excellent for drafting, summarizing, and rewriting
  • Accessible pricing for every team size
  • Broad general knowledge useful for many proposal tasks

Cons:

  • Can hallucinate regulatory details — requires human review
  • Not DoD-specific — general purpose models
  • No integration with your proposal content library

Our Recommended Stack

Small Team (1–5 people, fewer than 20 proposals/year)

  • Writing: Microsoft 365 (Word + SharePoint) — disciplined templates and version naming
  • Tracking: Smartsheet — easy, collaborative, no heavy setup
  • AI Research: Perplexity Pro — competitive intel and market research
  • AI Drafting: ChatGPT or Claude — first drafts and rewrites
  • Training: Acqlerate — knowledge foundation for everyone on the team
  • Estimated monthly cost: ~$80–$120/month total for a team of 3

Mid-Size Team (6–20 people, 20–50 proposals/year)

  • Writing: Loopio — content library pays off at this volume
  • Tracking: Smartsheet + Microsoft Project (for programs requiring IMS)
  • AI Research: Perplexity Pro — standard across the team
  • AI Drafting: ChatGPT Enterprise or Claude for Teams
  • Training: Acqlerate team seats

Large Team (20+ people, 50+ proposals/year)

  • Writing: Loopio or Qvidian/Upland — evaluate based on volume and governance needs
  • Tracking: Microsoft Project (IMS) + Smartsheet (operational) + Jira (if software programs)
  • AI: Enterprise AI agreements for ChatGPT/Claude + Perplexity Pro at team scale
  • Training: Acqlerate group/team licensing for new hires and upskilling

The one underrated move: Most GovCon teams invest in tools but not in understanding. A proposal manager who doesn't fully understand LPTA vs. BVTO source selection, or why the government evaluator is focused on cost risk in the technical approach, will write a less compelling proposal regardless of what software they're using. Frequently Asked Questions

What's the most important tool for a first-time proposal manager?

Start with what you have — Microsoft 365 — and invest in learning the acquisition process first. Understanding what the government evaluator is looking for, how source selection works, and how to read an RFP's Section L and M requirements will do more for your win rate than any software. That's exactly what Acqlerate's Capture Management module covers.

Is Perplexity actually useful for GovCon work?

Genuinely, yes. The ability to ask specific questions about government agencies, programs, and contractors and get cited answers from current sources makes it one of the most practically useful tools for competitive intelligence and pre-proposal market research. It's not a proposal writing tool — but for research, it's excellent.

Can AI tools write my proposals for me?

They can write drafts — but not winning proposals. AI tools accelerate the drafting process significantly, but a winning federal proposal requires specific knowledge of the customer, the technical requirements, your company's discriminators, and the evaluation criteria. AI drafts those sections faster; a skilled proposal manager makes them compelling and compliant. The combination is powerful — neither alone is sufficient.

Do I need Loopio if we only respond to a few RFPs a year?

Probably not. Loopio's ROI depends on volume and reuse — the content library becomes valuable when you're answering similar questions across many proposals. For low-volume shops, a well-organized SharePoint or Google Drive with strong templates is more practical and dramatically cheaper.
