The DoD Acquisition Lifecycle: From Capability Gap to Fielded System

A plain-English walkthrough of every phase in the Major Capability Acquisition pathway — what happens, what documents you need, how long it takes, and where programs most often go wrong.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 10 min read · For: USG Personnel

If you ask someone outside the defense world how long it takes to develop a new military aircraft, they'll guess maybe three to five years. The real answer, for a major acquisition program, is often fifteen to twenty. The defense acquisition lifecycle isn't just slow — it's deliberate. Every phase exists for a reason. Understanding those reasons — and the decision gates between them — is fundamental to working effectively in this system.

This guide focuses on the Major Capability Acquisition (MCA) pathway, governed by DoDI 5000.85, which is the dominant framework for large-scale hardware programs: ships, aircraft, ground vehicles, missiles. For the full context on how MCA fits within the six AAF pathways, see The Complete Guide to DoD Defense Acquisition.

Before the Lifecycle Starts: JCIDS and the Capability Gap

The acquisition lifecycle doesn't begin in a program office — it begins when a warfighter identifies a capability gap. The Joint Capabilities Integration and Development System (JCIDS) is the process through which the military formally documents that gap and validates requirements.

The key output that initiates acquisition is the Initial Capabilities Document (ICD). The ICD doesn't prescribe a solution — it describes the operational problem. "We can't defeat this threat with current systems." "We have a logistics gap in this environment." The acquisition system takes the ICD and begins figuring out how to solve it.

Why This Matters: Requirements instability is the single biggest cause of cost and schedule growth in defense programs. When requirements change mid-program — after design has begun — it costs exponentially more to accommodate them. Programs that start with poorly-validated ICDs are setting themselves up for trouble at Milestone B and beyond.

The MCA Lifecycle: Five Phases, Three Milestones

The MCA pathway has five sequential phases separated by three milestone decision gates. Here's how the full lifecycle flows:

Phase Name Enters Via Key Activity Primary Document Typical Duration
Pre-Acquisition Materiel Solution Analysis (MSA) Materiel Development Decision (MDD) Analysis of Alternatives (AoA); technology assessment ICD; AoA report; Initial Cost Estimate 1–3 years
Phase A Technology Maturation & Risk Reduction (TMRR) Milestone A Competitive prototyping; requirements refinement; risk reduction Capability Development Document (CDD); Technology Development Strategy 2–5 years
Phase B Engineering & Manufacturing Development (EMD) Milestone B (Program Initiation) Final design; system integration; CDR; LRIP quantity planning Approved CDD; Acquisition Strategy; T&E Master Plan (TEMP) 3–8 years
Phase C Production & Deployment (P&D) Milestone C LRIP; IOT&E; Full-Rate Production decision (FRP) Capability Production Document (CPD); LRIP determination 3–10+ years
Phase D Operations & Support (O&S) Full-Rate Production decision Operational sustainment; modifications; lifecycle management Life Cycle Sustainment Plan (LCSP) 20–50+ years

Phase 0: Materiel Solution Analysis (MSA)

The MSA phase begins with a Materiel Development Decision (MDD) — the formal determination that a materiel solution is appropriate for the capability gap described in the ICD. Not every capability gap requires a new system; the MDD is the gate that separates cases where you need new development from cases where existing systems can close the gap.

What Happens in MSA

The program office's primary task during MSA is the Analysis of Alternatives (AoA). The AoA evaluates multiple approaches to solving the capability gap: existing systems, modified systems, new development, non-materiel solutions. The goal is to select the most promising technical approach before committing significant resources to development.

Simultaneously, the program office begins:

  • Developing an initial life-cycle cost estimate
  • Conducting a technology readiness assessment
  • Drafting a Technology Development Strategy
  • Working with the requirements community to refine the ICD toward a draft CDD

Exit: Milestone A

Milestone A is the decision to enter the TMRR phase. The MDA reviews whether the program has a viable solution identified, adequate cost estimates, an approved ICD, and a technology development strategy. The exit criteria are set by the MDA based on the program's ACAT level.

Phase A: Technology Maturation and Risk Reduction (TMRR)

After Milestone A, the program shifts from analysis to active risk reduction. The name says it all: the goal is to mature the technology and reduce the risk of committing to full development.

What Happens in TMRR

Competitive prototyping is the signature activity of this phase. Congress mandated competitive prototyping for most ACAT I programs (10 U.S.C. § 4251) because multiple competing approaches dramatically reduce technical risk before a single vendor is selected. Program offices evaluate competing designs, conduct cost-performance trade analyses, and select the best approach to carry into EMD.

Other major TMRR activities include:

  • Maturing the Capability Development Document (CDD) — the detailed requirements document that will govern EMD. Must be validated by the user community (JROC for ACAT I).
  • Conducting a Preliminary Design Review (PDR) for some programs
  • Releasing draft Requests for Proposals (RFPs) for EMD contracts
  • Developing the Test and Evaluation Master Plan (TEMP)
  • Establishing the Acquisition Program Baseline (APB) — the official cost, schedule, and performance commitments that will be tracked going forward

Exit: Milestone B — Program Initiation

Milestone B is the most consequential decision gate in the entire lifecycle. It marks formal program initiation — the point at which a program officially enters the Defense Acquisition System and receives the resources needed for full development.

Requirements for Milestone B approval include:

  • Approved CDD (signed by the validation authority)
  • Completed Acquisition Strategy
  • Full FYDP funding (the program must be funded across the Future Years Defense Program)
  • An independent cost estimate (CAPE for ACAT ID programs)
  • A TEMP with approved test approach
  • Competition analysis and LRIP quantity determination

The Milestone B Reality Check: Programs that reach Milestone B without FYDP funding get turned away. This is the most common reason milestone decisions slip — the program is technically ready but the money hasn't come through in the POM. Program managers and their staffs spend enormous effort on PPBE advocacy in parallel with technical development precisely to avoid this problem.

Phase B: Engineering and Manufacturing Development (EMD)

EMD is where the hardware gets built. After Milestone B, a prime contractor is typically selected via competitive source selection, and the program transitions from prototyping to building the final production-representative design.

What Happens in EMD

EMD has two major sub-phases:

  • Integrated System Design — System architecture finalized, preliminary designs reviewed, risk areas addressed.
  • System Capability and Manufacturing Process Demonstration — Manufacturing processes proven, final design validated through a Critical Design Review (CDR), developmental testing conducted.

The Critical Design Review (CDR) is the major technical milestone within EMD. CDR confirms that the design is complete and stable enough to proceed to production. A failed CDR is a serious event — it typically means more development work and schedule delay before Milestone C.

During EMD, the requirements document evolves from the CDD to the Capability Production Document (CPD), which specifies exactly what the production system will deliver. The CPD must be approved before Milestone C.

Exit: Milestone C

Milestone C approves entry into Low-Rate Initial Production (LRIP) — the first actual production units. Requirements for Milestone C include:

  • Approved CPD
  • Successful developmental testing and a positive IOT&E plan
  • LRIP determination (quantities approved)
  • Updated independent cost estimate
  • Demonstrated manufacturing capability

Phase C: Production and Deployment (P&D)

P&D is where the program transitions from development to delivering fielded units to operational units.

Low-Rate Initial Production (LRIP)

LRIP allows production of a limited quantity — typically no more than 10% of the total production buy — before the system is formally cleared for Full-Rate Production. LRIP units are used for Initial Operational Test and Evaluation (IOT&E), conducted by the independent Director of Operational Test and Evaluation (DOT&E) for ACAT ID programs.

IOT&E evaluates the system in realistic operational conditions with actual users. This is distinct from developmental testing (DT), which is conducted by the program office and focuses on whether the system meets specifications. IOT&E asks: does this system actually work for warfighters in the field?

Full-Rate Production Decision (FRP/LRIP)

The Full-Rate Production decision is the final major milestone — the MDA's approval to produce at the full planned rate. FRP requires a successful IOT&E assessment and all other statutory requirements. At this point, the program typically has firm production contracts in place and is delivering units to operational units regularly.

Phase D: Operations and Support (O&S)

O&S is the longest and most expensive phase of any major system's life. The F-15 has been in service since 1976. The B-52 has been flying since 1952. O&S typically accounts for 60–80% of a system's total lifecycle cost — a fact that's often underappreciated during development when everyone's focused on getting to IOC.

Key O&S Activities

  • Sustainment — Maintenance, spare parts, depot support, contractor logistics support (CLS)
  • Modifications and upgrades — Capability improvements via Engineering Change Proposals (ECPs) and product improvements
  • Life-cycle cost tracking — Against the Life Cycle Sustainment Plan (LCSP)
  • Disposal planning — Demilitarization and disposal when the system is retired

The O&S Cost Trap: Programs routinely underestimate O&S costs during development. CAPE's independent cost estimates consistently find that program offices low-ball sustainment. For contractors, O&S is where long-term revenue lives — sustainment contracts, upgrades, and tech refreshes often exceed initial development contract value over a system's lifetime.

Common Failure Points in the Acquisition Lifecycle

DoD's own reports and program histories point to recurring reasons programs fail to deliver on time and on budget:

1. Requirements Creep

The most common culprit. Requirements that expand after Milestone B — without corresponding cost and schedule adjustments — almost always lead to overruns. The Warfighter always wants more capability; the PM's job is to manage that reality against an approved baseline.

2. Optimism Bias in Cost Estimates

Program offices have structural incentives to produce low cost estimates to win funding. CAPE exists precisely to counterbalance this. Programs that enter EMD with unrealistic baselines hit Nunn-McCurdy breach territory when reality catches up.

3. Concurrency: Buying Before Testing

Congressional pressure to show production progress sometimes pushes programs to buy LRIP units before testing is complete. When tests reveal problems, LRIP units must be retrofitted — at enormous cost. The F-35 is the canonical example, with over 800 LRIP aircraft delivered before many developmental test findings were resolved.

4. Technology Immaturity at Milestone B

Programs that enter EMD with immature technologies — below Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 6, as a rough benchmark — face significant technical risk. The TMRR phase exists to address this, but programs sometimes rush through it.

5. Funding Instability

Programs that get restructured or had their PPBE funding cut mid-stream lose momentum, drive up unit costs (fewer units spread over fixed overhead), and delay fielding. This is a systemic problem that no individual program manager can fully control.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Milestone B in defense acquisition?

Milestone B is program initiation — the most important decision gate in the MCA pathway. It's where the Milestone Decision Authority officially authorizes a program to enter Engineering and Manufacturing Development (EMD), the full-scale development phase. At Milestone B, the program must have an approved Capability Development Document (CDD), an Acquisition Strategy, full FYDP funding, and an independent cost estimate. Without FYDP funding, programs cannot receive Milestone B approval regardless of technical readiness.

What is the difference between IOT&E and developmental testing?

Developmental testing (DT) is conducted by the program office and tests whether the system meets its technical specifications — it's performed in controlled conditions, often by the contractor. Initial Operational Test and Evaluation (IOT&E) is conducted by an independent organization (DOT&E for ACAT ID programs) using actual military users in realistic operational conditions. IOT&E answers the operational question: can warfighters use this system effectively in the field? Full-Rate Production cannot be approved without a completed IOT&E.

How long does the MCA acquisition lifecycle typically take?

A full MCA lifecycle from MDD to Full-Rate Production typically takes 10–20 years for major weapons systems. The TMRR phase alone can take 2–5 years. EMD, depending on complexity, is typically 3–8 years. Congressional mandates and PPBE cycles can extend any phase significantly. Operations and Support then runs for another 20–50 years. The total lifecycle cost — including O&S — often exceeds initial development costs by a factor of three to five.

What is the Analysis of Alternatives (AoA)?

The Analysis of Alternatives (AoA) is a formal evaluation conducted during the Materiel Solution Analysis phase that assesses multiple technical approaches to solving a capability gap. It analyzes the operational effectiveness, suitability, risk, and cost of each alternative — including the option of doing nothing (the baseline). The AoA is a key Milestone A requirement for ACAT I programs and is sponsored by the requirements community (JROC for joint programs), not the program office, to preserve independence.

What happens if a program misses its Acquisition Program Baseline?

If a program's costs exceed APB thresholds, it triggers reporting requirements. For ACAT I programs, unit cost growth of 15% or more above the current APB constitutes a "significant" Nunn-McCurdy breach and requires notification to Congress. Growth of 25% or more is a "critical" breach and requires the Secretary of Defense to certify the program or cancel it. Below ACAT I, APB breaches trigger internal DoD reporting and may require a program restructure, but there is no mandatory congressional notification.
