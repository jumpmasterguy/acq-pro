Why the Government Loses Protests & What You Must Do Now

Sustained bid protests derail critical programs and waste taxpayer dollars; understanding their causes is crucial for all acquisition professionals.

By Acqlerate · July 2026 · 6 min read · For: USG & Contractor

The Persistent Shadow of Sustained Protests

As the Department of Defense (DoD) navigates the complex terrain of modernization and strategic competition in 2025 and 2026, the specter of sustained Government Accountability Office (GAO) bid protests continues to loom large. These aren't mere administrative hurdles; a sustained protest signifies a fundamental flaw in an agency's procurement decision, demanding significant corrective action. The "so what" here is profound: sustained protests don't just delay contracts; they delay critical warfighting capabilities, inflate costs, and erode trust in the acquisition process. For both government and industry, understanding why these protests succeed is the first step toward preventing them.

The Core Problem: Why Agencies Trip Up

The GAO's annual reports consistently highlight a few key reasons for sustaining protests, and these issues are highly likely to persist into 2025-2026. These aren't new, esoteric legal arguments; they are fundamental breakdowns in the procurement process that acquisition teams frequently encounter:

  • Unreasonable Technical Evaluation: Agencies often fail to properly assess proposals according to their own stated criteria. This can manifest as arbitrary downgrades, inconsistent application of evaluation standards, or a failure to recognize innovative approaches that meet requirements.
  • Inadequate Documentation of the Source Selection Decision: This is arguably the most common pitfall. If the agency's record doesn't clearly, comprehensively, and contemporaneously explain *why* certain decisions were made – why one offeror was chosen over another, or why a proposal received a particular rating – the GAO will likely find it indefensible.
  • Flawed Cost or Price Realism Analysis: Especially in complex services or development contracts, agencies sometimes accept unrealistically low proposed costs without sufficiently scrutinizing them. If an awardee's price seems too good to be true, and the agency can't justify its realism analysis, it’s a prime protest target.
  • Unequal Discussions or Solicitation Improprieties: Fair and equal treatment is paramount. Agencies run into trouble when they don't treat all offerors equitably during negotiations, provide misleading information, or issue ambiguous or flawed solicitations that disadvantage certain bidders.

The "so what" for agencies is clear: these are not minor procedural quibbles. They are systemic vulnerabilities that, when exposed, force significant rework and delay. For contractors, these are the battlegrounds where protests are most likely to succeed.

These cases aren't isolated incidents. While the formal sustainment rate hovers around 13-15%, over half of all protests lead to voluntary corrective action by agencies, effectively acknowledging an error before a formal GAO decision. The cost of these errors is staggering:

  • Program Delays: Sustained protests typically delay awards by 6-12 months, sometimes longer, translating to billions in delayed capability delivery.
  • Direct Rework Costs: Agencies can spend millions in re-evaluating proposals, conducting new discussions, rewriting source selection documents, and legal fees. For major programs, this easily reaches tens of millions.
  • Contractor Costs: Litigating a complex protest can cost an offeror anywhere from $250,000 to over $1 million.

The "so what" here is that these aren't just legal battles; they are expensive, time-consuming setbacks that directly impact our national security posture and taxpayer dollars.

What Agencies Must Do (and Why)

For DoD Program Managers and acquisition professionals, the path to avoiding sustained protests is clear, albeit demanding. It boils down to diligence, documentation, and proactive risk management:

  • Rigor in Documentation is Paramount: If it's not documented, it didn't happen in the eyes of the GAO. Maintain a clear, comprehensive, and contemporaneous record of *every* decision, rationale, and discussion. This is your agency's best defense.
  • Master Evaluation Methodologies: Ensure evaluation criteria are unambiguous and consistently applied. Train evaluators thoroughly on the solicitation's requirements and the perils of undocumented "gut feelings."
  • Proactive Legal Engagement: Involve legal counsel early and often, especially for high-value or complex procurements. They can identify and mitigate protest risks before they escalate.
  • Robust Debriefings: Provide thorough and meaningful debriefings to unsuccessful offerors. A clear understanding of the agency's decision can sometimes prevent a protest or narrow its scope.
  • Risk Mitigation Planning: Integrate protest risk into your program schedules and budgets. Have contingency plans for potential delays or corrective actions.

The GAO's Golden Rule: If it's not documented, it didn't happen. This principle is at the heart of nearly every sustained protest. Robust, contemporaneous documentation is your agency's best defense against challenges.

The "so what" for government teams is that these actions aren't optional best practices; they are essential safeguards against mission-critical delays and wasteful expenditure. Getting it right upfront saves exponentially more time and money down the line.

What Contractors Must Do (and Why)

For defense contractors, the bid protest mechanism is a vital tool for ensuring fair competition. However, it requires strategic thinking and careful execution:

  • Strategic Protest Decision-Making: Evaluate the merits of a potential protest carefully. A sustained protest is powerful, but frivolous filings damage relationships and consume significant resources. Focus on clear, demonstrable agency errors that prejudiced your offer.
  • Thorough Proposal Preparation: Submit comprehensive, well-documented, and compliant proposals. The stronger your initial proposal, the clearer any agency evaluation errors will appear if you need to protest.
  • Leverage Pre-Award Debriefings: Use the debriefing process to gain a clear understanding of the agency's evaluation. This information is critical for determining whether a protest has merit and for focusing your arguments.
  • Understand Protest Grounds: Familiarize yourself with common protest grounds—evaluation errors, flawed solicitations, unequal treatment, cost realism issues. This knowledge helps you identify potential agency missteps when reviewing your debriefing.
  • Focus on Specificity: When filing a protest, be specific about the alleged agency errors and how they prejudiced your offer. General complaints rarely succeed; a detailed, fact-based argument is key.

The "so what" for contractors is that strategic, well-founded protests are a legitimate and necessary part of a healthy acquisition ecosystem. They hold agencies accountable and ensure a level playing field, ultimately benefiting both industry and the warfighter by promoting better, fairer procurements.

Conclusion: A Shared Responsibility

The challenges of defense acquisition in 2025-2026 are immense, and the oversight provided by the GAO's bid protest function remains a critical check and balance. Sustained protests represent significant failures in the acquisition process, costing time, money, and potentially delaying vital capabilities. For defense PMs and acquisition professionals, the message is unequivocal: rigorous planning, meticulous documentation, and consistent application of procurement law are non-negotiable. For contractors, understanding the rules and strategically leveraging the protest mechanism is essential for ensuring fair competition and protecting their interests. Ultimately, improving acquisition outcomes and minimizing sustained protests is a shared responsibility, crucial for delivering on the nation's defense priorities.
