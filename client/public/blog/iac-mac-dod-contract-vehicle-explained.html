IAC MACs: The DoD Contract Vehicle Every Defense Contractor PM Should Know

IAC MACs give the government fast access to elite technical expertise, without running a full acquisition every time. Here's what they are, how they work, and why being on one matters for your career.

By Acqlerate · June 2026 · 7 min read · For: Contractor PM

If you've spent any time in defense contracting, you know the government loves acronyms. It's basically a second language. So when someone mentions an IAC MAC, you might be tempted to smile and nod like you know exactly what that is.

Don't. This one actually matters, especially if you're trying to grow into DoD technical work as a Program or Project Manager.

Let's break it down from the beginning.

What Is a MAC?

A MAC stands for Multiple Award Contract (yes, another acronym. Stick with us.) It's a contract vehicle where the government awards contracts to multiple companies at the same time, all under the same scope. Instead of running a full, competitive acquisition every time it needs something, the government pre-selects a pool of qualified vendors and then holds competitions within that pool for individual task orders.

Think of it like an approved vendor list, except the government competed the list itself, so the individual buys off of it are faster, cheaper, and legally cleaner.

MACs fall under the broader IDIQ (Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity) contract structure. IDIQ means the government doesn't commit to a specific quantity of work upfront. It sets a ceiling and orders what it needs over time. MACs are a specific flavor of IDIQ where multiple contractors are on the vehicle at once.

You've probably heard of other MACs: GSA's OASIS+ and Alliant 2, or the Navy's SeaPort-NxG. Those are large, widely used vehicles. IAC MACs are a more specialized breed.

What Is an IAC?

Now we need to layer in the second acronym. (Acronym sandwich incoming.)

An IAC is an Information Analysis Center, a DoD-sponsored research organization with deep technical expertise in specific domains. IACs have been part of the defense infrastructure since 1946. They exist to collect, analyze, and disseminate Scientific and Technical Information (STI) on behalf of DoD and the broader national security community.

The DoD IAC program is managed by the Defense Technical Information Center (DTIC), which is the DoD's central repository for scientific and technical information. DTIC is the librarian of the defense technology world. It stores decades of research, technical reports, and data that the DoD has funded.

The IAC program currently operates through three Basic Centers of Operation (BCOs), each covering a primary technical domain:

  • CSIAC: Cybersecurity and Information Systems
  • DSIAC: Defense Systems (weapons, autonomous systems, materials, energetics)
  • HDIAC: Homeland Defense and Security (CBRN, biometrics, critical infrastructure)

These centers don't just hold information. They employ technical experts, staff research tasks, and connect government customers to the right knowledge fast. They're not staffing agencies. They're research shops with actual domain depth.

What Is an IAC MAC?

Here's where it clicks together.

An IAC MAC is a pre-competed contract vehicle that gives the government rapid access to the technical expertise of the IAC network, without having to run a full acquisition from scratch every time.

The current IAC MAC is a $48 billion, multiple-award IDIQ contract overseen by DTIC. It covers 22 Technical Focus Areas (TFAs) across the three IAC domains. The vehicle has three pools, and the current pools run through 29 September 2027 (extensions could stretch them into 2028), so watch for the follow-on competition:

  • Pool 1 (Full and Open): For task orders over $15M
  • Pool 2 (Small Business Set-Aside): For task orders under $15M
  • Pool 3 (CBRN Laboratory): For work requiring certified CBRN facilities

When a DoD component needs research support in one of those 22 areas (say, autonomous systems or cybersecurity), it can go to the IAC MAC instead of writing a new solicitation from scratch. DTIC reports an average of 5.5 months from solicitation to task order award in FY24. That's quick for DoD.

The IAC MAC isn't just fast. DTIC advertises a "Preferred Use" letter from the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for the vehicle. Translation: if a requirement fits the IAC MAC, program offices are encouraged to look at it first.

The IAC MAC and Other Ways Into the IACs

The IAC MAC is the big vehicle, but it isn't the only way to buy IAC work. DTIC lists a ladder of options, from free answers to five-year task orders. (Fewer acronyms this time. You're welcome.)

The DoD IAC MAC: The main event. A $48B IDIQ with 22 technical focus areas and three pools holding 28 contracts (15 in Pool 1, 10 in Pool 2, 3 in Pool 3). Task orders have no set minimum or maximum value and can run up to five years. This is the one most defense contractors mean when they say "IAC MAC."

Technical Inquiries: Free answers from IAC experts for questions that take four hours or less. Extended inquiries (5 to 160 hours of research, up to $50,000) are customer-funded.

IAC Task Orders: Smaller, customer-funded tasks through the three centers, capped at $1 million and 12 months. Useful when the work is real but doesn't justify a full IAC MAC task order.

What Makes an IAC MAC Different from a Standard MAC or IDIQ

Most IDIQs and MACs are designed to buy services or products. You go on the vehicle, you compete for work, you deliver what the PWS says, you get paid. Clean, transactional, repeatable.

IAC MACs operate differently in three important ways.

The work is research and technical, not commodity services. When the government puts a task order on the IAC MAC, it's typically because it needs something that doesn't exist yet: an analysis, a technical assessment, a research study, a prototype evaluation. This is not staff augmentation. This is RDT&E (Research, Development, Test, and Evaluation) and R&D-related analytical services. The contract scope covers 22 technical focus areas precisely because these requirements are complex and domain-specific.

Access is restricted. You can't just show up on the IAC MAC. You have to compete to get on the vehicle, demonstrate relevant technical capability across the focus areas, and get vetted before you ever see a task order. The pool of qualified contractors is intentionally limited. That's the point. DTIC's award list shows just 28 contracts across the three pools. That's a small number compared to the size of the defense industrial base.

The government treats it as a preferred pathway. DTIC points customers to a Preferred Use letter from the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense. When a program office has a cybersecurity analysis requirement or a weapons systems study, it's encouraged to look at the IAC MAC before spinning up a standalone procurement.

The Deliverables: STIs and the DTIC Archive

Here's something most contractor PMs don't fully appreciate about IAC work: the deliverables aren't just reports that go into a drawer and disappear.

The primary output of IAC MAC task orders is STI (Scientific and Technical Information). This includes technical reports, research findings, data packages, software documentation, engineering analyses, and related work products. These are formal, documented outputs produced according to specific government standards.

Under DFARS 252.235-7011, the clause DoD uses in research and development contracts, contractors submit an electronic copy of the approved final scientific or technical report to DTIC. Those reports get cataloged, marked for distribution, and archived in DTIC's collection, which authorized DoD users, researchers, and acquisition professionals search through DTIC Discover.

(So the government keeps your work forever. No pressure.)

This matters for two reasons. First, your work has a longer shelf life than a typical service contract. It becomes part of the institutional knowledge base DoD draws on for future programs. Second, it means the quality and documentation standards are higher. You're not just delivering a service. You're producing knowledge that will be referenced for years.

Why This Matters for You as a Contractor PM

If your goal is to grow into DoD technical work, and specifically into program management roles on research-heavy contracts, IAC MACs are a vehicle worth targeting.

Here's the practical case:

Less competition per task order. With 28 contracts spread across three pools, you're not competing against the entire defense industrial base. You're competing within a vetted pool. Task orders go to those primes first. The pool is competitive, but it's not a 200-bidder open market.

Higher-value work. IAC MAC work spans cybersecurity, autonomous systems, advanced materials, directed energy, CBRN defense, modeling and simulation, and more. This is substantive, technically challenging work, the kind that builds your resume and your company's past performance record for larger programs.

Direct access to DoD technical communities. Working on IAC task orders puts your team in direct contact with DoD S&T and acquisition offices. These are the relationships that generate future requirements, grow your understanding of where DoD is investing, and position you for the next contract.

Preferred use means recurring demand. With a preferred-use letter behind it and a $48 billion ceiling, the IAC MAC is built for repeat business. It's not a one-time contract. It's an ordering platform with institutional backing.

Getting on the IAC MAC, and performing well on it, is one of the most effective paths into DoD research and technical work for a contractor PM who wants to move up.

The Bottom Line

The IAC MAC is not a general-purpose contract vehicle. It's not where you go to staff a help desk or deliver IT hardware. It exists specifically to give DoD fast, competitive access to the best technical minds in defense research, and it's the government's own preferred way to buy that kind of work.

As a contractor PM trying to build a DoD technical portfolio, understanding how the IAC MAC works (what it covers, how task orders flow, what you deliver, and how to get on it) is foundational knowledge. This is the kind of vehicle that defines career trajectories in GovCon.
