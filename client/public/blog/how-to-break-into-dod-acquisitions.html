How to Break Into DoD Acquisitions: A Practical Career Guide

Defense acquisitions is a well-paid, important career field, and one of the least well-understood by people entering it. Here's the practical path in, from both the government and contractor sides.

By Acqlerate · January 2026 · 12 min read · For: Career Changers & New Entrants

Why Defense Acquisitions Is Worth Pursuing

Before the how, a word on the why. Defense acquisitions is not glamorous in the conventional sense. You won't be on the front page of TechCrunch. But here's what you will have:

  • Stability, with an asterisk. The demand is huge: Congress appropriated $839.2 billion for DoD in FY2026, and someone has to buy and manage everything that money pays for. But 2025 proved no job is untouchable. A federal hiring freeze and deferred resignation offers cut about 78,000 DoD civilians that year (GAO), and contractors felt the budget swings too.
  • Compensation that surprises most outsiders. Government acquisition careers can reach GS-15, which in 2026 pays up to $197,200, the legal cap for the General Schedule. On the contractor side, ClearanceJobs' 2026 survey put average total pay for cleared professionals at about $126,000. Senior contractor pay varies widely by company and role.
  • Work that actually matters. The capabilities that the acquisition community delivers (or fails to deliver) directly affect the readiness of the U.S. military. Few career fields have that direct a line to national security outcomes.
  • A professional community that rewards institutional knowledge. This field has a steep learning curve and specialized vocabulary that creates a real moat for professionals who invest in understanding it deeply. That knowledge compounds.

The Two Paths: Government vs. Contractor

Defense acquisitions is not one career. It's two parallel ecosystems with different entry points, compensation structures, and career trajectories. Knowing which path you're targeting from the start dramatically changes what you should do next.

Breaking In: The Government Track

Step 1: Understand the workforce series

The government acquisition workforce is organized by occupational series on USAJOBS. The most important ones for new entrants:

  • Series 1102: Contracting Specialist / KO. The core acquisition series. DoD generally requires a bachelor's degree in any major (10 U.S.C. 1724) and DAWIA certification for full career progression. This is the path to becoming a warranted Contracting Officer.
  • Series 0340 / 1101: Program Management. DoD program management jobs are posted under several series, including 0340 (Program Management) and 1101 (General Business and Industry), and engineers in program offices often sit in engineering series. Search by job title as well as series number.
  • Series 0501: Financial Administration. Budget analysts, cost estimators, and financial managers supporting acquisition programs.

Step 2: Target entry programs

The DoD has several structured entry programs that are often the fastest path in (WarU keeps a list of them):

  • Pathways Recent Graduates Program: for candidates within 2 years of finishing a degree (up to 6 years for veterans delayed by military service). A 1-year program with training and mentorship, after which you may be converted to a permanent job.
  • Air Force PALACE Acquire (PAQ): a two- to three-year program that develops recent graduates in engineering, program management, cyber/IT, financial management and logistics.
  • Air Force Copper Cap: the contracting counterpart to PAQ, a two- to three-year program that develops contract specialists.
  • Other service and agency programs: examples include the Naval Acquisition Development Program, the Army Fellows Program and DLA's Pathways to Career Excellence. Search "[Agency Name] acquisition intern" on USAJOBS.

2025–2026 reality check: A federal hiring freeze ran from 20 January to 15 October 2025, and Executive Order 14356 now requires each agency's strategic hiring committee to approve new hires. These programs still exist, but openings are fewer and slower. Apply broadly and keep the contractor track open as a parallel path.

Step 3: Get your clearance moving

Many DoD positions require at least a Secret clearance. For industry cases, DCSA data showed the fastest 90% of Secret clearances taking about 156 days (roughly five months) in early FY2026, and some take longer. Apply for positions that sponsor clearances from day one. The faster you start the process, the sooner you're eligible for the full range of programs and assignments.

Step 4: Start at WarU (formerly DAU)

DAU (now Warfighting Acquisition University, or WarU) is free for DoD employees and provides the training behind DAWIA certification, which DoD grants only to its own employees in acquisition-coded positions. Start with foundational courses. ACQ 1010 (Fundamentals of Systems Acquisition Management), the successor to the old ACQ 101, is a standard first course. The faster you finish your required training, the broader your assignment eligibility becomes.

Pro tip for USG track: Don't wait for your agency to assign you courses. Log in to WarU (waru.edu, formerly DAU.edu) and self-enroll in foundational courses the first week. It signals initiative and accelerates your certification timeline.

Breaking In: The Contractor Track

Step 1: Understand where the jobs actually are

Defense contracting jobs cluster in a few metro areas, such as Northern Virginia / DC Metro, Huntsville AL, San Antonio TX, Colorado Springs CO, Tampa FL, and San Diego CA. If you're not in or willing to move to a defense hub, remote options exist but are more limited for entry-level roles.

Large services firms that hire entry-level acquisition support staff include Booz Allen Hamilton, Leidos, SAIC, Peraton, Accenture Federal, General Dynamics IT, ManTech, CACI, Parsons, and Amentum. Plus dozens of mid-size firms in each geography.

Step 2: Target the right roles

Entry-level contractor acquisition roles go by several titles. Look for all of these:

  • Program Analyst / Program Support Specialist: supports a PM or program office on a cost-reimbursable support contract. The most common entry point.
  • Capture Analyst / BD Analyst: supports business development and capture activities. Good path for people with analytical skills and comfort with ambiguity.
  • Contract Specialist / Contract Administrator: supports contracts management function. Close to the CO and contracting process.
  • Cost Analyst / Financial Analyst: supports program finance, EVM, budget management. Strong path for quantitatively oriented candidates.
  • Proposal Writer / Proposal Coordinator: entry point into the BD/capture pipeline. Strong writing skills are the core requirement.

Step 3: Build the vocabulary first

The single fastest way to accelerate entry into defense contracting is to be fluent in the language before your first interview. Hiring managers at defense firms are evaluating whether you'll need months of hand-holding on terminology or whether you can contribute immediately. Knowing what an IDIQ is, why the PPBE cycle matters, what a COR does, and how a source selection works will set you apart from candidates who show up unprepared.

Step 4: Get cleared or find a sponsor

A security clearance is one of the most valuable professional assets in the defense contracting market, often worth more than any single certification. Entry-level roles that sponsor clearances exist but are competitive. If you can't find a clearance sponsor immediately, look for roles at cleared facilities where you're working on unclassified programs. The informal exposure to the environment is valuable while you wait for clearance eligibility.

The Skills That Actually Get You Hired

Whether you're targeting the government or contractor side, the professionals who advance fastest share a common skillset that goes beyond technical knowledge:

  • Acquisition vocabulary fluency. You need to speak the language: FAR, DFARS, PPBE, EVM, IDIQ, source selection, CPARS. Acquisition professionals form immediate impressions of competence based on vocabulary. Misusing a term is a signal. Using it correctly and in context is a signal in the other direction.
  • Ability to operate in bureaucratic complexity. Defense acquisition involves layers of oversight, multiple stakeholders with competing equities, and processes that move slowly. The professionals who thrive are those who understand how to navigate the system, not fight it, while still driving results.
  • Document quality. Acquisition is a document-intensive field. Briefings, acquisition strategies, source selection documents, proposals: the ability to produce clear, precise, well-structured written work pays off at every level.
  • Analytical rigor. Whether you're in finance (EVM, cost estimating), contracting (price analysis), program management (schedule analysis, risk management), or BD (pWin assessment, competitive analysis), quantitative reasoning is a constant requirement.

Your First 90 Days

Once you land your first acquisition role (government or contractor), the single best investment you can make is building foundational knowledge deliberately and quickly. The people who advance in this field are the ones who understand the whole system, not just their lane. A contracting officer who understands program management makes better decisions. A contractor PM who understands appropriations law never gets caught flat-footed in a budget conversation. That cross-functional literacy is what Acqlerate is designed to build.
