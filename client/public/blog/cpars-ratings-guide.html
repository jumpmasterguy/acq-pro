CPARS Ratings: Why Your Past Performance Score Is Your Most Valuable Asset

An Exceptional CPARS rating can win you a contract even if your price is higher. An Unsatisfactory one can disqualify you before evaluators read your technical volume.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 8 min read · For: Contractor

TL;DR. CPARS is the government's report card on your contract work, and source selection teams read it before scoring you. Ratings stay in play for three years after the contract's performance ends (six for construction and architect-engineer work).

  • New to this? A government rep who watches your work, often the COR, drafts the rating. The Assessing Official, often the Contracting Officer or PM, signs it.
  • Contractor PM? Manage toward it from day one: flag problems early and brief your COR with data.
  • Got a bad rating? You have 14 calendar days to comment before it becomes visible to source selection teams. An unanswered Unsatisfactory is like a one-star review with no reply from the owner: readers assume you agree.
  • No federal history yet? You get a Neutral rating, not a negative one.

Past performance must be evaluated in every competitive source selection unless the contracting officer documents why it isn't appropriate (FAR 15.104 in the overhauled FAR text). That makes your track record a scored factor in almost every competition you enter. These ratings matter enormously, and contractors know it.

CPARS (the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System) is the federal government's official database of contractor performance records. Every evaluation your government customer writes about your work lives in CPARS. Every source selection team at every federal agency can read it before deciding whether to put your company on the award.

Your CPARS record is not just a compliance artifact. It is a competitive asset, or a competitive liability. This guide explains what it is, how it works, and how to manage it strategically.

What Is CPARS?

CPARS is the government-wide, web-based system through which federal agencies assess and record contractor performance on contracts above reporting thresholds. It used to feed a separate read-only system, PPIRS (the Past Performance Information Retrieval System). PPIRS was merged into CPARS in 2019 (FAR rule), so source selection officials now read your evaluations in CPARS itself.

Under FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) Subpart 42.15 (renumbered 42.11 in the FAR overhaul text), agencies are required to prepare past performance evaluations:

  • At least annually during contract performance (interim evaluations)
  • When the work under the contract or order is completed (final evaluation)

DoD's CPARS guidance gives the government 120 calendar days after the end of each period of performance to finish the evaluation (CPARS guidance).

The FAR requires evaluations for contracts and orders above the simplified acquisition threshold (now $350,000), for construction contracts of $900,000 or more, and for architect-engineer contracts of $45,000 or more, plus any construction or A-E contract terminated for default. DoD's CPARS guidance lists its own reporting thresholds by business sector, for example more than $1 million for services and IT and more than $5 million for systems and operations support. Agencies may still evaluate smaller contracts when they choose to.

Source selection officials can use CPARS evaluations for three years after performance on the contract is complete (six years for construction and architect-engineer contracts), per FAR 42.1503(g). During that window, any federal agency's source selection team can read your full rating narrative, not just the adjectival label, but the written explanation behind it.

How CPARS Ratings Work: The Five Levels

Rating FAR Definition (paraphrased from Table 42-1) Competitive Effect Contractor Action Required
Exceptional Performance meets contractual requirements and exceeds many to the Government's benefit. Accomplished with few minor problems for which contractor corrective actions were highly effective. Multiple significant benefit events (or one of great magnitude). No significant weaknesses. Highest competitive advantage, and it can help justify a higher price under Best Value Tradeoff. Hard to earn: the FAR expects multiple significant benefit events (or one very large one). Position proactively throughout contract. Document every above-and-beyond action. Brief the COR/CO on achievements before evaluation period closes. Review the draft rating and provide substantive comments if appropriate.
Very Good Performance meets contractual requirements and exceeds some to the Government's benefit. Accomplished with some minor problems for which corrective actions were effective. At least one significant benefit event. No significant weaknesses. Strong competitive asset. A realistic target for consistently strong performers. Keeps you competitive in most source selections. Identify at least one major achievement to cite with the evaluator. Address all minor issues promptly. If you believe an Exceptional rating is warranted, document the evidence and discuss with the COR before the evaluation period.
Satisfactory Performance meets contractual requirements. Contains some minor problems for which corrective actions appear or were satisfactory. No significant weaknesses. Contractors cannot be rated below Satisfactory solely for not exceeding requirements. Neutral: maintains eligibility but provides no competitive advantage. In a competitive source selection, a field of Satisfactory ratings is undifferentiated on past performance. Understand why you're not Very Good. Were corrective actions visible to the COR? Did you document proactive problem resolution? A Satisfactory is a signal to improve your performance management and COR communication strategy.
Marginal Performance does not meet some contractual requirements. A serious problem for which the contractor has not yet identified corrective actions, or whose proposed actions appear only marginally effective or were not fully implemented. Supported by a significant event the contractor had trouble overcoming and the notice the government sent about it. Significant competitive disadvantage. Source selection officials will note this and weight it against you in best value tradeoffs. Expect evaluators to ask what you did about it. Respond formally in writing within 14 calendar days (your right under FAR 42.1503(d)). Document your corrective actions with specificity. If the rating is inaccurate, say you don't concur so it gets reviewed above the contracting officer. This rating can and should be challenged if not warranted.
Unsatisfactory Performance does not meet most contractual requirements and recovery is not likely in a timely manner. Serious problems for which the contractor's corrective actions appear or were ineffective. Multiple significant events the contractor had trouble overcoming (or one of very serious magnitude), with impact to the Government. A serious red flag. Evaluators look at your whole record, but a recent, relevant Unsatisfactory can drag down your confidence rating even if your other ratings are strong. It signals high performance risk. Dispute immediately and aggressively. An Unsatisfactory with no contractor comment looks much worse than one with a detailed, documented rebuttal. Engage legal counsel if necessary. A single uncontested Unsatisfactory can damage your pipeline for years.

Who Writes Your CPARS Rating

Understanding who evaluates you helps you manage the relationship that determines your score.

The Assessing Official Representative (AOR) enters the ratings and narrative in CPARS. On many contracts that's the Contracting Officer's Representative (COR), the government employee who monitors day-to-day contract performance. They're the person you're working with most closely throughout the contract. Their technical expertise and daily observations form the basis of your performance narrative.

The Assessing Official (AO) reviews, signs, and releases the evaluation to you. Under DoD's CPARS guidance, the AO may be the contracting officer, a contract specialist, the program manager, or an equivalent official. If you don't concur with the evaluation, a Reviewing Official one level above the AO must add comments and sign it (CPARS guidance).

In some cases (particularly on larger programs or complex technical contracts), additional program office staff (Program Managers, technical representatives) contribute to the evaluation. The key is that someone who has actually observed your performance should be writing the rating. A CO who has no direct knowledge of your work should be relying on the COR's informed assessment, not writing a generic evaluation.

The relationship is everything: CORs are people doing a demanding job. Contractors who communicate proactively, deliver on commitments, and make the COR's life easier get better CPARS ratings, not because the system is subjective, but because the documented record of their behavior reflects better performance. Be the contractor your COR wants to write a glowing evaluation for. That means regular status updates, early flagging of problems, and visible corrective actions.

How CPARS Ratings Affect Future Competitions

Source selection officials reviewing your proposal will pull your CPARS record before scoring your Past Performance factor. Here's what they're looking for and how it affects your competitive position:

Relevance Matters as Much as Quality

Source selection boards evaluate past performance on two dimensions: quality (the rating) and relevance (how closely the prior contract matches the current requirement in scope, complexity, dollar value, and technical domain). An Exceptional rating on a $500K janitorial contract doesn't help you much on a $50M software development program. You need to build performance records that are not just high-quality but directly analogous to the work you're pursuing.

Exceptional and Very Good Ratings Create Best Value Arguments

In a Best Value Tradeoff (BVTO) acquisition, a source selection authority can award to a higher-priced offeror if the technical or past performance advantages justify the premium. A contractor with Exceptional CPARS ratings can legitimately charge more and still win, because the past performance record reduces the government's performance risk, which has real dollar value. This is one of the most powerful competitive levers available in GovCon.

Marginal or Unsatisfactory Ratings Stick Around for Years

Evaluations stay available to source selection teams until three years after the contract's performance ends (six for construction and A-E). On a long contract, an early interim rating can follow you across every proposal you submit for that entire period. The damage isn't just to the past performance factor. It infects the entire competitive narrative. Evaluators reading a Marginal rating will look harder at your risk mitigation, your management approach, and your cost realism. A bad CPARS record doesn't just hurt one factor; it raises questions across the board.

How to Proactively Position for an Exceptional Rating

Don't wait for evaluation time to think about your CPARS. Manage toward it from Day 1 of contract performance.

1. Understand the Evaluation Areas

CPARS evaluates contractors across several standard areas depending on contract type. Common areas include:

  • Technical / Quality: Conformance to requirements, workmanship, innovation
  • Schedule: Adherence to milestones, on-time delivery, responsiveness
  • Cost Control: Forecasting, variance management, avoiding overruns (on cost-type contracts)
  • Management / Business Relations: Communication, subcontractor management, problem-solving attitude, COR relationship
  • Small Business Utilization: Meeting subcontracting plan goals, using SBs in meaningful roles

Know which areas apply to your contract. Assign an internal owner for each one. Build a performance dashboard that tracks your metrics against the evaluation criteria throughout the year.

2. Communicate Proactively With Your COR

Many bad CPARS ratings start as communication failures, not performance failures. The COR finds out about a problem in the deliverable review, not from you proactively flagging it in week two when you still had time to fix it. Document every achievement, every challenge, and every corrective action in writing. Your contract file should tell a story of competent, transparent performance.

3. Brief the COR Before the Evaluation Period

Before the CPARS evaluation is written, schedule a meeting with your COR to walk through your performance achievements. Bring data: on-time delivery rates, quality metrics, cost variance reports, innovation examples, subcontracting plan compliance. Give the COR the language to write a strong evaluation. Don't assume they remember everything from the past 12 months. They're busy people managing multiple contracts.

4. Review and Respond to the Draft Rating

Under FAR 42.1503(d), you get up to 14 calendar days from notification to submit comments, rebuttals, or more information. Under DoD's CPARS guidance, the evaluation becomes available for source selection on day 15 after the Assessing Official signs it, with or without your comments. CPARS keeps your comment window open for 60 days, but anything you send after day 14 lands on a record source selection teams can already see. Use the first 14 days. If you believe the rating is too low (and you have documented evidence), provide specific, factual, professional comments that address each area. A well-reasoned contractor comment can cause the Assessing Official to revise an assessment upward. It also becomes part of the permanent record that source selection officials read.

How to Dispute a Bad CPARS Rating

If you receive a rating you believe is inaccurate, act immediately and professionally. The process:

  1. Review it right away: Read the evaluation as soon as CPARS notifies you. Document exactly what you disagree with and why. If you want to talk it through, DoD's CPARS guidance lets you request a meeting with the Assessing Official, in writing, within 7 calendar days of receiving the evaluation.
  2. Submit written comments: Provide a detailed, evidence-backed rebuttal within the 14-day window. Reference the contract, the specific deliverables, and the documented record of your performance. Cite communications, delivery receipts, quality reports: anything that contradicts the rating narrative.
  3. Mark that you don't concur: The FAR requires disagreements to be reviewed at a level above the contracting officer. In CPARS, when you don't concur, the Reviewing Official must add comments and sign the evaluation. Document everything.
  4. File a claim if it matters enough: If the agency won't fix an inaccurate rating, you can file a claim with the contracting officer under the Contract Disputes Act and appeal a denial to a Board of Contract Appeals or the Court of Federal Claims. A board can't assign you a specific rating, but it can send the evaluation back for a fair redo (Inside Government Contracts on an ASBCA decision).
  5. Know what a protest can't do: A GAO bid protest isn't the place to challenge a CPARS rating by itself, because a rating isn't a procurement decision. However, if an inaccurate CPARS rating contributes to a lost competition, you may have grounds to challenge the source selection. Consult legal counsel for material ratings disputes.

The most important rule: Never let a bad CPARS rating stand unchallenged. A Marginal or Unsatisfactory with zero contractor response looks like tacit agreement. A Marginal rating with a detailed, factual rebuttal, especially one that identifies specific procedural errors by the government, is a far less damaging record. Source selection officials read the contractor comments. Make yours count.

How CPARS Feeds Into Source Selections

When evaluators pull your CPARS record during a source selection, here's what happens:

  1. They assess the recency of your evaluations (within three years for most contracts).
  2. They assess the relevance of each prior contract to the current requirement (scope, dollar value, complexity, domain).
  3. They assign a combined past performance confidence rating (in DoD: Substantial, Satisfactory, Neutral, Limited, or No Confidence, per the DoD Source Selection Procedures) based on the totality of the record.
  4. The SSA (Source Selection Authority) uses this confidence rating in the best value tradeoff against Technical and Price factors.

If you have no relevant past performance record at all (as most new entrants do), you receive a Neutral Confidence rating, because the FAR says you may not be evaluated favorably or unfavorably on past performance. This is neutral, not negative. Many experienced proposal teams use a Neutral past performance rating as an opening to describe their team's individual experience and their management plan's risk-mitigation approach. It's not ideal, but it's workable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can see my CPARS records?

Any federal acquisition official with a need to know: primarily source selection teams, contracting officers, and program managers evaluating your qualifications for a contract. FAR 42.1503(d) bars releasing a completed evaluation to anyone other than government personnel and the contractor being evaluated while it can be used for source selection, so competitors and the general public can't see it. However, you as the contractor can access your own records in CPARS.

What if I have no CPARS records? Is that a problem?

Not necessarily a fatal one. New entrants with no federal contract history receive a "Neutral" past performance confidence rating in source selections. Source selection authorities are permitted to evaluate this neutrally. It's not a Marginal or Unsatisfactory. To address it, your proposal should lean heavily on the relevant experience of your key personnel and articulate a management approach that reduces performance risk. A strong team with a clear risk mitigation plan can overcome a thin past performance record on smaller contracts.

Can a contractor challenge a CPARS rating through a bid protest?

Generally no. A CPARS rating isn't a procurement decision, so a bid protest isn't the tool for challenging it directly. However, if an inaccurate CPARS rating causes an unfair evaluation in a specific source selection, that evaluation can be challenged through the protest process. For the rating itself, use the FAR's process first: respond in writing within 14 days, mark that you don't concur so it gets reviewed above the contracting officer, and build a documented record. If that fails, a Contract Disputes Act claim to the contracting officer can go to a Board of Contract Appeals or the Court of Federal Claims.

How is the CPARS rating for a subcontractor different from a prime?

CPARS evaluations are written for the prime contractor, the company that holds the contract with the government. Subcontractors do not receive direct CPARS evaluations; their performance is reflected in the prime's evaluation. When you're acting as a sub, your prime's CPARS record reflects your work, for better or worse. This is one reason choosing your prime partners carefully matters: a poorly performing prime can produce a bad CPARS record that reflects on you, even if your portion of the work was excellent.

How long does a CPARS rating affect my competitive standing?

Source selection teams can use CPARS evaluations for three years after performance on the contract or order is complete (six years for construction and architect-engineer contracts). After that window, the record is off the table. The clock runs from the end of the contract, not the date of the rating, so a bad interim rating early in a long contract can stay in play until three years after the whole contract ends. Act fast when ratings are in dispute.
