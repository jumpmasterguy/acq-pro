CPARS Ratings: Why Your Past Performance Score Is Your Most Valuable Asset

An Exceptional CPARS rating can win you a contract even if your price is higher. An Unsatisfactory one can disqualify you before evaluators read your technical volume.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 8 min read · For: Contractor

In 2023, past performance challenges were raised in 52 GAO (Government Accountability Office) bid protest decisions and 16 Court of Federal Claims cases. In 2022, that number was 61 GAO decisions. The sheer volume of protests over past performance evaluations tells you one thing: these ratings matter enormously — and contractors know it.

CPARS — the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System — is the federal government's official database of contractor performance records. Every evaluation your government customer writes about your work lives in CPARS. Every source selection team at every federal agency can read it before deciding whether to put your company on the award.

Your CPARS record is not just a compliance artifact. It is a competitive asset — or a competitive liability. This guide explains what it is, how it works, and how to manage it strategically.

What Is CPARS?

CPARS is the government-wide, web-based system through which federal agencies assess and record contractor performance on contracts above reporting thresholds. It feeds directly into PPIRS — the Past Performance Information Retrieval System — which is the read side: what source selection officials access during proposal evaluation.

Under FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) Subpart 42.15, agencies are required to prepare past performance evaluations:

  • At least annually during contract performance
  • At contract completion
  • At the exercise of each option period

Dollar thresholds for mandatory CPARS reporting vary by contract type but generally begin at $2 million for services and $7.5 million for construction. However, agencies may evaluate performance on contracts below these thresholds when they choose to.

CPARS evaluations remain accessible in PPIRS for three years for most contracts (six years for construction and architect-engineer contracts). During that window, any federal agency's source selection team can read your full rating narrative — not just the adjectival label, but the written explanation behind it.

How CPARS Ratings Work: The Five Levels

Rating Official FAR Definition Frequency in Winning Proposals Contractor Action Required
Exceptional Performance meets contractual requirements and exceeds many to the Government's benefit. Accomplished with few minor problems for which contractor corrective actions were highly effective. Multiple significant benefit events. No significant weaknesses. Highest competitive advantage — often the differentiator that justifies a higher price under Best Value Tradeoff. Rare; reserved for truly outstanding performance. Position proactively throughout contract. Document every above-and-beyond action. Brief the COR/CO on achievements before evaluation period closes. Review the draft rating and provide substantive comments if appropriate.
Very Good Performance meets contractual requirements and exceeds some to the Government's benefit. Accomplished with some minor problems for which corrective actions were effective. At least one significant benefit event. No significant weaknesses. Strong competitive asset. Most consistently high-performing contractors land here. Keeps you competitive in most source selections. Identify at least one major achievement to cite with the evaluator. Address all minor issues promptly. If you believe an Exceptional rating is warranted, document the evidence and discuss with the COR before the evaluation period.
Satisfactory Performance meets contractual requirements. Contains some minor problems for which corrective actions appear or were satisfactory. No significant weaknesses. Contractors cannot be rated below Satisfactory solely for not exceeding requirements. Neutral — maintains eligibility but provides no competitive advantage. In a competitive source selection, a field of Satisfactory ratings is undifferentiated on past performance. Understand why you're not Very Good. Were corrective actions visible to the COR? Did you document proactive problem resolution? A Satisfactory is a signal to improve your performance management and COR communication strategy.
Marginal Performance does not meet some contractual requirements. Problems for which corrective actions taken appear or were only marginally effective. At least one significant weakness identified. Significant competitive disadvantage. Source selection officials will note this and weight it against you in best value tradeoffs. Some agencies use it as a near-disqualifier. Respond formally in writing within 14 days (your right under FAR). Document your corrective actions with specificity. Escalate the dispute through the contracting hierarchy if the rating is inaccurate. This rating can and should be challenged if not warranted.
Unsatisfactory Performance does not meet most contractual requirements and recovery is not likely in a timely manner. Multiple significant events that the contractor had trouble overcoming. Significant impact to the Government. Near-certain disqualifier. Exceptional or Very Good ratings elsewhere cannot overcome an Unsatisfactory in most source selections. This rating signals unacceptable performance risk. Dispute immediately and aggressively. An Unsatisfactory with no contractor comment looks much worse than one with a detailed, documented rebuttal. Engage legal counsel if necessary. A single uncontested Unsatisfactory can damage your pipeline for three years.

Who Writes Your CPARS Rating

Understanding who evaluates you helps you manage the relationship that determines your score.

The Contracting Officer's Representative (COR) is typically the primary author of CPARS evaluations. The COR is the government employee who monitors day-to-day contract performance — they're the person you're working with most closely throughout the contract. Their technical expertise and daily observations form the basis of your performance narrative.

The Contracting Officer (CO) reviews and approves the final CPARS evaluation before it's released. The CO has legal authority over the contract and is ultimately responsible for the accuracy of the performance record.

In some cases — particularly on larger programs or complex technical contracts — additional program office staff (Program Managers, technical representatives) contribute to the evaluation. The key is that someone who has actually observed your performance is required to write the rating. A CO who has no direct knowledge of your work should be relying on the COR's informed assessment, not writing a generic evaluation.

The relationship is everything: CORs are people doing a demanding job. Contractors who communicate proactively, deliver on commitments, and make the COR's life easier get better CPARS ratings — not because the system is subjective, but because the documented record of their behavior reflects genuinely better performance. Be the contractor your COR wants to write a glowing evaluation for. That means regular status updates, early flagging of problems, and visible corrective actions.

How CPARS Ratings Affect Future Competitions

Every source selection official reviewing your proposal will access your CPARS record in PPIRS before scoring your Past Performance factor. Here's what they're looking for and how it affects your competitive position:

Relevance Matters as Much as Quality

Source selection boards evaluate past performance on two dimensions: quality (the rating) and relevance (how closely the prior contract matches the current requirement in scope, complexity, dollar value, and technical domain). An Outstanding rating on a $500K janitorial contract doesn't help you much on a $50M software development program. You need to build performance records that are not just high-quality but directly analogous to the work you're pursuing.

Exceptional and Very Good Ratings Create Best Value Arguments

In a Best Value Tradeoff (BVTO) acquisition, a source selection authority can award to a higher-priced offeror if the technical or past performance advantages justify the premium. A contractor with Exceptional CPARS ratings can legitimately charge more and still win — because the past performance record reduces the government's performance risk, which has real dollar value. This is one of the most powerful competitive levers available in GovCon.

Marginal or Unsatisfactory Ratings Survive Three Years

CPARS records stay in PPIRS for three years (six for construction). A single bad rating can follow you across every proposal you submit for that entire period. The damage isn't just to the past performance factor — it infects the entire competitive narrative. Evaluators reading a Marginal rating will look harder at your risk mitigation, your management approach, and your cost realism. A bad CPARS record doesn't just hurt one factor; it raises questions across the board.

How to Proactively Position for an Exceptional Rating

Don't wait for evaluation time to think about your CPARS. Manage toward it from Day 1 of contract performance.

1. Understand the Evaluation Areas

CPARS evaluates contractors across several standard areas depending on contract type. Common areas include:

  • Technical / Quality: Conformance to requirements, workmanship, innovation
  • Schedule: Adherence to milestones, on-time delivery, responsiveness
  • Cost Control: Forecasting, variance management, avoiding overruns (on cost-type contracts)
  • Management / Business Relations: Communication, subcontractor management, problem-solving attitude, COR relationship
  • Small Business Utilization: Meeting subcontracting plan goals, using SBs in meaningful roles

Know which areas apply to your contract. Assign an internal owner for each one. Build a performance dashboard that tracks your metrics against the evaluation criteria throughout the year.

2. Communicate Proactively With Your COR

Most bad CPARS ratings stem from communication failures, not performance failures. The COR finds out about a problem in the deliverable review — not from you proactively flagging it in week two when you still had time to fix it. Document every achievement, every challenge, and every corrective action in writing. Your contract file should tell a story of competent, transparent performance.

3. Brief the COR Before the Evaluation Period

Before the CPARS evaluation is written, schedule a meeting with your COR to walk through your performance achievements. Bring data: on-time delivery rates, quality metrics, cost variance reports, innovation examples, subcontracting plan compliance. Give the COR the language to write a strong evaluation — don't assume they remember everything from the past 12 months. They're busy people managing multiple contracts.

4. Review and Respond to the Draft Rating

Under FAR, you have the right to review a CPARS draft evaluation and provide comments within 14 days before it's finalized. Use this right. If you believe the rating is too low — and you have documented evidence — provide specific, factual, professional comments that address each area. A well-reasoned contractor comment can cause the CO to revise an assessment upward. It also becomes part of the permanent record that source selection officials read.

How to Dispute a Bad CPARS Rating

If you receive a rating you believe is inaccurate, act immediately and professionally. The process:

  1. Review the draft: Request the draft immediately when notified. Document exactly what you disagree with and why.
  2. Submit written comments: Provide a detailed, evidence-backed rebuttal within the 14-day window. Reference the contract, the specific deliverables, and the documented record of your performance. Cite communications, delivery receipts, quality reports — anything that contradicts the rating narrative.
  3. Escalate through the contracting hierarchy: If the CO does not revise the rating, you can escalate to the CO's supervisor or the agency's small business advocate. Document everything.
  4. Request a higher-level review: FAR 42.1503 provides a process for challenging CPARS evaluations. Some agencies have formal review boards for disputed ratings.
  5. Legal options: A GAO protest is generally not available to challenge CPARS ratings directly (they're administrative actions, not procurement decisions). However, if an inaccurate CPARS rating contributes to a lost competition, you may have grounds to challenge the source selection. Consult legal counsel for material ratings disputes.

The most important rule: Never let a bad CPARS rating stand unchallenged. A Marginal or Unsatisfactory with zero contractor response looks like tacit agreement. A Marginal rating with a detailed, factual rebuttal — especially one that identifies specific procedural errors by the government — is a far less damaging record. Source selection officials read the contractor comments. Make yours count.

How CPARS Feeds Into Source Selections

When evaluators access your PPIRS record during a source selection, here's what happens:

  1. They assess the recency of your evaluations (within three years for most contracts).
  2. They assess the relevance of each prior contract to the current requirement (scope, dollar value, complexity, domain).
  3. They assign a combined past performance confidence rating — typically Outstanding / Substantial Confidence / Satisfactory Confidence / Limited Confidence / No Confidence — based on the totality of the record.
  4. The SSA (Source Selection Authority) uses this confidence rating in the best value tradeoff against Technical and Price factors.

If you have no relevant past performance record at all — as most new entrants do — you typically receive a "Neutral" or "Unknown" confidence rating. This is neutral, not negative. Many experienced proposal teams use a Neutral past performance rating as an opening to describe their team's individual experience and their management plan's risk-mitigation approach. It's not ideal, but it's workable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can see my CPARS records?

Any federal acquisition official with a need to know — primarily source selection teams, contracting officers, and program managers evaluating your qualifications for a contract. CPARS records are protected from public disclosure under the Privacy Act; they are not accessible to competitors or the general public. However, you as the contractor can access your own records in CPARS.

What if I have no CPARS records — is that a problem?

Not necessarily a fatal one. New entrants with no federal contract history receive a "Neutral" past performance confidence rating in source selections. Source selection authorities are permitted to evaluate this neutrally — it's not a Marginal or Unsatisfactory. To address it, your proposal should lean heavily on the relevant experience of your key personnel and articulate a management approach that reduces performance risk. A strong team with a clear risk mitigation plan can overcome a thin past performance record on smaller contracts.

Can a contractor challenge a CPARS rating through a bid protest?

Generally no — CPARS ratings are administrative actions, not procurement decisions, and the GAO and COFC typically don't have jurisdiction to directly modify them. However, if an inaccurate CPARS rating causes an unfair evaluation in a specific source selection, that evaluation can be challenged through the protest process. The more effective path is the FAR-provided administrative dispute mechanism — respond in writing, escalate through the chain, and create a documented record of the dispute before the rating impacts a competition.

How is the CPARS rating for a subcontractor different from a prime?

CPARS evaluations are written for the prime contractor — the company that holds the contract with the government. Subcontractors do not receive direct CPARS evaluations; their performance is reflected in the prime's evaluation. When you're acting as a sub, your prime's CPARS record reflects your work — for better or worse. This is one reason choosing your prime partners carefully matters: a poorly performing prime can produce a bad CPARS record that reflects on you, even if your portion of the work was excellent.

How long does a CPARS rating affect my competitive standing?

Most CPARS records are considered in source selections for three years from the end of the relevant contract performance period (six years for construction and architect-engineer contracts). After that window, the record is no longer visible to source selection teams. This means a bad rating from five years ago doesn't hurt you today, but a bad rating from six months ago follows you for 2.5 more years of competitions. Act fast when ratings are in dispute.
