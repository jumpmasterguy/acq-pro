CRs: The Hidden Risk Crippling Your Defense Program's Future

For USG personnel, understanding how continuing resolutions undermine strategic planning, inflate costs, and erode readiness is crucial for program survival.

By Acqlerate · May 2026 · 8 min read · For: USG Personnel

The Perpetual Pause: Why Budget Gridlock is Your Program's Silent Threat

As USG personnel, you’re no stranger to the term "Continuing Resolution" (CR). What began as an infrequent stopgap has unfortunately become an annual, multi-month tradition, fundamentally undermining strategic planning, inflating costs, and eroding the competitive edge of the American military-industrial complex. For defense program managers (PMs) and their teams, navigating this predictable unpredictability is the new normal – a normal that is increasingly unsustainable. This isn't just an inconvenience; it's a profound, hidden risk to your program's very ability to deliver critical capabilities to the warfighter.

FY25 & FY26: Expecting More of the Same (or Worse)

The U.S. government has operated under a CR for significant portions of the fiscal year for over a decade. Fiscal Year 2024 offers a stark reminder: the Department of Defense (DoD) operated under *four* separate CRs for over five months, from October 1, 2023, until March 8, 2024. This protracted delay, nearly half the fiscal year, meant:
  • **Frozen Spending:** DoD programs operated at FY23 levels ($816.7 billion), despite an FY24 request of $886 billion. This created an immediate effective funding shortfall due to inflation.
  • **"New Start" Prohibition:** Approximately 100 new programs across the services, including critical research efforts in AI, hypersonics, and next-generation sensing, were delayed.
  • **Industrial Base Strain:** Programs designed to ramp up production, such as the Virginia-class submarine and F-35 fighter jet, were forced to maintain lower rates, creating inefficiencies and uncertainty for suppliers.
Looking ahead, the outlook for FY25 and FY26 suggests a continuation, if not an exacerbation, of this trend. The Fiscal Responsibility Act (FRA) of 2023 set statutory spending caps for FY24 and FY25, limiting base defense spending to $886 billion and $895 billion respectively. While these caps provide a framework, they haven't eliminated the deep ideological divides in Congress. The DoD's FY25 budget request of $895.2 billion, adhering to the FRA caps, is already considered "flat" in real terms after accounting for inflation. Given this historical pattern and current political climate, it is **highly probable** that the DoD will begin Fiscal Year 2025 (October 1, 2024) operating under a CR. This initial CR could extend well into calendar year 2025, mirroring the FY24 experience. For FY26, the FRA caps expire, meaning Congress will face an open field for defense spending debates, potentially leading to even more protracted and contentious negotiations. **So what?** Expect significant delays and plan for reduced buying power. The political landscape makes timely appropriations highly unlikely for the foreseeable future, making proactive planning essential for your program's survival.

Direct Hits: How CRs Cripple Program Managers

The persistent use of CRs has moved beyond a mere inconvenience to become a fundamental obstacle to effective acquisition for Program Managers.
  • **Strategic Paralysis for New Starts:** Any "new start" program in the FY25 budget request will be **frozen indefinitely** until full appropriations pass. This means no contract awards, no hiring for program offices, and no initial development.

    Practical Impact: PMs must prepare for significant delays. Pre-award activities can continue, but the critical contract award is stalled. This forces PMs to consider "bridge" strategies or risk losing initial momentum and industry interest.

  • **Production Rate Inefficiencies & Capability Delays:** Programs slated for production ramps are stuck at prior-year rates, disrupting planned economies of scale and delaying critical capabilities to the warfighter.

    Practical Impact: PMs face higher per-unit costs, delayed delivery schedules, and the risk of prime contractors and their supply chains shedding skilled labor due to uncertainty. Communicating these impacts to leadership and managing expectations with warfighters becomes paramount.

  • **Research & Development (R&D) Stagnation:** CRs disproportionately harm early-stage R&D. While existing contracts continue, new research grants, prototyping efforts, and competitive solicitations for innovative technologies are often delayed or canceled.

    Practical Impact: PMs overseeing cutting-edge efforts will struggle to maintain the pace of innovation, potentially ceding technological advantage to adversaries. Funding for university research partnerships also suffers.

  • **Workforce Instability & Morale:** Hiring freezes for civilian DoD personnel and uncertainty for contractors create a "brain drain." Talented individuals may seek more stable employment.

    Practical Impact: PMs will struggle to staff critical roles, leading to increased workload for existing personnel and potential burnout. Contractors face challenges in retaining specialized engineers and technicians without clear funding signals.

  • **Inflationary Erosion of Buying Power:** Operating at prior-year funding levels for months means a real-dollar cut due to inflation. For FY25, a CR means operating on FY24 budget levels ($886B) while inflation continues to eat away at purchasing power.

    Practical Impact: PMs must make difficult choices, often cutting scope, deferring maintenance, or reducing quantities for existing programs to stay within effective budget constraints.

So what? PMs need proactive strategies to mitigate these predictable disruptions; waiting for full appropriations is a losing game. Engage early, plan for contingencies, and communicate transparently.

The Ripple Effect: When the Industrial Base Feels the Squeeze

The impact of CRs extends far beyond government offices, creating significant challenges for the defense industrial base – your essential partners in delivering capabilities.
  • **Cash Flow Crisis, Especially for Small Businesses:** Delayed contract awards, slow start-up funding, and uncertainty about production rates directly impact revenue streams. Small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs), often critical suppliers in the lower tiers, are particularly vulnerable.

    Practical Impact: Contractors must maintain larger cash reserves, diversify their client base, and be prepared for extended payment cycles. SMBs may face bankruptcy or be acquired by larger firms, further consolidating the industrial base.

  • **Workforce Management Challenges:** Uncertainty about future contracts makes it difficult to hire and retain specialized talent. Layoffs or hiring freezes become necessary to manage costs during CR periods.

    Practical Impact: Companies risk losing key personnel to commercial sectors or competitors. Rebuilding these teams once funding arrives is costly and time-consuming, leading to further program delays.

  • **Supply Chain Disruptions:** Fluctuations in production schedules due to CRs ripple down the supply chain. Suppliers face unpredictable demand, making it difficult to plan material procurement and manufacturing capacity.

    Practical Impact: Increased lead times, higher material costs, and potential loss of suppliers who exit the defense market due to instability.

  • **Reduced Investment in Internal R&D:** When government funding is uncertain, contractors often scale back their own internal research and development (IRAD) investments to preserve capital.

    Practical Impact: This stifles innovation within the private sector, making it harder for the DoD to tap into cutting-edge commercial technologies.

So what? Understand your contractors' pain points; their stability is your program's stability. Proactive communication, realistic expectations, and exploring avenues for risk sharing are vital to maintaining a healthy industrial base.

The challenges faced by both government and industry under CRs are deeply intertwined, creating a cumulative effect that erodes national security.

Every month of delay in fielding a new technology or increasing production capacity translates directly into a strategic disadvantage on the global stage.

The continued instability undermines the DoD's ability to execute its National Defense Strategy, particularly in areas like deterring aggression from peer competitors and rapidly fielding advanced capabilities. The cost of this political gridlock is measured not just in dollars, but in delayed capabilities, lost talent, and a diminished competitive edge on the global stage.

Navigating the Gridlock: What You Can Do

While you can't control congressional action, you *can* control how your program prepares for and responds to CRs. Here are practical steps:
  • **Scenario Planning is Paramount:** Develop detailed contingency plans for various CR durations (e.g., 3-month, 6-month, 9-month). Identify critical path activities, assess funding flexibilities, and understand the cascading impacts.
  • **Prioritize and Protect:** Clearly identify your program's absolute essential functions and capabilities. During a CR, focus resources on maintaining these core elements, even if it means deferring less critical activities.
  • **Leverage Prior-Year Funding:** For existing programs, explore unobligated balances from prior fiscal years to maintain momentum where possible. This is not a solution for new starts, but can be a lifeline for ongoing efforts.
  • **Proactive Communication:** Maintain open and frequent communication with your leadership, warfighters, and industry partners. Transparency about potential delays and their impacts helps manage expectations and build trust.
  • **Intensify Pre-Award Activities:** For "new starts," use the CR period to refine requirements, conduct market research, hold industry days, and prepare Request for Proposals (RFPs) so you are ready to award contracts the moment full appropriations pass.
  • **Advocate with Data:** Document the real-world impacts of CRs on your program – the cost increases, schedule delays, and lost opportunities. This data is crucial for advocating for more timely and stable funding.

So what? Be proactive, not reactive. Develop robust contingency plans, communicate relentlessly, and leverage every tool at your disposal to minimize disruptions. Your ability to navigate this gridlock directly impacts your program's success and national security.
