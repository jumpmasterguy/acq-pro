How to Win as a Small Business in Federal Contracting

The government has set aside billions of dollars specifically for small businesses. Here's how to position your company to win your share.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 9 min read · For: Contractor

In fiscal year 2023, the federal government awarded over $163 billion in prime contracts to small businesses — surpassing the statutory 23% goal for the eleventh consecutive year. The DoD alone set its own small business goal at 22.5% of applicable contract dollars. These aren't aspirational numbers; they're legally mandated targets that contracting officers are measured against.

If you qualify as a small business under the SBA's (Small Business Administration) size standards, you have access to a set-aside marketplace that Fortune 500 companies literally cannot enter. The question isn't whether the opportunity exists — it's whether your business is positioned to compete for it.

The Five Socioeconomic Small Business Programs

The SBA administers five major socioeconomic certification programs, each with distinct eligibility requirements and competitive advantages. Understanding which programs apply to your business is the first strategic decision you'll make in GovCon.

Program Eligibility Requirements Annual DoD Goal Key Advantages Best Vehicles
Small Business (SB) — Base category Meet SBA size standards for primary NAICS code (varies: typically <$8M–$47M revenue or <500–1,500 employees depending on industry) 22.5% of all applicable DoD contract dollars Mandatory set-asides when 2+ SBs can compete; access to all socioeconomic vehicle pools; contracting officers must consider SB for acquisitions >$15K GSA MAS, OASIS+ SB pool, agency IDIQs, BPAs
8(a) Business Development 51%+ owned by socially/economically disadvantaged U.S. citizens; personal net worth <$850K; adjusted gross income <$400K; assets <$6.5M; in business 2+ years; no prior 8(a) participation 5% of all applicable DoD prime contract dollars Sole-source awards up to $4.5M (services) / $7M (manufacturing); 9-year program with structured BD support; access to SBA Mentor-Protégé Program; joint venture flexibility 8(a) STARS III ($50B ceiling, IT services), OASIS+ 8(a) pool, agency-specific 8(a) set-asides
SDVOSB — Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business 51%+ owned by service-disabled veterans with VA disability rating; veteran controls daily management and long-term decision-making; meet SBA size standards 3% of all applicable DoD prime contract dollars Set-aside and sole-source contracts; VA contract priority (VA uses SDVOSB/VOSB preference on all eligible acquisitions); growing non-VA set-aside use across DoD VA-specific SDVOSB vehicles, GSA Polaris SDVOSB pool, OASIS+ SDVOSB pool

HUBZone — Historically Underutilized Business Zone Principal office in a designated HUBZone; 35%+ of employees must live in a HUBZone; 51%+ U.S. citizen-owned; meet SBA size standards 3% of all applicable DoD prime contract dollars Set-aside competitions; 10% price evaluation preference in full-and-open competitions (can offset a higher price); geography-based — not tied to owner demographics GSA Polaris HUBZone pool, OASIS+ HUBZone pool, agency HUBZone set-asides

Stack your certifications: You can hold multiple certifications simultaneously. An 8(a)-certified SDVOSB, for example, can compete in 8(a) set-asides, SDVOSB set-asides, and standard SB set-asides — dramatically expanding your pool of accessible opportunities. If your business qualifies for more than one certification, pursue them all. All applications are processed through SBA's MySBA Certifications portal.

How SB Set-Asides Actually Work

When a contracting officer (CO) is preparing an acquisition, FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) Part 19 requires them to consider small business set-asides for every acquisition above the micro-purchase threshold ($15,000). For acquisitions between $15,000 and $350,000, the automatic set-aside rule applies — these must be set aside for small businesses if the simplified acquisition threshold conditions are met.

For larger acquisitions, the CO must determine whether there's a "reasonable expectation" that two or more small businesses can submit competitive offers at fair market prices. If yes — the acquisition is set aside for small businesses. This is the "Rule of Two" — and it's the mechanism that creates the billions in set-aside opportunities every year.

The practical implication: if you see a large contract being competed as a full-and-open (unrestricted) competition, it may be because no one told the contracting officer that small businesses can handle it. This is where your BD and market engagement pays off. If you're in front of the program office 12 months before the acquisition, you can influence whether it's set aside — and whether your certification category gets the nod.

The Best IDIQ Vehicles for Small Businesses

IDIQ — Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity — contracts are multi-award vehicles where the government pre-qualifies a pool of contractors, then issues task orders to compete within that pool. Getting on the right IDIQ is like getting a seat at the table for hundreds of future opportunities.

The major vehicles for small businesses:

OASIS+ SB (GSA)

GSA's One Acquisition Solution for Integrated Services Plus is the government's premier vehicle for professional services. OASIS+ has dedicated pools for SB, 8(a), SDVOSB, WOSB, and HUBZone businesses. Scope covers 13 professional services domains including management consulting, engineering, logistics, and R&D. Ceiling is approximately $60 billion. OASIS+ uses a continuous on-ramp model, so you can apply when you're ready rather than waiting for a fixed window.

8(a) STARS III (GSA)

The premier GWAC (Government-Wide Acquisition Contract) exclusively for SBA-certified 8(a) IT services firms. $50 billion ceiling. Sole-source task orders up to $5.5 million. Over 1,100 industry partners. Ordering period runs through July 2026 (with a 3-year option extending to 2029). If you're an 8(a)-certified IT services company, this is your highest-priority vehicle.

SEWP V / VI (NASA)

Solutions for Enterprise-Wide Procurement covers IT products, hardware, software, and cloud. NASA-managed, available government-wide. SEWP V ceiling was $20 billion; SEWP VI (forthcoming) is projected at $75 billion+. Small business categories provide a dedicated lane for SB IT providers, especially those selling products and product-based solutions.

GSA MAS (Multiple Award Schedule)

The evergreen on-ramp to federal buyers. GSA MAS covers commercial products and services across virtually every category. It's not set-aside by default, but agencies can restrict MAS task orders to SB pools. Getting on MAS is a foundational step for most small businesses — it signals that GSA has reviewed your rates and found them reasonable, which is a credibility signal to program offices.

Agency-Specific IDIQs

Many DoD agencies maintain their own IDIQ contracts for frequently-needed services. Army's MAPS (Marketplace for the Acquisition of Professional Services), SOCOM's SOFGSD (Special Operations Forces Global Services Delivery), and numerous program-specific vehicles offer smaller pools and more focused competition. These often have lower past performance thresholds than the big GWACs — making them ideal first IDIQ targets for newer small businesses.

Building Past Performance as a Small Business

Past performance is the hardest problem for any new GovCon entrant — and the most important one to solve early. Here's the sequenced approach:

Step 1: Subcontract Your Way In

Large prime contractors on contracts over $750,000 are required to establish subcontracting plans committing to SB utilization goals. Find the primes already working your target agencies and offer yourself as a subcontractor. You'll do real work, build a performance record, learn the customer's culture, and start generating references — all without the compliance burden of a prime contract.

Use SAM.gov's sub-net (subcontracting network) and the SBA's Dynamic Small Business Search (DSBS) to make yourself discoverable to primes looking for small business subs.

Step 2: Pursue Simplified Acquisitions

Contracts under $350,000 use streamlined procedures. Fewer proposals, faster awards, less competition. These contracts won't make you rich, but they build your CPARS record — the past performance database that every future evaluator will consult. A string of Outstanding CPARS ratings on $500K contracts is a legitimate foundation for bidding $5M and eventually $50M programs.

Step 3: Use OTAs and Innovation Vehicles

Other Transaction Authority (OTA) agreements are non-FAR vehicles that DoD uses for prototype development and research projects. They move faster, require less compliance overhead, and are popular entry points for technology-focused small businesses. AFWERX, Army Futures Command, and numerous DoD research labs run OTA programs specifically designed to bring in non-traditional companies.

Step 4: Leverage the Mentor-Protégé Program

The SBA Mentor-Protégé Program (MPP) pairs established large contractors (mentors) with eligible small businesses (protégés) in a formal developmental relationship. Under the program:

  • Mentors bring protégés in as joint venture partners on prime contracts
  • Joint ventures between mentor and protégé are exempt from small business size affiliation rules — meaning you can pursue large prime contracts you couldn't otherwise access
  • Mentors provide technical assistance, management training, and business development support
  • 8(a) firms have access to a parallel DoD-specific mentor-protégé program with additional benefits

The compounding effect: Past performance compounds. Your first $500K contract produces a CPARS record that makes winning a $2M contract easier. That CPARS record makes winning a $10M contract possible. That record qualifies you for IDIQ vehicles with $50M task orders. Every contract you win and perform well on is an investment in every contract that follows. Perform well — obsessively — on every single one.

Teaming With Large Primes

Large primes — the Lockheed Martins, BAH (Booz Allen Hamilton), Leidos, and SACOs of the world — need small businesses for three reasons:

  1. Subcontracting plan compliance: Their prime contracts require them to hit SB utilization goals.
  2. Capability gaps: They don't do everything in-house. Small businesses with specialized expertise get pulled onto large program teams.
  3. Set-aside teaming: On set-aside contracts, a large prime cannot be the prime — but they can be your sub. Switching the team structure to lead with a qualified SB often makes an opportunity accessible that otherwise wouldn't be.

Approaching primes effectively means leading with your capabilities, not your certification status. The DoD's own guidance is clear: don't lead with your socioeconomic status — lead with what you can do. Your HUBZone or SDVOSB status is a compliance benefit to the prime, not the reason they should pick you. Be the best technical solution first; let the certification be the tie-breaker.

Common Mistakes Small Businesses Make

  • Chasing everything: Small businesses often lack the BD discipline to say no. Bidding 20 poorly-qualified opportunities at 10% pWin each is less efficient than bidding four well-qualified opportunities at 55% pWin each. Apply the 80/20 rule ruthlessly.
  • Underinvesting in proposals: Winning government contracts requires professional proposal development. A sloppy, non-compliant proposal wastes your bid and proposal (B&P) investment entirely. Learn the process — or hire someone who knows it. See our full guide: The BD-to-Capture Lifecycle.
  • Letting certifications lapse: An expired 8(a) certification, an outdated SAM.gov registration, or a missed HUBZone recertification can disqualify you at the worst possible moment — mid-pursuit on a program you've been shaping for 18 months.
  • Outgrowing your certification and not planning for it: 8(a) firms have a 9-year clock. WOSB and SDVOSB firms must recertify annually. Plan your revenue growth strategy around your certification windows — what happens to your pipeline when you size out of the small business threshold?

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my company qualifies as a small business?

SBA defines small business size standards by NAICS code. Look up your primary NAICS code in SBA's Size Standards Tool at sba.gov. Standards are expressed as either annual revenue (e.g., under $8M, $16M, $47M) or employee count (e.g., under 500, 750, 1,500 employees) depending on the industry. You certify your size status in SAM.gov.

Can I hold more than one SBA certification at once?

Yes. There's no rule against holding multiple certifications simultaneously, and many businesses do. An SDVOSB can also be 8(a)-certified. A woman-owned business can also qualify as HUBZone. Each certification is assessed and maintained separately through MySBA Certifications. Holding multiple certifications dramatically expands the set of contracts you can pursue.

What's the 8(a) program's 9-year limit, and how should I plan for it?

The 8(a) program lasts nine years maximum — four in the developmental stage, five in the transitional stage. You can only participate once as an individual owner. During your 8(a) term, aggressively use sole-source awards to build past performance, accumulate CPARS ratings, win IDIQ vehicles that outlast your certification, and develop the commercial relationships and reputation that will sustain you as a full-and-open competitor after your 8(a) graduation. Plan the post-8(a) strategy starting in Year 5 or 6.

What's the difference between a set-aside and a sole-source award?

A set-aside is a competitive procurement limited to businesses in a specific category (e.g., all 8(a) firms, all SDVOSBs). Multiple companies compete and submit proposals. A sole-source award is made to a single company without competition — the government determines that only one company can meet the requirement, or uses a program-specific authority (like the 8(a) program's sole-source threshold). Sole-source awards are faster and eliminate competition entirely, but they're not available for all contract types or values.

How do I find prime contractors to team with?

Start with SAM.gov's subcontracting network and SBA's Dynamic Small Business Search (DSBS). Research which large primes are winning contracts at your target agencies — USASpending.gov shows award history by agency and contractor. Then approach them directly through their small business liaison offices, which most large contractors maintain specifically to identify SB partners. Industry associations like NDIA (National Defense Industrial Association) and AFCEA also facilitate prime-sub relationships.
