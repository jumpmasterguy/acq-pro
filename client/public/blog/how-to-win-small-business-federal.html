How to Win as a Small Business in Federal Contracting

The government has set aside billions of dollars specifically for small businesses. Here's how to position your company to win your share.

By Acqlerate · March 2026, updated September 2026 · 9 min read · For: Contractor

TL;DR. If a contracting officer expects two or more capable small firms to bid at fair prices (the Rule of Two), the work is set aside for small business. Be one of those firms, and be visible early.

  • New to this? Pursue every certification you qualify for: 8(a), SDVOSB, WOSB, HUBZone. They stack.
  • Short on past performance? Subcontract to primes, win small simplified acquisitions and use Mentor-Protégé to build your CPARS record.
  • Teaming with a prime? Lead with capability. Your certification is the tie-breaker, not the pitch.
  • Bidding everything? That's a lottery habit, not a business plan. Pick fewer, better-qualified bids.

In fiscal year 2025, the federal government awarded about $179 billion in prime contracts to small businesses, nearly 28% of eligible contract dollars and well above the statutory 23% goal. Every major agency, DoD included, is graded on its small business goals each year in SBA's scorecard. These aren't aspirational numbers; they're legal targets that contracting officers are measured against.

If you qualify as a small business under the SBA's (Small Business Administration) size standards, you have access to a set-aside marketplace that Fortune 500 companies literally cannot enter. The question isn't whether the opportunity exists. It's whether your business is positioned to compete for it.

The Five Socioeconomic Small Business Programs

The table below covers the base small business category and SBA's four socioeconomic certification programs (8(a), SDVOSB, WOSB and HUBZone), each with distinct eligibility requirements and competitive advantages. Understanding which programs apply to your business is the first strategic decision you'll make in GovCon.

8(a) Business Development 51%+ owned by socially/economically disadvantaged U.S. citizens; personal net worth <$850K; adjusted gross income <$400K; assets <$6.5M; in business 2+ years; no prior 8(a) participation 5% SDB goal (8(a) awards count toward it) Sole-source awards up to $5.5M (services) / $8.5M (manufacturing) since October 2025; 9-year program with structured BD support; access to SBA Mentor-Protégé Program; joint venture flexibility 8(a) STARS III ($50B ceiling, IT services), OASIS+ 8(a) pool, agency-specific 8(a) set-asides

Stack your certifications: You can hold multiple certifications simultaneously. An 8(a)-certified SDVOSB, for example, can compete in 8(a) set-asides, SDVOSB set-asides, and standard SB set-asides, dramatically expanding your pool of accessible opportunities. If your business qualifies for more than one certification, pursue them all. You apply to SBA for each one (WOSB can also go through an SBA-approved third-party certifier).

8(a) in 2026: The program is under heavy review. In January 2026 SBA said it will no longer presume social disadvantage based on race and suspended more than 1,000 participants that missed a financial data call. The Department of War also ordered a review of set-aside and sole-source contracts over $20 million. If you're in 8(a) or applying, expect more paperwork and scrutiny.

How SB Set-Asides Actually Work

When a contracting officer (CO) is preparing an acquisition, FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) Part 19 requires them to consider small business set-asides for every acquisition above the micro-purchase threshold ($15,000). For acquisitions between $15,000 and $350,000, the automatic set-aside rule applies: these must be set aside for small businesses unless the contracting officer can't reasonably expect offers from two or more responsible small businesses (FAR 19.502-2).

For larger acquisitions, the CO must determine whether there's a "reasonable expectation" that two or more small businesses can submit competitive offers at fair market prices. If yes, the acquisition is set aside for small businesses. That's the Rule of Two, and it's the mechanism that creates billions in set-aside opportunities every year.

The practical implication: if you see a large contract being competed as a full-and-open (unrestricted) competition, it may be because no one told the contracting officer that small businesses can handle it. This is where your BD and market engagement pays off. If you're in front of the program office 12 months before the acquisition, you can influence whether it's set aside, and whether your certification category gets the nod.

The Best IDIQ Vehicles for Small Businesses

IDIQ (Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity) contracts are multi-award vehicles where the government pre-qualifies a pool of contractors, then issues task orders to compete within that pool. Getting on the right IDIQ is like getting a seat at the table for hundreds of future opportunities.

The major vehicles for small businesses:

OASIS+ SB (GSA)

GSA's One Acquisition Solution for Integrated Services Plus is the government's premier vehicle for professional services. OASIS+ has dedicated pools for SB, 8(a), SDVOSB, WOSB, and HUBZone businesses. Scope covers 13 professional services domains including management consulting, engineering, logistics, and R&D. There is no program ceiling. Since January 2026, OASIS+ solicitations stay open continuously, so you can apply when you're ready rather than waiting for a fixed window.

8(a) STARS III (GSA)

The premier GWAC (Government-Wide Acquisition Contract) exclusively for SBA-certified 8(a) IT services firms. $50 billion ceiling. Directed (sole-source) task orders up to $5.5 million for most firms. More than 1,100 firms were awarded spots. The ordering period runs to 1 July 2029. If you're an 8(a)-certified IT services company, this is your highest-priority vehicle.

SEWP V / VI (NASA)

Solutions for Enterprise-Wide Procurement covers IT products, hardware, software, and cloud. NASA-managed, available government-wide. NASA awarded SEWP VI in June 2026: 2,115 awards across three categories, most of them to small firms, with ordering expected to start around 1 November 2026 while SEWP V is extended to cover the transition. It's a strong lane for small IT providers, especially those selling products and product-based solutions.

GSA MAS (Multiple Award Schedule)

The evergreen on-ramp to federal buyers. GSA MAS covers commercial products and services across virtually every category. It's not set-aside by default, but agencies can restrict MAS task orders to SB pools. Getting on MAS is a foundational step for most small businesses. It signals that GSA has reviewed your rates and found them reasonable, which is a credibility signal to program offices.

Agency-Specific IDIQs

Many DoD agencies maintain their own IDIQ contracts for frequently-needed services. The Army's new MAPS (Marketplace for the Acquisition of Professional Services, with proposals due in May 2026), SOCOM's SOFGSD (Special Operations Forces Global Services Delivery, a small business set-aside), and numerous program-specific vehicles offer smaller pools and more focused competition. They're worth a look as early IDIQ targets for newer small businesses.

Building Past Performance as a Small Business

Past performance is the hardest problem for any new GovCon entrant, and the most important one to solve early. Here's the sequenced approach:

Step 1: Subcontract Your Way In

Large prime contractors on contracts over $900,000 (raised from $750,000 on 1 October 2025) are required to establish subcontracting plans committing to SB utilization goals. Find the primes already working your target agencies and offer yourself as a subcontractor. You'll do real work, build a performance record, learn the customer's culture, and start generating references, all without the compliance burden of a prime contract.

Use SBA's SubNet (subcontracting network) and SBA's Small Business Search (SBS, which replaced the old Dynamic Small Business Search) to make yourself discoverable to primes looking for small business subs.

Step 2: Pursue Simplified Acquisitions

Contracts under $350,000 use streamlined procedures. Fewer proposals, faster awards, less competition. These contracts won't make you rich, but they build your CPARS record, the past performance database that every future evaluator will consult. A string of Outstanding CPARS ratings on $500K contracts is a legitimate foundation for bidding $5M and eventually $50M programs.

Step 3: Use OTAs and Innovation Vehicles

Other Transaction Authority (OTA) agreements are non-FAR vehicles that DoD uses for prototype development and research projects. They move faster, require less compliance overhead, and are popular entry points for technology-focused small businesses. AFWERX and numerous DoD research labs run programs specifically designed to bring in non-traditional companies.

Step 4: Use the Mentor-Protégé Program

The SBA Mentor-Protégé Program (MPP) pairs established large contractors (mentors) with eligible small businesses (protégés) in a formal developmental relationship. Under the program:

  • Mentors bring protégés in as joint venture partners on prime contracts
  • Joint ventures between mentor and protégé are exempt from small business size affiliation rules, meaning you can pursue large prime contracts you couldn't otherwise access
  • Mentors provide technical assistance, management training, and business development support
  • DoD also runs its own, separate Mentor-Protégé Program for eligible small businesses

The compounding effect: Past performance compounds. For example (hypothetical): your first $500K contract produces a CPARS record that makes winning a $2M contract easier. That CPARS record makes winning a $10M contract possible. That record qualifies you for IDIQ vehicles with $50M task orders. Every contract you win and perform well on is an investment in every contract that follows. Perform well, obsessively, on every single one.

Teaming With Large Primes

Large primes (the Lockheed Martins, BAH (Booz Allen Hamilton), Leidos, and SAICs of the world) need small businesses for three reasons:

  1. Subcontracting plan compliance: Their prime contracts require them to hit SB utilization goals.
  2. Capability gaps: They don't do everything in-house. Small businesses with specialized expertise get pulled onto large program teams.
  3. Set-aside teaming: On set-aside contracts, a large prime cannot be the prime, but they can be your sub, within the limits on subcontracting (on services, you can't pay more than 50% of what the government pays you to subs that aren't similarly situated). Switching the team structure to lead with a qualified SB often makes an opportunity accessible that otherwise wouldn't be.

Approaching primes effectively means leading with your capabilities, not your certification status. Our advice: don't lead with your socioeconomic status. Lead with what you can do. Your HUBZone or SDVOSB status is a compliance benefit to the prime, not the reason they should pick you. Be the best technical solution first; let the certification be the tie-breaker.

Common Mistakes Small Businesses Make

  • Chasing everything: Small businesses often lack the BD discipline to say no. For example (hypothetical): 20 poorly qualified bids at a 10% win probability (pWin) each yield about two wins; four well-qualified bids at 55% each yield about the same, for a fifth of the work. Apply the 80/20 rule ruthlessly.
  • Underinvesting in proposals: Winning government contracts requires professional proposal development. A sloppy, non-compliant proposal wastes your bid and proposal (B&P) investment entirely. Learn the process, or hire someone who knows it. See our full guide: The BD-to-Capture Lifecycle.
  • Letting certifications lapse: An expired 8(a) certification, an outdated SAM.gov registration, or a missed HUBZone recertification can disqualify you at the worst possible moment: mid-pursuit on a program you've been shaping for 18 months.
  • Outgrowing your certification and not planning for it: 8(a) firms have a 9-year clock. WOSB, SDVOSB and HUBZone certifications must be renewed every three years. Plan your revenue growth strategy around your certification windows. What happens to your pipeline when you size out of the small business threshold?

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my company qualifies as a small business?

SBA defines small business size standards by NAICS code. Look up your primary NAICS code in SBA's Size Standards Tool at sba.gov. Standards are expressed as either average annual receipts (e.g., $19 million, $34 million or $47 million) or employee count (e.g., 500, 750 or 1,500 employees) depending on the industry. You certify your size status in SAM.gov.

Can I hold more than one SBA certification at once?

Yes. There's no rule against holding multiple certifications simultaneously, and many businesses do. An SDVOSB can also be 8(a)-certified. A woman-owned business can also qualify as HUBZone. Each certification is assessed and maintained separately with SBA. Holding multiple certifications dramatically expands the set of contracts you can pursue.

What's the 8(a) program's 9-year limit, and how should I plan for it?

The 8(a) program lasts nine years maximum: four in the developmental stage, five in the transitional stage. You can only participate once as an individual owner. During your 8(a) term, aggressively use sole-source awards to build past performance, accumulate CPARS ratings, win IDIQ vehicles that outlast your certification, and develop the commercial relationships and reputation that will sustain you as a full-and-open competitor after your 8(a) graduation. Plan the post-8(a) strategy starting in Year 5 or 6.

What's the difference between a set-aside and a sole-source award?

A set-aside is a competitive procurement limited to businesses in a specific category (e.g., all 8(a) firms, all SDVOSBs). Multiple companies compete and submit proposals. A sole-source award is made to a single company without competition. The government determines that only one company can meet the requirement, or uses a program-specific authority (like the 8(a) program's sole-source threshold). Sole-source awards are faster and eliminate competition entirely, but they're not available for all contract types or values.

How do I find prime contractors to team with?

Start with SBA's SubNet and SBA's Small Business Search (SBS). Research which large primes are winning contracts at your target agencies. USASpending.gov shows award history by agency and contractor. Then approach them directly through their small business liaison offices, which most large contractors maintain specifically to identify SB partners. Industry associations like NDIA (National Defense Industrial Association) and AFCEA also facilitate prime-sub relationships.
