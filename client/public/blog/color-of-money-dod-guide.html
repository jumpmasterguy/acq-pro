Color of Money in DoD: A Plain-English Guide to Defense Appropriations

Not all government dollars are the same — use the wrong one and you're looking at an Anti-Deficiency Act violation. Here's what every appropriation type means and how to stay on the right side of the law.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG & Contractor

Imagine you're a program manager and you have leftover O&M funds burning a hole in your pocket with two weeks left in the fiscal year. Your program needs a new server rack. Can you buy it with O&M? Maybe. Can you buy it with RDT&E? Probably not. Does it matter? Yes — potentially career-endingly so.

In the defense world, money isn't just money. Congress appropriates funds in distinct categories — each with its own authorized purpose, time limit, and legal constraints. Getting the category wrong isn't a paperwork problem. It's a potential violation of the Anti-Deficiency Act (ADA), one of the most serious legal exposures in federal government.

This guide explains every major appropriation type, what you can and can't buy with each, how long the money lasts, and the most common mistakes that trip up program managers and contractors alike.

For the broader defense finance picture — PPBE, EVM, and ADA basics — see our pillar guide: Defense Finance for Program Managers: The Complete Guide.

The Three Iron Rules: Purpose, Time, Amount

Before diving into specific appropriation types, anchor everything to the three fundamental constraints governing all federal spending:

  • Purpose rule: Funds can only be used for the purpose for which they were appropriated. (31 U.S.C. § 1301(a) — the "Appropriations Act Clause" or "purpose statute.")
  • Time rule: Funds expire. You cannot obligate money outside its period of availability.
  • Amount rule: You cannot spend more than was appropriated — not by a dollar.

Color of money violations almost always stem from the purpose rule — using one type of appropriation to pay for something that should have been funded by a different type.

The bona fide needs rule: Beyond the purpose rule, there's also the bona fide needs rule — funds may only be obligated to meet a genuine need that arises in the fiscal year of the appropriation. You can't obligate this year's funds for next year's work, or vice versa.

Operations & Maintenance (O&M): The Workhorse Appropriation

O&M is the most heavily used appropriation in the DoD budget — and the one most frequently misapplied. It funds the day-to-day business of running a military: training exercises, equipment maintenance, base operations, professional services contracts, and sustaining systems that have already been fielded.

Obligation period: 1 year. O&M funds must be obligated before the end of the fiscal year in which they were appropriated. This creates intense end-of-year pressure as program offices rush to obligate remaining O&M balances — often called the "September surge."

The capital threshold issue: One of the most common O&M errors involves buying equipment. The general rule: if an item is a "capital investment" — typically equipment with a unit cost above $250,000 that has an expected useful life of more than two years — it should be Procurement, not O&M. Below that threshold, O&M is generally appropriate. The specifics can vary by service and by policy guidance.

Minor construction vs. MILCON: O&M can fund minor military construction up to $2 million per project (unspecified minor construction). Above that threshold, the project needs MILCON appropriations — which require specific congressional authorization. Using O&M for a $3M construction project is a textbook ADA violation.

Procurement: Buying the Stuff That Gets Delivered

Procurement funds are for buying end items — the actual hardware, systems, and equipment that the military fields and operates. Think aircraft, ships, vehicles, weapons systems, and production-quantity electronics.

Obligation period: 3 years. This longer window reflects the reality that procuring major systems often takes time — from contract award through delivery can span multiple fiscal years.

The production vs. development line: The most common Procurement mistake is using it to fund work that's still in development. If a system hasn't completed developmental testing and isn't ready for production, it should be funded with RDT&E, not Procurement. Using Procurement for development work — or RDT&E for production buys — is a purpose violation.

Practical example: A program buys 10 prototype radios for testing with RDT&E. The testing goes well. The decision is made to buy 500 radios for fielding. That production buy must come from Procurement funds — not from any remaining RDT&E balance, even if technically possible to obligate.

RDT&E: Funding the Work of Discovery

Research, Development, Test and Evaluation funds cover the scientific and engineering work of creating new defense capabilities — from basic research through system development and demonstration. The DoD RDT&E budget is structured into six budget activity (BA) categories, from BA-1 (Basic Research) through BA-6 (Management Support).

Obligation period: 2 years. RDT&E funds can be obligated across two fiscal years, giving programs more time than O&M but less than Procurement.

Key distinctions within RDT&E:

  • BA-1 through BA-3 (Basic Research, Applied Research, Advanced Technology Development) fund early-stage science and technology — often through universities and research labs.
  • BA-4 (Advanced Component Development and Prototypes) is where most prototype and proof-of-concept work happens — the most frequently confused zone between RDT&E and Procurement.
  • BA-5 (System Development and Demonstration) funds the full development of a specific weapon system program.
  • BA-6 (Management Support) and BA-7 (Operational Systems Development) cover management activities and development on already-fielded systems.

MILPERS: The Pay Account

Military Personnel appropriations are straightforward in concept: they fund pay and allowances for active duty military members. Basic pay, housing allowance (BAH), subsistence allowance (BAS), and related benefits all flow through MILPERS.

The key distinction: MILPERS is strictly for uniformed military. Civilian employee pay comes from a separate account (Operations & Maintenance covers most DoD civilian salaries). The two are not interchangeable.

For program managers, MILPERS is rarely a source of color of money problems — it's tightly controlled and generally managed at higher headquarters levels. The more common issue is confusing MILPERS-funded military labor with O&M-funded contractor services when calculating program costs.

MILCON: Building the Base

Military Construction appropriations fund the construction and major improvement of facilities — from new buildings and hangars to base infrastructure upgrades. MILCON has the longest obligation window (5 years) because large construction projects take time to design, award, and complete.

The MILCON authorization requirement: Unlike most other appropriations, every individual MILCON project above the minor threshold must be specifically authorized by Congress in the NDAA and then appropriated in the Defense Appropriations Act. This means a program office can't simply decide to build a new facility — it has to be in the President's Budget, authorized by Congress, and appropriated, all of which takes years.

The minor construction workaround: O&M funds can be used for "unspecified minor construction" up to $2 million per project. This is legitimate but frequently tested — splitting a $4 million project into two $2 million projects to avoid the MILCON threshold is called splitting and is a well-known ADA violation.

Anti-Deficiency Act Consequences: The Stakes Are Real

Color of money violations are generally ADA violations. The consequences aren't theoretical:

  • Mandatory investigation and formal reporting to the President, OMB, and Congress.
  • Administrative action against responsible officials — up to and including removal from federal service.
  • Potential referral to the Department of Justice for willful violations.
  • Public reporting requirement — ADA violations are published in OMB's annual report to Congress.

The most important word is responsible — not just the person who signed the contract, but anyone in the chain who knew or should have known the funds were being misused. Program managers and budget officers share responsibility.

The "just a few thousand dollars" myth: There is no de minimis exception to the ADA. A $5,000 misuse of appropriated funds is just as legally serious as a $5 million one. The investigation and reporting requirements apply regardless of dollar amount.

Practical Color of Money Scenarios

Scenario 1 — The laptop question: A PM needs to buy 50 laptops for the program office. Individual unit cost is $1,500. Total cost is $75,000. Answer: O&M is appropriate (below the capital threshold, service-as-commodity use).

Scenario 2 — The server rack: The program needs a $400,000 server rack for a test lab. It has a 10-year useful life. Answer: Likely Procurement — capital equipment with multi-year useful life above the threshold. Don't use O&M here.

Scenario 3 — The prototype dilemma: RDT&E funds are available, but the program wants to build 50 units of a new component for fielding — not testing. Answer: This needs Procurement funds. Building production-quantity items with RDT&E is a purpose violation, even if the contract could technically be structured as a "development" effort.

Scenario 4 — The maintenance contract expiring: An O&M-funded maintenance contract expires September 30. The program wants to exercise an option in October to cover the next fiscal year. Answer: The option exercise in October must be funded with next year's O&M. You cannot use FY25 O&M to fund FY26 services — that would violate the bona fide needs rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "color of money" mean in DoD?

"Color of money" is informal DoD jargon for the concept that different appropriation types serve different purposes and have different rules. Just as different colors are distinct, O&M, Procurement, RDT&E, MILPERS, and MILCON are legally distinct — using the wrong one for a given purchase is a violation of the purpose statute (31 U.S.C. § 1301(a)) and potentially the Anti-Deficiency Act.

How do I know which appropriation to use for a purchase?

The key questions are: (1) What is the nature of the item — is it research/development, a production end item, a service, personnel pay, or construction? (2) What is the unit cost and expected useful life? (3) Is the item ready for production, or is it still in development? Your contracting officer, budget officer, and legal counsel are your go-to resources when the answer isn't clear. Never obligate funds when there's genuine ambiguity about appropriation type — get it in writing.

Can I move money between appropriation types?

Generally no — not without specific statutory authority. Transfers between appropriation accounts require either a standing transfer authority (provided in law) or a specific reprogramming action approved by Congress. Informally redirecting funds from one appropriation to cover costs belonging to another is a violation. The flexibility that exists is within an appropriation account, not between them.

What is the $250,000 capital threshold rule?

DoD policy generally distinguishes between "expense" items (funded by O&M) and "investment" items (funded by Procurement) based on unit cost and useful life. The threshold has historically been around $250,000, but specific guidance varies by service and is periodically updated. Items above the threshold with useful lives exceeding two years are generally capital investments requiring Procurement funds. When in doubt, check current DoD Financial Management Regulation (FMR) Volume 2A guidance.

What happens to expired O&M funds?

O&M funds expire for new obligations on September 30 of the fiscal year. After expiration, the funds enter a 5-year "expired" status during which they can still be used to adjust prior-year obligations (upward or downward) but not for new obligations. After the 5-year expired period, the account is closed and any remaining balance is cancelled. Cancelled funds cannot be restored — if a contractor submits a valid invoice against a cancelled account, the obligation must be funded from current appropriations.
