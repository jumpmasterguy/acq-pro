Color of Money in DoD: A Plain-English Guide to Defense Appropriations

Not all government dollars are the same. Use the wrong one and you're looking at an Anti-Deficiency Act violation. Here's what every appropriation type means and how to stay on the right side of the law.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG & Contractor

TL;DR. Each type of DoD money has its own purpose, time limit and amount. Use the wrong one and you risk an Anti-Deficiency Act violation, even for small sums.

  • New to this? O&M (1 year) runs operations, RDT&E (2 years) funds development and testing, Procurement (3 years, or 5 for Navy shipbuilding) buys end items, and MILCON (5 years) builds facilities.
  • Government PM? Production quantities need Procurement, even with RDT&E sitting unspent. Don't fund next year's need with this year's money.
  • Planning construction? O&M covers minor construction projects up to $4 million. Splitting a bigger job to duck the cap is a classic, the way "the dog ate my homework" is a classic.

Imagine you're a program manager and you have leftover O&M funds burning a hole in your pocket with two weeks left in the fiscal year. Your program needs a new server rack. Can you buy it with O&M? Maybe. Can you buy it with RDT&E? Probably not. Does it matter? Yes, and potentially career-endingly so.

In the defense world, money isn't just money. Congress appropriates funds in distinct categories, each with its own authorized purpose, time limit, and legal constraints. Getting the category wrong isn't a paperwork problem. It's a potential violation of the Anti-Deficiency Act (ADA), one of the most serious legal exposures in federal government.

This guide explains every major appropriation type, what you can and can't buy with each, how long the money lasts, and the most common mistakes that trip up program managers and contractors alike.

The Three Iron Rules: Purpose, Time, Amount

Before diving into specific appropriation types, anchor everything to the three fundamental constraints governing all federal spending:

  • Purpose rule: Funds can only be used for the purpose for which they were appropriated. (31 U.S.C. § 1301(a): the "Appropriations Act Clause" or "purpose statute.")
  • Time rule: Funds expire. You cannot obligate money outside its period of availability.
  • Amount rule: You cannot spend more than was appropriated. Not by a dollar.

Color of money violations almost always stem from the purpose rule: using one type of appropriation to pay for something that should have been funded by a different type.

The bona fide needs rule: Beyond the purpose rule, there's also the bona fide needs rule: funds may only be obligated to meet a genuine need that arises in the fiscal year of the appropriation. You can't obligate this year's funds for next year's work, or vice versa. One big exception: DoD can use current-year money for a severable service contract that starts this fiscal year and runs no more than 12 months (10 U.S.C. 3133).

Operations & Maintenance (O&M): The Workhorse Appropriation

O&M is the biggest title in the DoD budget ($294.4 billion of the $839.2 billion in the FY2026 defense appropriations act), and one of the easiest to misapply. It funds the day-to-day business of running a military: training exercises, equipment maintenance, base operations, professional services contracts, and sustaining systems that have already been fielded.

Obligation period: 1 year. O&M funds must be obligated before the end of the fiscal year in which they were appropriated. This creates intense end-of-year pressure as program offices rush to obligate remaining O&M balances, often called the "September surge."

The capital threshold issue: One of the most common O&M errors involves buying equipment. The general rule in the DoD Financial Management Regulation (Volume 2A, Chapter 1): equipment with a system unit cost of $250,000 or more is an "investment" and should be bought with Procurement, not O&M. Below that threshold, O&M is generally appropriate. Centrally managed items are investments no matter what they cost. The specifics can vary by service and by policy guidance.

Minor construction vs. MILCON: O&M can fund an unspecified minor military construction project costing up to $4 million (10 U.S.C. 2805(c), raised from $2 million by the FY2024 NDAA). Minor projects up to $9 million can use the lump of MILCON money Congress sets aside for unspecified minor construction. Above $9 million, the project needs its own congressional authorization and MILCON appropriation. Using O&M for a $5 million construction project is a textbook purpose violation, and usually an ADA violation.

Procurement: Buying the Stuff That Gets Delivered

Procurement funds are for buying end items: the actual hardware, systems, and equipment that the military fields and operates. Think aircraft, ships, vehicles, weapons systems, and production-quantity electronics.

Obligation period: 3 years. This longer window reflects the reality that procuring major systems often takes time. From contract award through delivery can span multiple fiscal years.

The production vs. development line: The most common Procurement mistake is using it to fund work that's still in development. If a system hasn't completed developmental testing and isn't ready for production, it should be funded with RDT&E, not Procurement. Using Procurement for development work (or RDT&E for production buys) is a purpose violation.

Practical example: A program buys 10 prototype radios for testing with RDT&E. The testing goes well. The decision is made to buy 500 radios for fielding. That production buy must come from Procurement funds, not from any remaining RDT&E balance, even if technically possible to obligate.

RDT&E: Funding the Work of Discovery

Research, Development, Test and Evaluation funds cover the scientific and engineering work of creating new defense capabilities, from basic research through system development and demonstration. The DoD RDT&E budget is structured into eight budget activities (BAs), from BA-1 (Basic Research) through BA-8 (Software and Digital Technology Pilot Programs).

Obligation period: 2 years. RDT&E funds can be obligated across two fiscal years, giving programs more time than O&M but less than Procurement.

Key distinctions within RDT&E:

  • BA-1 through BA-3 (Basic Research, Applied Research, Advanced Technology Development) fund early-stage science and technology, often through universities and research labs.
  • BA-4 (Advanced Component Development and Prototypes) is where most prototype and proof-of-concept work happens, the most frequently confused zone between RDT&E and Procurement.
  • BA-5 (System Development and Demonstration) funds the full development of a specific weapon system program.
  • BA-6 (RDT&E Management Support) and BA-7 (Operational System Development) cover management activities and development on already-fielded systems.
  • BA-8 (Software and Digital Technology Pilot Programs) funds a small set of software programs under a pilot.

MILPERS: The Pay Account

Military Personnel appropriations are simple in concept: they fund pay and allowances for military members. Active duty, Reserve and National Guard pay each come from their own personnel accounts. Basic pay, housing allowance (BAH), subsistence allowance (BAS), and related benefits all flow through MILPERS.

The key distinction: MILPERS is strictly for uniformed military. Civilian employee pay comes from a separate account (Operations & Maintenance covers most DoD civilian salaries). The two are not interchangeable.

For program managers, MILPERS is rarely a source of color of money problems. It's tightly controlled and generally managed at higher headquarters levels. The more common issue is confusing MILPERS-funded military labor with O&M-funded contractor services when calculating program costs.

MILCON: Building the Base

Military Construction appropriations fund the construction and major improvement of facilities, from new buildings and hangars to base infrastructure upgrades. MILCON has one of the longest obligation windows (5 years, the same as Navy shipbuilding) because large construction projects take time to design, award, and complete.

The MILCON authorization requirement: Unlike most other appropriations, every individual MILCON project above the $9 million unspecified minor construction ceiling must be specifically authorized by Congress in the NDAA and then appropriated in the Defense Appropriations Act. This means a program office can't simply decide to build a new facility. It has to be in the President's Budget, authorized by Congress, and appropriated, all of which takes years.

The minor construction workaround: O&M funds can be used for "unspecified minor construction" up to $4 million per project. This is legitimate but frequently tested. Splitting an $8 million project into two $4 million projects to stay under the O&M limit is called project splitting. It's prohibited, and it's a well-known path to an ADA violation.

Anti-Deficiency Act Consequences: The Stakes Are Real

A color of money mistake becomes an ADA violation when the correct appropriation doesn't have enough money available to fix it, and many do. The consequences aren't theoretical:

  • Mandatory investigation and an immediate report to the President and Congress, with a copy to GAO (31 U.S.C. 1351).
  • Administrative action against responsible officials, up to and including suspension without pay or removal from federal service.
  • Criminal penalties for knowing and willful violations: a fine of up to $5,000, up to two years in prison, or both (31 U.S.C. 1350).
  • Public reporting: GAO publishes annual compilations of the ADA violation reports agencies send it.

The most important word is responsible. Investigators look past the person who signed the contract to whoever's decisions caused the violation, so program managers and budget officers can be named too.

The "just a few thousand dollars" myth: There is no de minimis exception to the ADA. A $5,000 misuse of appropriated funds is just as legally serious as a $5 million one. The investigation and reporting requirements apply regardless of dollar amount.

Practical Color of Money Scenarios

Scenario 1. The laptop question: A PM needs to buy 50 laptops for the program office. Individual unit cost is $1,500. Total cost is $75,000. Answer: O&M is appropriate (well below the $250,000 threshold, assuming the laptops aren't centrally managed items).

Scenario 2. The server rack: The program needs a $400,000 server rack for a test lab. It has a 10-year useful life. Answer: Likely Procurement: capital equipment with multi-year useful life above the threshold. Don't use O&M here.

Scenario 3. The prototype dilemma: RDT&E funds are available, but the program wants to build 50 units of a new component for fielding, not testing. Answer: This needs Procurement funds. Building production-quantity items with RDT&E is a purpose violation, even if the contract could technically be structured as a "development" effort.

Scenario 4. The maintenance contract expiring: An O&M-funded maintenance contract expires September 30. The program wants to exercise an option in October to cover the next fiscal year. Answer: The option exercise in October must be funded with next year's O&M. You cannot use FY25 O&M to fund FY26 services. That would violate the bona fide needs rule. (The severable services exception doesn't save you here: it lets a service period that starts in FY25 run up to 12 months on FY25 money, but an October option starts a new period in FY26.)

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "color of money" mean in DoD?

"Color of money" is informal DoD jargon for the concept that different appropriation types serve different purposes and have different rules. Just as different colors are distinct, O&M, Procurement, RDT&E, MILPERS, and MILCON are legally distinct. Using the wrong one for a given purchase is a violation of the purpose statute (31 U.S.C. § 1301(a)) and potentially the Anti-Deficiency Act.

How do I know which appropriation to use for a purchase?

The key questions are: (1) What is the nature of the item? Is it research/development, a production end item, a service, personnel pay, or construction? (2) What is the unit cost and expected useful life? (3) Is the item ready for production, or is it still in development? Your contracting officer, budget officer, and legal counsel are your go-to resources when the answer isn't clear. Never obligate funds when there's genuine ambiguity about appropriation type. Get it in writing.

Can I move money between appropriation types?

Generally no, not without specific statutory authority. Transfers between appropriation accounts require either a standing transfer authority (provided in law) or a specific reprogramming action approved by Congress. Informally redirecting funds from one appropriation to cover costs belonging to another is a violation. The flexibility that exists is within an appropriation account, not between them, and even that is capped: for FY2026, a below-threshold reprogramming can move no more than $15 million or 20 percent of a line item, whichever is less, without congressional approval (FY2026 joint explanatory statement).

What is the $250,000 capital threshold rule?

DoD policy distinguishes between "expense" items (funded by O&M) and "investment" items (funded by Procurement). The DoD Financial Management Regulation (FMR) Volume 2A, Chapter 1 sets the expense/investment threshold at a system unit cost of $250,000: at or above it is an investment, below it is generally an expense. Centrally managed items are investments regardless of cost. Service guidance adds detail and can change, so check the current FMR and your comptroller before you buy.

What happens to expired O&M funds?

O&M funds expire for new obligations on September 30 of the fiscal year. After expiration, the funds enter a 5-year "expired" status during which they can still be used to adjust prior-year obligations (upward or downward) but not for new obligations. After the 5-year expired period, the account is closed and any remaining balance is cancelled. Cancelled funds cannot be restored. If a contractor submits a valid invoice against a cancelled account, it must be paid from current appropriations available for the same purpose, and those charges can't exceed 1 percent of the current appropriation (31 U.S.C. 1553(b)).
