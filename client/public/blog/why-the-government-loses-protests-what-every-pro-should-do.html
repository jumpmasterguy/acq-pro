Why the Government Loses Protests & What Every Pro Should Do

The GAO's 51% effectiveness rate demands action. Here's how to navigate common pitfalls and secure your next defense acquisition.

By Acqlerate · May 2026 · 7 min read · For: USG & Contractor
The landscape of U.S. defense acquisition is unforgiving. Every year, billions of dollars in critical programs are stalled, re-evaluated, or re-awarded due to bid protests. While the Government Accountability Office (GAO) might only sustain about 14% of protests that go to a full decision, that number doesn't tell the whole story. The more critical statistic for 2025-2026? The **51% "effectiveness rate."** This means in over half of all protest cases, the agency either voluntarily took corrective action or the GAO found in favor of the protestor. That's a massive disruption, and it underscores a consistent truth: many protests have legitimate grounds. For both government Program Managers (PMs) and defense contractors, understanding *why* the government loses these protests is crucial for success.

The Top Three Reasons Agencies Lose Bid Protests

Based on recent GAO decisions and ongoing trends, three core issues consistently lead to sustained protests or agency corrective action. These aren't new problems, but their persistence highlights fundamental vulnerabilities in the acquisition process.

1. Flawed Technical Evaluations & Unreasonable Rejections

**The Problem:** Agencies frequently struggle to adequately document and justify their technical evaluation decisions, especially in complex procurements with multiple factors. This often leads to inconsistent application of solicitation criteria, introduction of unstated requirements, or a failure to reasonably consider the merits of a proposal. A common scenario involves rejecting a proposal as "unacceptable" without clear, defensible evidence that it failed to meet a mandatory requirement, or applying a subjective standard not outlined in the solicitation. **So What?** * **For USG PMs:** Your evaluation teams must be rigorously trained and calibrated. Every finding, every strength, weakness, deficiency, or significant weakness, needs clear, traceable documentation back to the solicitation's specific requirements. If you're rejecting a proposal, the rationale must be bulletproof and directly linked to a stated mandatory criterion. Don't introduce unstated evaluation factors. * **For Contractors:** Meticulously cross-reference every element of your proposal against *every single* solicitation requirement and evaluation criterion. Assume the agency will evaluate strictly. If your proposal is rejected, demand a comprehensive debriefing to understand the exact rationale. Look for inconsistencies with the solicitation or unstated criteria.

The 51% Effectiveness Rate is Your Compass: Don't get fixated on the 14% sustainment rate. The fact that agencies take corrective action in over half of all protest cases means legitimate issues are frequently raised. This is why thoroughness and compliance are non-negotiable for both sides.

2. Inadequate Organizational Conflict of Interest (OCI) Analysis

**The Problem:** As defense contractors increasingly offer a broad spectrum of services—from system design to testing and sustainment—the potential for OCIs proliferates. Agencies often fail to conduct sufficiently thorough OCI analyses, or they inadequately mitigate identified conflicts. This is particularly true with "other transaction" (OT) agreements, where the perception of less regulatory oversight can lead to lax OCI reviews. The GAO consistently finds that agencies either didn't investigate a potential OCI adequately or their mitigation plan was unreasonable. **So What?** * **For USG PMs:** OCI analysis isn't a checkbox exercise; it's a critical risk mitigation activity. Dedicate resources to a robust OCI review early in the acquisition lifecycle. Engage legal counsel. Document your findings, your analysis of potential conflicts (e.g., unequal access to information, impaired objectivity, biased ground rules), and any proposed mitigation strategies meticulously. * **For Contractors:** Proactively identify and disclose any potential OCIs in your proposals, along with a comprehensive mitigation plan. This demonstrates your commitment to ethical conduct and transparency. If you're protesting, an unmitigated OCI, especially one involving an unequal access to information or an unfair competitive advantage, can be a very strong ground.

3. Unreasonable Cost/Price Evaluations

**The Problem:** In a budget-constrained environment, agencies are under pressure to demonstrate "best value." However, their cost realism analyses for cost-reimbursement contracts, or price reasonableness determinations for fixed-price contracts, are frequently found wanting. This can manifest as accepting an unrealistically low price without adequate justification (increasing performance risk) or failing to properly analyze whether proposed costs are realistic for the work described. **So What?** * **For USG PMs:** Ensure your cost/price analysis teams are well-trained and empowered. Their rationale for accepting or rejecting prices must be thoroughly documented, defensible, and consistent with the solicitation's evaluation criteria. For cost-reimbursement contracts, a robust cost realism analysis is non-negotiable. For fixed-price, be ready to defend how you determined a price was reasonable and not indicative of performance risk. * **For Contractors:** Submit realistic, well-substantiated pricing. For cost-reimbursement, be prepared to justify every element of your cost proposal. For fixed-price, ensure your price is competitive but also executable. If you're protesting, look for signs that the awardee's price was unrealistically low and that the agency's evaluation of that price was perfunctory or illogical.

Navigating New Protest Battlegrounds: Policy Shifts in 2025-2026

The acquisition landscape is dynamic, with new policies continually introducing fresh grounds for protest. Staying ahead requires understanding these evolving requirements. * **CMMC 2.0 Implementation:** As the DoD's Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification 2.0 becomes more prevalent in solicitations throughout 2025 and 2026, expect protests challenging agency evaluations of compliance. * **So What?** Contractors must prioritize achieving and demonstrating CMMC compliance. Agencies must ensure their evaluation of CMMC requirements is consistent, well-documented, and strictly adheres to the solicitation. * **NDAA Provisions (Small Business, SCRM, IP):** Annual National Defense Authorization Acts frequently introduce new policy directives. Continued emphasis on small business contracting can lead to protests challenging size status or agency set-aside decisions. Heightened focus on Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) for critical technologies will introduce new evaluation factors that agencies must clearly define and evaluate. Ongoing debates around Intellectual Property (IP) rights could lead to protests if agencies misapply or misinterpret IP clauses. * **So What?** Both sides must stay current with NDAA impacts. Contractors need to understand new small business requirements, supply chain mandates, and IP clauses. Agencies must clearly translate these policy directives into unambiguous solicitation language and evaluation criteria. * **FAR Council Updates (Sustainable Acquisition, Domestic Preference):** The FAR Council's continuous updates, such as new requirements for evaluating environmental impacts or stricter enforcement of domestic preference rules (Buy American Act/Berry Amendment), will introduce new compliance hurdles. * **So What?** Agencies must consistently apply and document these new evaluation criteria. Contractors must ensure their proposals demonstrate compliance with these evolving regulatory demands.

Your Playbook: Proactive Strategies for Success

Avoiding or successfully navigating a bid protest requires vigilance and a commitment to acquisition rigor from both sides. Here's a comparative playbook:
Acquisition Area For USG Program Managers (PMs) For Defense Contractors
Solicitations & Proposals Draft clear, unambiguous solicitations with well-defined evaluation criteria. Avoid unstated requirements. Meticulously review your proposal against *every* solicitation requirement. Assume strict evaluation.
Evaluation & Documentation Document *everything*—every finding, every strength/weakness, every decision. Stick strictly to stated criteria. Ensure your proposal clearly addresses all evaluation criteria. Document your compliance.
OCI Management Conduct thorough OCI analyses early. Document findings and mitigation strategies meticulously. Engage legal counsel. Proactively identify and disclose potential OCIs with robust mitigation plans.
Cost/Price Analysis Ensure cost/price analysis teams are trained. Document defensible rationale for accepting/rejecting prices. Submit realistic, well-substantiated pricing. Be prepared to justify all costs.
Debriefings Provide comprehensive, candid, and consistent debriefings. This often averts protests. Always request a debriefing, even if not protesting. Use it to understand agency rationale and improve.
Legal Engagement Engage legal counsel early and often. Provide regular protest trends training to acquisition teams. Engage experienced procurement counsel if considering a protest or defending an award.
Compliance Ensure consistent application of new policies (CMMC, SCRM, etc.) in solicitations and evaluations. Stay current with evolving requirements (CMMC 2.0, SCRM, IP, sustainable acquisition). Non-compliance is a weak spot.

Conclusion

The GAO bid protest system isn't just a hurdle; it's a vital mechanism for ensuring fairness, transparency, and integrity in federal procurement. In 2025-2026, with intense competition, complex technical requirements, and evolving policy landscapes, the potential for sustained protests remains constant. The high "effectiveness rate" of protests should serve as a stark reminder that agencies frequently make errors that warrant corrective action. For both government PMs and defense contractors, the path forward is clear: prioritize meticulous documentation, clear communication, unwavering adherence to procurement regulations, and proactive engagement with evolving policies. By understanding the common reasons protests are sustained and implementing these practical strategies, acquisition professionals can minimize risk, avoid costly delays, and ultimately contribute to more successful program execution. The shadow of the sustained protest is real, but an informed, rigorous approach can navigate its challenges effectively.
