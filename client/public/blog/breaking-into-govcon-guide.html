Breaking Into GovCon: The Complete Guide for Defense Contractors

How to build a real business — or a real career — in defense contracting, from your first SAM.gov registration to your first contract win.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 14 min read · For: Contractor

The U.S. federal government is the single largest buyer of goods and services on the planet, spending over $700 billion annually — with more than $400 billion flowing through the Department of Defense (DoD) alone. If you're thinking about breaking into government contracting, you're looking at a market that never dries up, rarely recessions out, and actively sets aside a significant slice of its budget for smaller competitors.

But GovCon — government contracting slang for the whole ecosystem — is not a market you stumble into. It has its own rules, its own language, and its own rhythms. This guide gives you the plain-English roadmap: why it's worth pursuing, how the BD-to-capture lifecycle actually works, what certifications matter, and how to land your first realistic win.

Why GovCon Is a Stable Career and Business Path

Let's start with the "why" before the "how."

Demand is structural, not cyclical. DoD programs operate on multi-year budgets written years in advance. Unlike commercial clients who cut spending when Q3 misses projections, government agencies have legally appropriated funds they must obligate. A contract awarded today might generate revenue for five to ten years.

Competition is rule-bound and transparent. When the government needs something, it publishes a solicitation on SAM.gov (the System for Award Management — the federal government's official contracting portal). Anyone with the right qualifications can compete. The evaluation criteria are written in the request for proposal (RFP) itself. You can see exactly what they'll score you on before you write a single word.

Small businesses get a mandated slice. Congress requires federal agencies to award at least 23% of all prime contract dollars to small businesses. The DoD runs its own small business programs on top of that. That means there are contracts literally set aside — available only to companies like yours — that the Lockheed Martins of the world cannot touch.

Past performance compounds. Once you win a contract and perform well, you get a formal record of that performance in the government's system (more on CPARS later). That record becomes a competitive asset in every future proposal. The longer you're in the game, the stronger your position.

Realistic timeline: The DoD's own guidance says plan for at least 18 months from first registration to first contract award. Most new entrants win their first award between 18 and 36 months in. Budget your runway accordingly — this is a long game with durable rewards.

The BD-to-Capture Lifecycle: Your High-Level Roadmap

Government contracting has a structured pursuit process that separates successful contractors from the ones constantly chasing RFPs and losing. The lifecycle looks like this:

  1. Business Development (BD): Market intelligence, relationship-building, and pipeline management. You identify opportunities 12–36 months before they hit the street.
  2. Capture: Once you've qualified an opportunity, you position yourself to win it before the RFP drops — influencing requirements, understanding the customer's priorities, shaping your solution.
  3. Proposal: You respond to the formal RFP. This is the part most people think of, but if you haven't done the first two stages, you're writing a losing proposal.
  4. Negotiation and Award: The government evaluates proposals, may conduct discussions, and selects a winner.
  5. Performance: You execute — and build the past performance record that feeds your next pursuit.

The key insight: you win or lose before the RFP drops. By the time a solicitation is published, the best-positioned competitors have been shaping it for 18 months. If you're writing your first contact with the customer in your proposal, you're already behind.

Proposal Development Fundamentals

When an RFP drops, your proposal is your product. Here's the architecture you need to understand:

Section L — Instructions to Offerors: This section tells you exactly how to structure your proposal — volumes, page limits, font requirements, what to include where. Non-compliance here gets you thrown out before evaluation even begins.

Section M — Evaluation Factors for Award: This section tells you how the government will score your proposal. It lists the evaluation factors (Technical Approach, Past Performance, Price, Management Approach, etc.) in order of importance. This is the bible you write to.

The cardinal mistake new contractors make: they write a proposal that talks about themselves instead of mapping directly to what Section M asks for. Evaluators can only score what they can find. If your discriminator isn't directly linked to an evaluation factor, it doesn't count.

How to Build Past Performance From Zero

Past performance is the chicken-and-egg problem of GovCon: you need it to win contracts, but you need contracts to build it. Here's how to break the cycle:

Start with subcontracting. Large prime contractors are required to establish subcontracting plans on contracts over $750,000 (simplified acquisition threshold). Position yourself as a sub on a prime's team. You'll do real work, get paid, and accumulate performance references — even if the prime holds the prime contract.

Pursue small dollar awards first. Micro-purchases (under $15,000) and simplified acquisitions (under $350,000) have fewer barriers to entry. Target contracting officers at agencies aligned with your core capabilities and offer to be their go-to for smaller needs.

Use Other Transaction Authority (OTA). OTAs are non-FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) agreements that DoD uses for prototype and research projects. They're faster and have fewer compliance requirements — a popular path for technology companies new to GovCon.

Leverage mentor-protégé programs. The SBA Mentor-Protégé Program pairs you with a larger, experienced contractor. Your mentor can bring you in as a subcontractor on their prime contracts, directly helping you build past performance and learn the business.

Formal performance records: Once your contract exceeds certain dollar thresholds (generally $2M for services), the government documents your performance in CPARS — the Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System. Those ratings, ranging from Unsatisfactory to Exceptional, follow you for three to six years and appear in every source selection. Treat every contract, no matter how small, as an audition. Key Certifications: DAWIA, PMP, and APMP

Certifications signal competence to government clients and evaluators — especially when you're positioning key personnel in proposals. Here's what actually matters on the contractor side:

Project Management Professional (PMP)

Issued by the Project Management Institute (PMI), the PMP is the most widely recognized project management credential in the world — and the one government evaluators and contracting officers most frequently see referenced in proposals. Requirements: 36 months of project leadership experience (60 months without a four-year degree), 35 hours of PM education, and a passing score on the exam. A PMP on your key personnel roster directly strengthens your past performance and management approach scores.

APMP Certification (Association of Proposal Management Professionals)

APMP certifications — Foundation, Practitioner, and Professional — are the industry standard for capture and proposal professionals. If your team is going to write competitive proposals, APMP training teaches you the Shipley methodology, compliance matrix management, color review processes, and proposal management discipline. Foundation level is approachable for new practitioners; Practitioner and Professional signal serious expertise.

DAWIA (Defense Acquisition Workforce Improvement Act)

DAWIA certifications — now organized into six functional areas under the Back-to-Basics initiative — are primarily for government employees in acquisition coded positions. However, contractors deeply benefit from understanding the DAWIA framework because it defines how your government customers think about their jobs. If you speak their language — understanding what a Program Manager (PM), Contracting Officer (CO), or Contracting Officer's Representative (COR) is responsible for — you will be a far more effective BD and capture professional. Many contractor employees also complete DAU (Defense Acquisition University) open courses at no cost to build this literacy.

Other Credentials Worth Pursuing

  • Certified Federal Contracts Manager (CFCM) — from NCMA (National Contract Management Association). Strong for contracts professionals.
  • Certified Professional Contracts Manager (CPCM) — senior NCMA credential for experienced practitioners.
  • Certified Defense Financial Manager (CDFM) — relevant for cost/pricing roles and finance-heavy pursuits.

Realistic Timelines and First Wins

Here's an honest sequencing of what breaking into GovCon actually looks like for most contractors:

Months 1–3: Get procurement-ready. Register in SAM.gov. Obtain a DUNS number (now replaced by the Unique Entity Identifier, or UEI). File your NAICS codes — the North American Industry Classification System codes that define what your business does. Get on the GSA (General Services Administration) Multiple Award Schedule (MAS) if you sell commercial products or services; it's an evergreen on-ramp to federal buyers. Work with your local APEX Accelerator (formerly PTAC — Procurement Technical Assistance Center) — a free resource funded by the DoD to help small businesses win federal contracts.

Months 3–12: Build market intelligence. Use SAM.gov, USASpending.gov, and tools like GovWin IQ or Bloomberg Government to understand who buys what you sell, which agencies are your targets, and when their current contracts expire (because that's when new opportunities emerge). Attend industry days, matchmaking events, and AFCEA (Armed Forces Communications and Electronics Association) or NDIA (National Defense Industrial Association) events. Start meeting program offices and contracting officers.

Months 6–18: Pursue first awards. Target subcontracting first. Respond to RFIs (Requests for Information) — they don't yield contracts, but they get you on the government's radar. Bid on smaller, simplified acquisitions where the barriers are lower. Win your first contract. Perform well. Get your first CPARS rating.

Months 18–36+: Leverage past performance. Use your first contract as the foundation for your next pursuit. Apply for small business certifications that match your ownership profile. Qualify for IDIQ (Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity) contract vehicles. Start positioning on larger opportunities 12–18 months out.

The 80/20 rule: The top BD and capture teams apply an 80/20 rule to their pipeline — they invest 80% of their bid and proposal (B&P) dollars on the 20% of opportunities where they've done real capture work and have a pWin (probability of win) above 50%. Chasing every RFP that drops is a recipe for a low win rate and a burned-out proposal team.

Building a GovCon Team That Can Win

You cannot win government contracts as a solo effort. Here are the core roles every competitive contractor needs:

  • Business Development (BD) Director: Owns pipeline, customer relationships, and opportunity identification. Works 12–36 months ahead of proposals.
  • Capture Manager: Takes over a specific opportunity once qualified and drives the win strategy. Responsible for competitive intelligence, teaming decisions, and customer shaping.
  • Proposal Manager: Runs the proposal from kickoff to submission — timeline, compliance, color reviews, and production.
  • Pricing/Cost Analyst: Builds the price-to-win (PTW) model and cost proposal. Ensures your price is competitive without leaving money on the table or triggering cost realism concerns.
  • Subject Matter Experts (SMEs): The technical and programmatic staff who write the content. They know the work; the proposal team teaches them to write to evaluators.

For small businesses, these roles often overlap. A BD Director might also serve as Capture Manager. The proposal manager might be a part-time consultant. What matters is that someone owns each function — especially the bridge between BD intel and proposal strategy.

Common First-Timer Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

  1. Bidding on everything. Being selective is a competitive advantage. Every proposal costs you real money in staff time. Bid where you've done capture work and your pWin is real.
  2. Starting at the RFP. By RFP drop, the competitive landscape is set. If you haven't met the program office, you don't know what they actually want.
  3. Writing a capabilities brochure, not a proposal. Government evaluators score against Section M. Write directly to the factors and subfactors. Prove everything.
  4. Ignoring price. The government uses price competition more than many contractors expect. Understand your competitors' cost structures. Build a price-to-win model.
  5. Weak past performance write-ups. CPARS ratings are inputs; how you present them is an output. Match past performance to the scope, scale, and complexity of what you're bidding. A $500K contract does not prove you can manage a $50M program.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to win your first government contract?

The DoD's own guidance recommends planning for at least 18 months from initial registration to first award. Most small businesses win their first contract between 18 and 36 months after starting the process — assuming they're actively pursuing opportunities, not just waiting for RFPs to drop. Companies that invest in BD and customer relationship-building tend to win sooner.

Do I need a security clearance to do defense contracting?

Not necessarily — many DoD contracts involve unclassified work. However, larger programs and higher-value work often require cleared personnel. Facility clearances (for the company) and individual clearances are processed through the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA). You can sponsor employees for clearances once you hold a contract requiring them, or hire cleared personnel from the start. Having cleared staff is a competitive differentiator for classified work.

What's the difference between a prime contractor and a subcontractor?

A prime contractor holds the contract directly with the government and bears full responsibility for contract performance. A subcontractor works under the prime on a specific portion of the work. For new entrants, subcontracting is a common first step — you gain past performance, learn the customer, and build relationships without carrying the full compliance burden of a prime contract. Eventually, most small businesses aim to become primes because prime contracts are more profitable and build stronger past performance records.

Is SAM.gov registration really required?

Yes — it is legally required for any business receiving federal contract awards or grants. SAM.gov registration must be active and renewed annually. Your Unique Entity Identifier (UEI), NAICS codes, and representations and certifications are all maintained in SAM. An expired or incomplete SAM registration can disqualify an otherwise winning proposal.

What certifications are most valuable for a defense contractor's key personnel?

For project management roles: the PMP (Project Management Professional) from PMI is the gold standard and is frequently required or preferred in solicitations. For capture and proposal staff: APMP certifications show evaluators that your team has professional proposal management discipline. Technical staff may benefit from Lean Six Sigma or relevant engineering credentials depending on the domain. DAU (Defense Acquisition University) open courses are free and build the acquisition literacy that makes all of these roles more effective.
