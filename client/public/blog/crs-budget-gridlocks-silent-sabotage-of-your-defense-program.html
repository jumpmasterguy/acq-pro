CRs: Budget Gridlock's Silent Sabotage of Your Defense Program

Continuing resolutions are more than a budget hiccup; they're a systemic drain on modernization and a strategic gift to adversaries under FY2026's tight caps.

By Acqlerate · June 2026 · 8 min read · For: USG Personnel
As USG personnel, you're on the front lines of delivering critical capabilities to our warfighters. You understand the complexities of defense acquisition, from requirements generation to contract execution. But there's a recurring, insidious threat that often flies under the radar until it hits your program directly: continuing resolutions (CRs). Far from a mere administrative delay, these stop-gap funding measures are a systemic drain on modernization, a strategic gift to adversaries, and a persistent headache for program managers and industry partners alike. The cumulative effect of years of fiscal uncertainty is now threatening to undermine critical national security priorities, especially as we look towards Fiscal Year 2026 and navigate the strict budget caps imposed by recent legislation. It's time to understand why budget gridlock is one of the biggest hidden risks to your defense program's future.

The Looming Shadow of Fiscal Limbo

For years, the Department of Defense (DoD) has operated in a state of "predictable unpredictability" when it comes to funding. The U.S. government has functioned under at least one continuing resolution for 15 of the last 17 fiscal years. This isn't just a pattern; it's the new normal. The primary driver of the anticipated FY2026 CR environment is the **Fiscal Responsibility Act (FRA) of 2023**. This bipartisan agreement, signed into law on June 3, 2023, set statutory spending caps for defense and non-defense discretionary spending through FY2025. For defense, the caps are approximately $895 billion for FY2025 and $909 billion for FY2026. While these represent modest growth, they are significantly below what many defense experts and the Pentagon's own strategic assessments (like the National Defense Strategy) suggest is necessary to counter pacing threats from China and Russia, and to recapitalize an aging force.

So what? CRs aren't just administrative delays; they are a direct threat to our national security goals, forcing the DoD to prioritize existing programs at the expense of critical modernization. For you, this means your carefully planned program could face significant disruption, regardless of its strategic importance.

A History of Disruption, A Future of Uncertainty

To understand FY2026, it's helpful to look at recent history. The FY2024 experience provides a stark illustration of the problem: * **FY2024 (October 1, 2023 - March 23, 2024):** The DoD endured a protracted CR period stretching over five months, requiring multiple extensions. A full appropriation wasn't signed until March 23, 2024—nearly half the fiscal year gone. * **Practical Implication:** For nearly half the fiscal year, DoD operated at FY2023 funding levels, with severe restrictions on new program starts, production rate increases, and multi-year procurement contracts. Critical modernization efforts were deferred. This sets a dangerous precedent for FY2025 and, critically, for FY2026. The narrow margins for negotiation under the FRA caps, coupled with persistent partisan divides, make the passage of on-time, full-year appropriations bills for FY2026 highly improbable. The following table highlights the stark difference between operating under a full appropriation versus a CR:
Acquisition Area Under Full Appropriation Under Continuing Resolution (CR)
New Program Starts Funding available for approved new initiatives. Prohibited. New programs or significant expansions cannot begin.
Production Rates Can increase production rates as planned. Frozen. Production remains at prior year's level, regardless of need.

So what? This isn't just about delayed paperwork; it's about delayed capabilities. Your program, even if deemed critical, will operate with one hand tied behind its back for potentially months, directly impacting timelines and costs.

How CRs Cripple Your Defense Program

The practical implications of prolonged CRs for defense acquisition programs are severe and multifaceted, impacting every stage from conceptualization to delivery:

  • Delayed "New Starts": CRs typically prohibit "new start" programs. This means any innovative, critical program identified in the FY2025 or FY2026 budget requests—particularly those addressing emerging threats or technologies like next-generation AI, advanced hypersonics, or space-based capabilities—cannot begin until a full appropriation is passed. Imagine the Next-Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) family of systems or new Joint All-Domain Command and Control (JADC2) initiatives facing these delays; crucial fielding dates are pushed back, and overall costs inevitably rise.

  • Inefficient Production Ramps and Rate Increases: If your program is slated for a production increase (e.g., more F-35s, Sentinel ICBMs, or Columbia-class submarines), a CR prevents this ramp-up. The Sentinel (GBSD) ICBM replacement program, a critical modernization effort, depends on consistent funding to scale up. A CR would force lower, less efficient production rates, driving up unit costs and extending the fielding timeline. Similarly, Columbia-class submarine construction, vital for strategic deterrence, faces disrupted supply chains and workforce planning, potentially adding billions to its lifecycle cost.

  • Stifled Research, Development, Test, and Evaluation (RDT&E): Innovation is inherently forward-looking. CRs force R&D efforts to operate on old priorities and funding levels, hindering agile development and the ability to pivot to new threats. Funding for advanced materials, quantum computing, or directed energy weapons often resides in RDT&E accounts. CRs limit the ability to fund promising new avenues of research or expand existing projects, potentially leading to a "brain drain" as top researchers seek more stable environments.

  • Supply Chain Instability and Increased Costs: Contractors, especially smaller businesses, rely on predictable funding. CRs create uncertainty, making it difficult to plan for material purchases, workforce hiring, and capital investments. Suppliers may delay orders, leading to longer lead times and higher prices when funding finally arrives. Some may exit the defense market entirely, further consolidating the industrial base and reducing competition, particularly for specialized components in areas like microelectronics or rare earth materials.

So what? These aren't abstract budget issues. They translate directly into delayed capabilities for the warfighter, increased costs for the taxpayer, and a strategic disadvantage against our adversaries. Your program's effectiveness is directly linked to timely, stable funding.

Navigating the Gridlock: Your Action Plan

While you can't control congressional budget decisions, you can proactively mitigate the impact of CRs on your program.

For Defense Program Managers (PMs):

  • Scenario Planning: Develop detailed contingency plans for extended CRs. Prioritize activities that *can* proceed, identify those that *must* be delayed, and document the associated risks and costs for each scenario.
  • Communication is Key: Maintain constant, transparent communication with all stakeholders—leadership, industry partners, and congressional staff. Manage expectations and clearly articulate the tangible impacts of CRs on your program's progress and national security goals.
  • Flexibility and Agility: Be prepared to re-baseline schedules, re-allocate resources, and potentially adjust program scope if full funding is delayed. This often means carrying higher levels of risk and having pre-approved alternative pathways.
  • Contract Management: Focus on managing existing contracts within CR limitations. Avoid new obligations or commitments that cannot be funded. Carefully track potential claims from contractors due to stop-work orders or delays, ensuring proper documentation.

For Defense Contractors:

  • Cash Flow Management: Prepare for potential payment delays or reduced order volumes. Diversify revenue streams where possible to reduce sole reliance on defense contracts during periods of uncertainty.
  • Workforce Planning: Exercise caution in hiring, particularly for new programs. Develop strategies to retain skilled personnel during uncertainty, as potential furloughs or reduced hours can lead to a critical loss of talent.
  • Bid & Proposal Strategy: Factor in the high probability of CRs into future bid proposals. Include potential cost escalation clauses or flexible delivery schedules. Be wary of bidding on "new start" programs without a clear path to full appropriation.
  • Supply Chain Resilience: Work closely with sub-tier suppliers to understand their capacity and financial health under CR scenarios. Consider holding buffer stock for critical components, though recognize the associated costs.
  • Advocacy: Engage with industry associations and directly with congressional offices to highlight the tangible, negative impacts of CRs on jobs, innovation, and national security.

So what? Proactive planning and clear communication are your best defenses against CR disruptions. By anticipating the problem, you can build resilience into your program and minimize the damage when gridlock inevitably strikes.

Beyond the Budget: A National Security Imperative

The outlook for defense acquisition in FY2026 is one of continued fiscal austerity compounded by the high likelihood of disruptive continuing resolutions. The Fiscal Responsibility Act has set the spending caps, but it has not solved the underlying political dysfunction that leads to these stop-gap measures. For defense acquisition professionals and the industrial base that supports them, this means operating in a persistent state of uncertainty, where strategic long-term planning is constantly undermined by short-term fiscal battles. The cost is not just measured in dollars; it's measured in lost opportunities, delayed capabilities, and a widening gap between the threats we face and the tools we have to counter them. Addressing this predictable unpredictability is no longer just a budgeting issue; it is a critical national security imperative. Understanding the risks and preparing for them is a fundamental part of your mission to deliver advanced, ready capabilities to those who defend our nation.

So what? Your role in navigating CRs is more than just managing a budget; it's about safeguarding our national security. By proactively preparing, you contribute directly to the resilience and effectiveness of our defense enterprise.
