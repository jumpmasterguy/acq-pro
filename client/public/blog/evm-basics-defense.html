EVM for Beginners: Earned Value Management in Plain English

Planned Value, Earned Value, Actual Cost — and the ratios that tell you whether a defense program is heading toward disaster. Here's everything beginners need to know about EVM.

By Acqlerate · February 2026 · 9 min read · For: USG Personnel

Imagine hiring a contractor to build a house. After three months, you've paid $150,000. The contractor says they've completed 40% of the work. The original plan called for 50% completion at this point. Is the project on track?

You can't answer that question just by looking at the check you wrote. You need to compare what you planned to accomplish, what you actually accomplished, and what you actually spent — all at the same point in time.

That's Earned Value Management. It's not complicated in concept. But in a defense program worth hundreds of millions — or billions — of dollars, getting EVM right is the difference between early warning and a painful surprise three years and a billion dollars too late.

This guide explains EVM from the ground up: what it measures, how to calculate the key metrics, what the thresholds mean, and what DCMA does when your numbers go red.

For the broader context — how EVM fits into defense finance, PPBE, and appropriations — see: Defense Finance for Program Managers: The Complete Guide.

What Is EVM and Why Does DoD Require It?

Earned Value Management (EVM) is a project management methodology that integrates three dimensions of project performance — scope, schedule, and cost — into a single measurement framework. Instead of just asking "how much have we spent?" EVM asks "how much have we accomplished, and what should that have cost?"

DoD requires EVM on contracts above certain thresholds because it provides an objective, early-warning indicator of program performance. A program that's only 20% complete but already showing cost overruns will almost certainly finish over budget — EVM surfaces that signal early, when corrective action is still possible.

Legal basis: DFARS 252.234-7002 requires contractors to establish and maintain an Earned Value Management System (EVMS) that complies with ANSI/EIA-748 guidelines on contracts that meet the thresholds below:

  • Cost-reimbursement (CR) contracts over $50 million
  • Fixed-price incentive (FPI) contracts over $20 million (development contracts)
  • Cost-plus-award-fee (CPAF) and other development contracts above these thresholds

Program managers may request EVM on contracts below these thresholds when program complexity or risk warrants it.

The Three Core EVM Values

Everything in EVM starts with three numbers. Get these right and the rest flows naturally.

  • Planned Value (PV): Also called the Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS). This is what you planned to accomplish by now, expressed in budget dollars. It's your baseline plan.
  • Earned Value (EV): Also called the Budgeted Cost of Work Performed (BCWP). This is what you actually accomplished, measured in the budgeted amount for that work — not what it actually cost. It's your accomplishment score.
  • Actual Cost (AC): Also called the Actual Cost of Work Performed (ACWP). This is what you actually spent to accomplish the work you've earned. It's your spending record.

Critical distinction: EV measures accomplishment in budget dollars — not actual dollars. If you planned to spend $100K to complete a task and you completed it, your EV is $100K — regardless of whether you spent $80K or $120K doing it. The variance comes from comparing EV to AC.

The Key EVM Metrics — Full Comparison Table

Metric Formula What It Measures Red Flag Threshold
Cost Variance (CV) EV − AC Are you over or under budget for work accomplished? Negative = over budget. CV < 0 (any negative value indicates overrun)
Schedule Variance (SV) EV − PV Are you ahead or behind schedule in dollar terms? Negative = behind schedule. SV < 0 (negative means behind plan)
Cost Performance Index (CPI) EV ÷ AC How efficiently are you spending? CPI = 1.0 means on budget; <1.0 means overrunning. CPI < 0.90 (DCMA concern threshold)
Schedule Performance Index (SPI) EV ÷ PV How efficiently are you progressing? SPI = 1.0 means on schedule; <1.0 means behind. SPI < 0.90
Variance at Completion (VAC) BAC − EAC How much over or under budget do you expect to finish? Negative = projected overrun. VAC < 0 (any projected overrun)
Estimate at Completion (EAC) AC + ETC (several formulas) What is the total program expected to cost when complete? EAC > BAC signals cost growth
To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI) (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) What CPI must you achieve on remaining work to finish within the original budget? >1.0 means you'd need to outperform your current rate. TCPI > 1.10 (likely unrealistic recovery)

A Plain-English Example

Let's say you have a software development contract with a total budget (BAC — Budget at Completion) of $10 million, planned to take 12 months.

At the 6-month mark:

  • PV: $5 million (you planned to be 50% done)
  • EV: $4 million (you've actually completed work worth $4M of the budget)
  • AC: $5 million (you've spent $5M to accomplish that $4M of work)

Now the metrics:

  • CPI = EV ÷ AC = $4M ÷ $5M = 0.80 — for every dollar you spend, you're getting 80 cents of work done. You're spending 25% more than planned per unit of work.
  • SPI = EV ÷ PV = $4M ÷ $5M = 0.80 — you've accomplished 80% of what you planned to accomplish by now. You're behind schedule.
  • EAC (using CPI formula) = BAC ÷ CPI = $10M ÷ 0.80 = $12.5 million — if you continue at the same efficiency, the program will cost $12.5M instead of $10M.
  • VAC = BAC − EAC = $10M − $12.5M = −$2.5 million — you're projecting a $2.5M overrun.

This program is in trouble — and you've surfaced that fact at the halfway point, not at 90% complete.

The 20% rule: Research by the DoD and independent analysts consistently shows that programs with a CPI below 0.90 at the 20% complete mark rarely recover to finish within their original budget. This is why DCMA — and your program office — pays close attention to early EVM signals.

The Budget at Completion and Performance Measurement Baseline

Two foundational terms in any EVM conversation:

  • Budget at Completion (BAC): The total authorized budget for the contract — the sum of all budgets for all work scope. This is the denominator in many EVM calculations.
  • Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB): The time-phased plan against which performance is measured. The PMB is built from the contractor's Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) and shows how the budget is planned to be earned over time. It's the source of your PV at any given reporting period.

The Five Contract Performance Report (CPR) Formats

Contractors on EVM-required contracts submit monthly Contract Performance Reports (CPRs) — the primary EVM reporting mechanism. The CPR has five formats, each covering a different dimension of performance:

  • Format 1 — WBS: Cost/schedule performance by Work Breakdown Structure element. Shows where cost and schedule variances are occurring by work package.
  • Format 2 — Organizational Categories: Performance broken down by contractor organizational unit (e.g., engineering, manufacturing, test). Useful for identifying which organizations are driving overruns.
  • Format 3 — Baseline: Changes to the Performance Measurement Baseline over time. Tracks Baseline Change Requests (BCRs) and Management Reserve (MR) usage.
  • Format 4 — Staffing: Actual vs. planned staffing levels by labor category. Helps identify whether schedule problems stem from staffing shortfalls.
  • Format 5 — Problem Analysis: A narrative explanation of significant variances — typically for any cost or schedule variance exceeding a defined threshold (e.g., $500K or 10% of the control account budget). This is where the contractor explains what went wrong and what they're doing about it.

DCMA's Role: The External EVM Watchdog

The Defense Contract Management Agency (DCMA) is responsible for EVM surveillance on behalf of the government. DCMA analysts are assigned to major defense programs and contractors and perform several EVM-related functions:

  • EVMS compliance reviews: Before a contractor can use EVM reporting on a DoD contract, DCMA must validate that the contractor's EVMS meets ANSI/EIA-748 guidelines. This involves an Integrated Baseline Review (IBR) — a deep dive into the PMB to ensure it's realistic and traceable to the contract scope.
  • Monthly surveillance: DCMA analysts review monthly CPR submissions, run their own analysis, and flag concerns to the program office.
  • Integrated Baseline Reviews (IBR): Typically conducted within 6 months of contract award, the IBR is a joint government-contractor review of the PMB to ensure mutual understanding of scope, schedule, and budget.
  • Corrective action requests: If DCMA identifies systemic EVM problems — like contractors manipulating EV without corresponding work accomplishment — they can issue corrective action requests and recommend decertification of the EVMS.

For more on DCMA's broader contract administration mission, see: DCAA vs. DCMA: What's the Difference and Why You Need to Know.

Common EVM Misconceptions

"EVM is just for contractors." Wrong. Government program managers are responsible for understanding and interpreting EVM data. If you can't read a CPR, you can't effectively manage your program's cost and schedule baseline.

"Good EVM means a healthy program." Not necessarily. A contractor can report technically compliant EVM while a program is in serious trouble — if the Performance Measurement Baseline was poorly constructed or scope was inadequately defined, EVM reports accurately what's happening against a flawed plan.

"Schedule Variance catches all schedule problems." SPI is measured in dollar-weighted schedule progress, not actual calendar time. A program can show a positive SPI while being behind schedule on critical path items. Always supplement EVM schedule data with Integrated Master Schedule (IMS) analysis.

"A CPI of 1.0 means the program is fine." CPI of exactly 1.0 can sometimes indicate EVM gaming — "managing to 1.0" by adjusting EV claims to match spending. Look for anomalously stable CPI over time as a potential red flag.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does EVM stand for and why does DoD use it?

EVM stands for Earned Value Management. DoD requires it on large contracts because it provides an objective, early-warning signal of cost and schedule performance by integrating scope, schedule, and cost into a single framework. Without EVM, a program can appear to be on budget simply because the money hasn't been spent yet — not because the work is actually on track.

What is the difference between CPI and SPI?

The Cost Performance Index (CPI) measures how efficiently the program is spending money relative to work accomplished: CPI = EV ÷ AC. A CPI below 1.0 means you're spending more than planned per unit of work. The Schedule Performance Index (SPI) measures how efficiently the program is progressing relative to the plan: SPI = EV ÷ PV. An SPI below 1.0 means less work has been accomplished than planned. A program can have a healthy CPI but a poor SPI, or vice versa.

When is EVM required on DoD contracts?

DFARS 252.234-7002 requires EVM compliance on cost-reimbursement contracts over $50 million and on fixed-price development contracts over $20 million. The contracting officer may also require EVM on lower-dollar contracts when risk or complexity warrants it. A validated, ANSI/EIA-748-compliant EVM System (EVMS) is required from the contractor.

What is an Integrated Baseline Review (IBR)?

An IBR is a joint government-contractor review of the Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB), typically conducted within 6 months of contract award. The goal is to verify that the PMB accurately reflects the contract scope, is technically achievable, and is properly resourced. DCMA facilitates and oversees IBRs on major programs. An IBR finding that the baseline is unrealistic is an early warning sign of future EVM problems.

What is a Contract Performance Report (CPR) and how often is it submitted?

A CPR is the primary EVM reporting document submitted by contractors to the government, typically monthly. It has five formats covering WBS-level performance, organizational performance, baseline changes, staffing, and problem analysis. The CPR is the key input for DCMA surveillance and program management reviews. Reviewing and understanding CPR data is a core skill for government program managers on EVM-required contracts.
