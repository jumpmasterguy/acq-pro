Why Your Contract Revenue Might Be Half What It Should Be

Acqlerate  ·  June 2025  ·  5 min read

Two companies. Same $10 million contract. One of them books $10 million in revenue. The other books $500,000.

Same contract. Completely different financial picture. And the company booking $500K didn't do anything wrong — they just don't know why it happened, or how to fix it on the next one.

This is one of the most misunderstood concepts in defense contracting finance. Most PMs have never heard of it. And it's sitting right there in your Statement of Work.

There Are Two Kinds of Defense Contractors (Financially Speaking)

Here's the split. Under ASC 606 — that's the accounting standard that governs how companies recognize revenue — every company on a contract falls into one of two categories:

You're either a Principal, or you're an Agent.

A Principal owns the delivery. They're the ones responsible for making the work happen, adding technical value, and bearing the risk if things go sideways. They book the full contract value as revenue.

An Agent is a pass-through. They coordinate, they facilitate, they manage the flow — but they're not really the ones doing the technical work. They only book their fee.

Quick Math $10M contract. $500K management fee.
Principal books: $10,000,000
Agent books: $500,000
Same contract. 20x difference on paper.

Why This Actually Matters to You

You might be thinking — okay, but the money is the same either way. The contract pays what it pays.

True. But revenue on paper isn't just about cash flow. It's about how your company looks.

Investors, lenders, bonding companies, and even future government contract evaluators look at your top-line revenue. A company with $10M in revenue looks very different from one with $500K — even if the actual profit margin is the same. It affects your bonding capacity, your ability to secure financing, your attractiveness as an acquisition target, and how you stack up in competitive bidding situations where past performance or company size matters.

For a small or mid-size GovCon firm, that distinction can be the difference between graduating to the next tier of contracts or staying stuck.

The SOW Is Where It Gets Decided

Here's where program managers have real influence — and most of them don't know it.

The language in your Statement of Work (SOW) is what determines whether you're a Principal or an Agent. Not the contract type. Not your company size. The specific words describing what you're actually doing.

If your SOW reads like a "hardware dump" — you're acquiring equipment, passing it to the government, and your role is procurement logistics — you're probably an Agent. You're facilitating a purchase, not adding technical value.

If your SOW reads like an engineering or integration contract — you're doing systems integration, hardening, testing, or technical development — you're probably a Principal. You own the outcome. You bear the risk. You add value.

Same underlying work can be described two different ways. One version makes you an Agent. The other makes you a Principal. That's not creative accounting — that's just writing the SOW to accurately reflect the value you're actually delivering.

Most PMs Write SOWs Without Thinking About This

They focus on the technical requirements. The deliverables. The timeline. That's their job and they're good at it.

But no one ever told them that the framing of what they're doing — the language they use to describe their role — has real financial consequences for the company. That a few sentences can shift millions of dollars from "fee line" to "top-line revenue."

Once you see it, you can't unsee it. And you start reading every SOW differently.
