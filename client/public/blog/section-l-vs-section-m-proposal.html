Section L vs. Section M: The RFP Anatomy Every Defense Contractor Must Master

Most proposals get these two sections backwards — and lose because of it. Here's what they actually are and how to use them to win.

By Acqlerate · March 2026 · 8 min read · For: Contractor

When a Request for Proposal (RFP) lands in your inbox, most contractors do the same thing: they scan the Statement of Work (SOW — the description of what work is required), look at the deadline, and start writing. That approach produces mediocre proposals and predictable losses.

The contractors who win consistently start somewhere else entirely — with two sections that sit at the back of most RFPs and that most people skim or skip: Section L and Section M.

This article is a companion to: Breaking Into GovCon: The Complete Guide for Defense Contractors.

What Is Section L?

Section L — formally titled "Instructions, Conditions, and Notices to Offerors" — is the rulebook for your proposal. It tells you:

  • How many volumes to submit (Technical, Management, Past Performance, Price/Cost, etc.)
  • Page limits for each volume and section
  • Font size, margin requirements, and file format
  • The submission deadline and delivery method
  • What specific content each volume must contain
  • Any required attachments, certifications, or forms
  • Questions and clarification procedures

Think of Section L as the application form. If the form asks for a birth certificate and you submit a driver's license, your application is rejected — not because your answer was wrong, but because you didn't follow instructions.

In government contracting, non-compliance with Section L can get your proposal eliminated before a single evaluator reads it. Contracting Officers (COs) — the government officials with legal authority to award contracts — are empowered to reject non-compliant proposals for administrative reasons. And some do, because every rejected proposal lightens the evaluation workload.

What Is Section M?

Section M — "Evaluation Factors for Award" — is the scoring guide. It tells you:

  • The evaluation factors the government will use to compare proposals (e.g., Technical Approach, Past Performance, Management Approach, Price)
  • The relative importance of each factor (e.g., "Technical is more important than Past Performance, which is more important than Price")
  • Subfactors within each factor and how they're weighted
  • The adjectival rating scale (Outstanding/Very Good/Acceptable/Marginal/Unacceptable, or equivalent)
  • Whether this is a Best Value Tradeoff (BVTO) or Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA) acquisition

Section M is the evaluator's job description. Every member of the Source Selection Evaluation Board (SSEB) — the team of government subject matter experts who read and score proposals — is limited by regulation to evaluating only what's described in Section M. They cannot give you credit for things they personally know about your company, or for capabilities that appear in your proposal but aren't mapped to an evaluation factor.

The critical insight: Evaluators can only score what they can find, and they can only find what maps to Section M. If you write a brilliant technical narrative that doesn't speak to the specific evaluation factors and subfactors in Section M, that brilliance is invisible to the scoring process. Write Section M first — then build Section L compliance around it.

Why Most Proposals Get It Backwards

The most common mistake in proposal writing is building the proposal around the Statement of Work (SOW) rather than around Section M. It makes intuitive sense — the SOW describes the work, so you describe how you'll do the work. But the SOW is not what evaluators score. Section M is.

The result is a proposal that's technically accurate but evaluatively invisible. You describe your approach beautifully, but evaluators can't find the specific proof points they need to assign a rating of Outstanding versus Very Good. You lose not because you're less capable — but because your competitor made it easier for evaluators to score them.

A second common error: organizing the proposal around Section M instead of Section L. This one's subtler and rarer, but it happens. Some contractors are so focused on Section M that they restructure their proposal to match the evaluation factors rather than the volume/section structure specified in Section L. This produces a compliant evaluation experience but a non-compliant proposal — and risks rejection at the administrative review.

The correct approach: structure to Section L, write to Section M.

How Evaluators Actually Score Proposals

Understanding the SSEB's process makes you a better proposal writer. Here's what actually happens:

  1. Compliance review: The CO or a designated reviewer checks that the proposal meets all Section L requirements — correct volumes, within page limits, required forms included. Non-compliant proposals may be eliminated here.
  2. Factor-by-factor evaluation: Each SSEB member is assigned specific evaluation factors. They read the relevant sections, looking for evidence of strengths, weaknesses, and deficiencies as defined in the solicitation and the FAR Part 15 framework.
  3. Consensus rating: SSEB members share findings, discuss evidence, and assign adjectival ratings to each factor and subfactor.
  4. Source Selection Authority (SSA) review: The SSA — a senior official, often the Program Manager — reviews the SSEB's findings, conducts a best value tradeoff analysis, and makes the final award decision.

Strengths, Weaknesses, and Deficiencies — Defined

Under FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) Part 15, the official definitions matter:

  • Strength: An aspect of an offeror's proposal that has merit or exceeds specified performance or capability requirements in a way that will be advantageous to the Government during contract performance.
  • Significant Strength: An aspect that has especially important merit or exceeds specified performance or capability requirements in a way that will be considerably advantageous to the Government during contract performance.
  • Weakness: A flaw in the proposal that increases the risk of unsuccessful contract performance.
  • Significant Weakness: A flaw that appreciably increases the risk of unsuccessful contract performance.
  • Deficiency: A material failure of a proposal to meet a Government requirement or a combination of significant weaknesses in a proposal that increases the risk of unsuccessful contract performance to an unacceptable level.

Your goal as a proposal writer is to give evaluators documented, specific reasons to record Strengths — especially Significant Strengths — for every major evaluation factor. Vague claims produce no strengths. Specific, evidence-backed claims that exceed requirements produce the adjectival ratings that win.

The Proposal Mapping Framework

Use this framework to align every section of your proposal before you write a word:

Section M Factor / Subfactor Section L Instruction (Volume/Section) Your Approach / Discriminator Evidence / Proof Point
Technical Approach — Understanding of Requirements Volume I, Section 1 — Technical Narrative (15 pages max) Demonstrate deep domain knowledge by referencing the customer's specific environment (e.g., named systems, locations, org structure from your capture work) Reference prior performance on Contract #XYZ performing identical tasks; cite specific technical metrics you delivered
Technical Approach — Proposed Solution Volume I, Section 2 — Methodology (10 pages max) Introduce a proprietary process, toolset, or framework that exceeds the minimum requirement and directly addresses the program office's known pain points Name the tool, quantify the improvement (e.g., "reduced reporting cycle time by 40% on Contract ABC"), include graphics showing the workflow
Management Approach — Key Personnel Volume II, Section 1 — Management Plan (12 pages); Attachment A — Resumes Lead PM is PMP-certified with 10 years managing analogous DoD programs; has existing relationships with the program office Resume demonstrates clearance, relevant past programs, specific deliverables; include letters of commitment
Past Performance — Relevance and Quality Volume III — Past Performance (5 references max, 2 pages each) Select references that match the RFP's scope, dollar value, and complexity; lead with your strongest CPARS-rated contract Include CPARS ratings (Outstanding or Very Good), dollar values, period of performance, and a narrative directly mapping to this RFP's requirements
Price / Cost Realism Volume IV — Price/Cost (separate from page limits) Price competitively based on your price-to-win (PTW) model; ensure your cost narrative supports the technical approach Labor mix, hours, and rates must be consistent with the technical volume; inconsistencies between technical and cost volumes are a red flag for evaluators

Build this mapping table in your proposal kickoff package. Every section owner should know exactly which Section M factor they're writing to — and every claim they make should be evidence-backed enough to support a documented Strength.

How to Write Discriminating Strengths

A discriminating strength is a proposal claim so specific, well-evidenced, and directly mapped to an evaluation factor that an evaluator cannot help but document it as a Strength in the source selection record.

Here's the difference between a weak claim and a discriminating strength:

Weak: "Our team has extensive experience supporting DoD programs and delivering high-quality results."

Discriminating Strength: "Our Program Manager, [Name], PMP, held the same role on Contract No. W912HQ-21-C-0043 with [Agency], a $12M logistics support contract of identical scope. That contract received an Outstanding CPARS rating for Technical Quality and an On-Time Delivery rate of 99.2% across 847 deliverables. We will apply the same [proprietary methodology name] workflow, which reduced scheduling conflicts by 35% and eliminated two of the three types of deficiencies noted in the prior contractor's final evaluation."

Notice what the discriminating strength does: it is specific (contract number, dollar value, named person), quantified (99.2%, 35%, 847), mapped to an evaluation factor (quality, schedule), and directly contrasts with the incumbent's performance. An evaluator reading that has everything they need to write a Strength finding.

What the SSEB looks for: Evaluators are busy subject matter experts reading stacks of proposals under tight deadlines. They are not hunting for reasons to give you strengths — they're looking for documented evidence that justifies a rating. Make their job easy. Use headers that echo Section M language. Bullet evidence. Quantify everything. A proposal that is easy to evaluate scores higher, all else equal, because evaluators find the proof points faster.

LPTA vs. Best Value Tradeoff — Choosing Your Strategy

Section M will tell you the source selection methodology:

LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable): The government will award to the lowest-priced proposal that meets all technical requirements. There's no credit for exceeding requirements. Your strategy here: meet every requirement precisely, don't over-propose, and compete aggressively on price.

Best Value Tradeoff (BVTO): The government will trade off technical merit against price. A higher-priced proposal can win if its technical advantages justify the premium. Your strategy here: create Significant Strengths that are so valuable to the government that paying more for them is clearly in their interest — and document that value explicitly.

Most professional services and complex technical contracts use BVTO. Most commodity procurements use LPTA. Know which you're in before you build your proposal strategy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I organize my proposal around Section M instead of Section L?

No — and this is a common trap. Section L defines the required structure (volumes, sections, page limits). Section M defines the evaluation criteria. Your proposal must be structured per Section L to be compliant. However, every word you write should be crafted to generate Strengths under the factors described in Section M. The skill is doing both simultaneously: compliant structure, persuasive content.

What happens if I exceed the page limits in Section L?

In most cases, evaluators are instructed to stop reading at the page limit. Any content beyond the limit is simply not evaluated — the hours your team spent writing it are wasted. Some solicitations are stricter and may reject over-limit proposals entirely. Always track page counts and cut ruthlessly if needed. A tight, well-argued 15 pages beats a meandering 20-page narrative every time.

How much weight does Past Performance typically carry in source selections?

It varies by solicitation, but Past Performance is typically the second-most-important factor after Technical Approach in competitive acquisitions. In many DoD solicitations, Past Performance and Technical together outweigh Price. An Outstanding CPARS record on relevant contracts is one of the strongest competitive differentiators available — especially for small businesses trying to unseat larger incumbents. What's the difference between a Weakness and a Deficiency?

A Weakness is a flaw that increases performance risk — it's negative, but doesn't necessarily make your proposal unacceptable. A Deficiency is a failure to meet a stated requirement, or a combination of significant weaknesses so severe that the risk becomes unacceptable. Deficiencies typically result in a rating of Unacceptable and disqualification from award. During discussions (if the government opens them), you'll get the chance to address both through an Evaluation Notice (EN) process.

Should I reference the Section M factors by name in my proposal?

Yes — deliberately and explicitly. Using the government's own language from Section M in your proposal headers and opening sentences helps evaluators find your evidence quickly. It signals that you've read the solicitation carefully and are writing directly to their criteria. Some proposal managers use parenthetical references ("see Section M, Factor 1, Subfactor a") to make the mapping explicit. This is not pandering — it's making the evaluator's job easier, which earns you higher scores.
