OTAs in 2026: What the $15B Surge Means for Your Defense Business

The DoD's reliance on Other Transaction Authority is intensifying, with projected spending reaching $15 billion annually by 2026, reshaping how contractors engage with the Pentagon.

By Acqlerate · April 2026 · 8 min read · For: Contractor

The New Normal: OTAs are No Longer "Other" in 2026

As we stand in April 2026, the term "Other Transaction Authority" (OTA) has shed its "other" status and firmly cemented itself as a core acquisition strategy for the Department of Defense. This isn't just a trend; it's the new normal. For defense contractors, understanding and adapting to this reality isn't optional – it's crucial for future success.

The numbers tell a clear story: DoD OTA obligations, which were less than $2 billion in FY2015, surged past $10 billion in FY2023. Looking back at FY2025 and projecting through FY2026, analysts confirm this upward trajectory, with **annual OTA prototype spending expected to hit between $12 billion and $15 billion.** This isn't just growth; it's an acceleration driven by strategic imperatives like the Replicator Initiative, which demands rapid fielding of thousands of autonomous systems within tight 18-24 month windows. OTAs are the vehicle making that speed possible.

The focus areas for this spending surge are precisely where the DoD needs cutting-edge capabilities, and fast:

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI) & Machine Learning (ML): From decision support to autonomous operations.
  • Autonomous Systems: Drones, robotics, and swarming capabilities across all domains.
  • Cybersecurity: Advanced threat detection and resilient networks.
  • Advanced Materials & Manufacturing: Hypersonics, 3D printing for rapid spares.
  • Space-based Capabilities: Small sats, on-orbit servicing, and advanced payloads.

So what for contractors? If your business operates in these critical technology areas, OTAs are no longer a niche or experimental pathway; they are a primary, high-velocity channel for DoD engagement and funding. Ignoring them means missing out on significant opportunities. You need to understand their mechanics, embrace their flexibility, and be ready to move quickly.

The Double-Edged Sword: Opportunity Meets Oversight

While the surge in OTA spending presents immense opportunities, it also brings increased scrutiny. As OTAs transition from an experimental tool to a bedrock component of the DoD's Middle Tier of Acquisition (MTA), accountability and transparency are paramount. Congress and oversight bodies like the Government Accountability Office (GAO) are demanding more robust metrics and clearer justification for OTA usage.

Recent years have seen a consistent push for better oversight. The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) included provisions aimed at improving data collection and reporting on OTA outcomes, particularly regarding the transition of prototypes to production. Expect further refinements in the upcoming FY2025 and FY2026 NDAAs, likely pushing for enhanced data collection, improved oversight mechanisms, and potentially stricter criteria for production OTA awards following prototypes.

A significant challenge remains the "valley of death" – the gap between successful prototyping and fielding at scale. The DoD is actively working to bridge this, emphasizing prototype OTAs with clear production pathways. This means:

  • Earlier engagement with traditional primes: Integrating larger defense contractors into OTA consortia or as follow-on partners.
  • Improved data rights negotiation: Ensuring the government has sufficient data rights to transition a prototype to a different vendor for production if necessary.
  • Formalized transition plans: Requiring detailed plans within the prototype agreement itself, outlining milestones for moving from prototype to a follow-on production OTA or FAR-based contract (10 U.S.C. § 4022(f)).

Key Insight: The DoD isn't just looking for cool tech anymore; it's looking for cool tech that can actually be put into the hands of warfighters at scale. Your prototype's pathway to production is now as critical as the prototype itself.

So what for contractors? Be prepared for greater scrutiny on your OTA proposals and performance. You need to demonstrate not just technical prowess but also a clear, viable plan for how your prototype can transition to sustained production. This includes a robust data rights strategy that protects your IP while still allowing the government the necessary flexibility for follow-on production. Don't just build a great prototype; build a great prototype with a future.

Navigating the OTA Landscape: Consortia, IP, and Agility

The primary gateway for many contractors into the OTA world, particularly for non-traditional and small businesses, continues to be the consortium model. Organizations like the National Armaments Consortium or the National Advanced Mobility Consortium provide a streamlined entry point, reducing administrative burdens for both government and industry. By 2026, these consortia are even more critical for accessing the diverse technological base required for initiatives like Replicator.

Intellectual Property (IP) remains a cornerstone, and often a sticking point, of OTA negotiations. Unlike FAR-based contracts with their often rigid IP clauses, OTAs offer more flexibility, allowing for tailored agreements. However, this flexibility demands sophistication. Contractors must protect their core IP while simultaneously being willing to negotiate government purpose rights or limited rights for specific applications to facilitate DoD adoption. A misstep here can either undermine your competitive advantage or prevent your technology from being adopted.

Finally, agility isn't just a buzzword in the OTA space; it's a fundamental requirement. OTAs are designed for rapid prototyping, iteration, and quick turnarounds. This means contractors must be prepared for evolving requirements, frequent communication, and a less bureaucratic, more collaborative approach than traditional FAR contracts. The ability to pivot quickly based on testing and feedback is paramount.

So what for contractors? If you're a non-traditional vendor, **joining relevant consortia is non-negotiable.** For all contractors, invest in understanding IP negotiation strategies specific to OTAs. Don't assume FAR IP clauses apply. Cultivate an agile internal culture that can respond rapidly to DoD needs and iterative development cycles. The speed of the OTA process demands a corresponding speed from your organization.

What This Means for Your Business: A Contractor's Playbook

The shift towards OTAs impacts different types of contractors in distinct ways. Understanding these nuances is key to crafting a winning strategy.

Factor Traditional Primes Non-Traditional Vendors
Primary Engagement Often as integrators or follow-on production partners; increasingly via consortia. Primarily through consortia; direct engagement with DIU, AFWERX, AFC.
Value Proposition Scaling successful prototypes, systems integration, manufacturing expertise, established DoD relationships. Cutting-edge technology, commercial innovation, speed of development, cost-effectiveness.
IP Strategy Adapt from FAR-centric to more flexible OTA negotiation; focus on integrating third-party IP. Protect core commercial IP while offering necessary government purpose/limited rights for DoD use.
Operational Tempo Must adapt to faster prototyping cycles and less formal processes. Leverage existing commercial agility; adapt to DoD-specific requirements and security needs.
"So What" Action Actively seek partnerships with non-traditionals; join consortia; position as the "bridge" to production. Join relevant consortia, clearly articulate DoD problem-solving, be IP savvy, and stay agile.

Consider recent examples that illustrate these trends:

  • DIU's Prowess: DIU continues to lead in OTA usage. A December 2023 prototype OTA with X-Bow Systems for advanced warhead technology, valued up to $60 million, exemplifies how OTAs are used for critical modernization with an expectation of rapid maturation and potential production by late 2025/early 2026. This is a clear signal for non-traditionals developing disruptive tech.
  • AFWERX's Agility Prime: Following successful flight demonstrations from 2023-2024 by companies like Joby Aviation and Archer Aviation under prototype OTAs for eVTOL aircraft, the Air Force is expected to issue follow-on prototype or initial production OTAs by mid-2025, potentially in the tens of millions of dollars per vendor. This shows the transition path in action.
  • Army Futures Command (AFC) Modernization: In January 2024, the Army awarded a prototype OTA to Anduril Industries for its Lattice AI platform to enhance JADC2 capabilities, with an initial value of $25 million. This prototype is slated for integration into Project Convergence exercises in 2025, with potential for rapid expansion via follow-on OTAs if successful. This highlights the speed and scale possible for innovative solutions.

So what for contractors? For non-traditional vendors, your commercial innovation and agility are your superpowers. Leverage consortia, articulate your solutions to specific DoD problems, and be smart about IP. For traditional primes, your role is evolving. Embrace collaboration with smaller, innovative firms. Position yourselves as integrators, manufacturing experts, and the reliable "bridge" that can take a successful prototype to full-rate production under a follow-on OTA or FAR contract. Both types of contractors must adapt to a less bureaucratic, more outcomes-focused environment.

Staying Ahead: Policy Shifts and Future Focus

The policy landscape around OTAs is not static. The FY2024 NDAA, signed in December 2023, reinforced the importance of improving data collection and reporting on OTA outcomes, particularly regarding the transition of prototypes to production. It also reiterated the importance of competition and fair opportunity when awarding follow-on production OTAs. This signals a continued push for more rigorous justification and accountability.

Furthermore, expect refinements to DoD Instruction 5000.80 ("Operation of the Middle Tier of Acquisition (MTA)") in 2025. These updates will likely provide clearer guidance on program management, risk assessment, and intellectual property considerations specific to OTA prototypes within the rapid acquisition framework. The DoD's renewed emphasis on robust commercial item determinations for all acquisition pathways, including OTAs where applicable, will also impact how vendors structure their proposals and how the government evaluates the "commerciality" of solutions offered.

So what for contractors? Staying informed about these policy changes isn't just about compliance; it's about strategic positioning. Understand how updated reporting requirements might affect your proposals. Keep an eye on evolving guidance for MTA programs, as this is where OTAs primarily live. Be prepared to clearly articulate the commerciality of your offerings, even within the flexible OTA framework. The future of defense acquisitions in 2026 and beyond is fast, flexible, and increasingly focused on measurable outcomes and clear pathways to scaling innovation.
