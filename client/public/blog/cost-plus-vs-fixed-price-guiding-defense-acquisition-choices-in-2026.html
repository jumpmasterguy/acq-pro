Cost-Plus vs. Fixed-Price: Guiding Defense Acquisition Choices in 2026

The contract type determines who absorbs cost risk, how your fee works, and what the government will scrutinize. Here's what you actually need to know — for both sides of the table.

By Acqlerate · April 2026 · 8 min read · For: USG & Contractor

Every contract you work on has a type. That type determines who absorbs cost risk, how your fee is calculated, and what the government will scrutinize. If you don't know which type you're on and why — that's the first thing to fix.

The choice between cost-plus and fixed-price isn't paperwork. It's a decision about who takes the risk if things go sideways. DoD has a strong preference for fixed-price, and it's not arbitrary — it's about shifting financial exposure to the contractor and creating an incentive to manage costs. But that preference has limits. When nobody knows what something will cost because nobody's built it before, forcing a firm price doesn't reduce risk. It just hides it until the program blows up.

Here's what you need to know about each type, when they're used, and what they mean for you in practice.

The core difference: who absorbs cost risk

Fixed-Price (FFP): You agree to deliver a defined product or service for a specific price. The government pays that price. If your costs run over, you eat the difference. If you come in under, you keep the savings. The government knows exactly what it's spending. You have every incentive to run lean.

Cost-Plus-Fixed-Fee (CPFF): The government reimburses your actual allowable costs, plus pays you a pre-negotiated fixed fee (your profit). That fee doesn't change whether you come in over or under. The government bears the cost risk. You have less financial pressure to cut costs — which is fine when nobody can accurately estimate them anyway.

Everything else follows from this. The oversight requirements, the audit exposure, the accounting system requirements, the modification process — all of it flows from who's holding the risk.

When fixed-price is the right call

FFP works when the scope is clear enough to price. If you know what you want, when you want it, and you have enough market data to know what it should cost — fixed price gives you the best value. The contractor has skin in the game. Efficiency is rewarded directly.

This is the default for production runs, sustainment, and services with well-defined performance metrics. Recurring support contracts, logistics, staffing augmentation — if you've done it before and you know what it costs, FFP is the answer.

The government's push for FFP has been consistent since Better Buying Power and it's not going away. The pressure to transition from cost-plus to fixed-price as programs mature is real. If you're on a CPFF development contract, your PM is already thinking about when to make that transition.

When cost-plus is the right call

CPFF exists for a reason. When you're developing technology nobody has built before, writing a firm price is fiction. You're not actually reducing risk — you're just making the contractor guess and then pad the estimate to survive. The government ends up paying more through scope creep, modifications, and eventual restructuring than it would have paid for a well-managed cost-plus contract.

CPFF is appropriate when:

  • Requirements are genuinely undefined — early-stage design, software where user feedback shapes scope, systems integration where nobody knows what the interdependencies will cost
  • Technology isn't mature — if TRL is below 6, you probably can't price it reliably
  • The government needs flexibility to pivot — R&D programs where the right answer isn't known at contract award
  • There's urgency — sometimes getting the right contractor moving fast matters more than achieving perfect pricing

The tradeoff: cost-plus means more oversight, more accounting scrutiny, and DCAA in your books. That's not a punishment — it's the deal. The government took on your cost risk. They get to verify the costs.

The contract types side by side

Contract Type Who Bears Cost Risk Use When Contractor's Deal
FFP — Firm Fixed Price Contractor Well-defined scope, mature tech, predictable costs Full profit if efficient. Full loss if you estimated wrong.
FPIF — Fixed Price Incentive Fee Shared up to ceiling Moderate risk, want to incentivize efficiency Share in savings if you come in under target cost
CPFF — Cost Plus Fixed Fee Government High tech risk, R&D, unclear requirements Fixed fee regardless of cost. Low upside, low downside. DCAA is your shadow.
CPIF — Cost Plus Incentive Fee Shared via share ratio High risk with measurable performance outcomes More fee if you hit targets. Less if you miss.
T&M — Time & Materials Mostly government Uncertain labor hours, services without defined scope Billed at ceiling rates. Government sets a not-to-exceed.

What this means for contractor PMs specifically

If you're a contractor PM on a fixed-price contract, your job is cost control. Every dollar over your internal estimate comes out of your fee. You have zero incentive to accept scope without a mod. You have every incentive to buy materials efficiently, staff to the right level, and track burn rate daily. A PM who doesn't know their task order ceiling and current obligations is flying blind.

If you're on a cost-plus contract, your job is compliance and performance. Your fee is fixed — you don't gain from cutting corners. What you do gain from is a strong CPARS rating and a reputation for clean cost accounting. DCAA has the right to your books under FAR 52.215-2. Don't be slow with access. Don't be sloppy with timecards. The contractors who build good DCAA relationships treat it like any professional review: open, transparent, documented.

One more thing: your fixed fee is a dollar amount, not a percentage. If your program runs 30% over cost estimate, your fee as a percentage of total cost just dropped. Understand your economics before you sign.

What this means for government PMs

The pressure to use fixed-price is real and it's not going away. But don't force it when it doesn't fit. A CPFF contract that's well-managed and honestly scoped will cost less than an FFP contract that gets modified 15 times because nobody could define requirements at award.

If you're on a cost-plus program: implement EVM, run structured technical reviews, and demand that the contractor's accounting system is DCAA-compliant before you start invoicing. These aren't optional when the government is holding the cost risk.

If you're targeting a transition to fixed-price: requirements need to be mature, costs need to be estimable, and you need a contractor who's actually built a version of this before. Rushing the transition to hit a policy metric is how programs end up with underbid fixed-price contracts and years of restructuring.
