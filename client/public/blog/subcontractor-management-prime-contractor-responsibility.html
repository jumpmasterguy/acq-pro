Subcontractor Management: What the Prime Owes the Government (And What You Actually Control)

Your subcontractor misses a deliverable. The government calls you — not them. Understanding what you actually own in the prime-sub relationship is how you avoid that call.

By Lucas Cruz · May 2026 · 9 min read · For: Contractor PMs

Let's start with the most important sentence in this entire post:

The prime contractor is accountable to the government for all work on the contract — including everything done by subcontractors.

Full stop. No exceptions. The government has no privity of contract with your subs. They don't know your subs, they didn't negotiate with your subs, and they have no obligation to deal with your subs when something goes wrong. You, the prime, signed the contract. You own the outcome.

That's not just a legal technicality. It's the operating reality that should drive every decision you make about how you structure, monitor, and manage your subcontractor relationships.

From Lucas

The most common trap I've watched contractor PMs fall into: they treat the sub like a vendor handling their own little corner of the world, and assume that if the sub has a contract to perform, the sub will figure it out. They won't always. And when they don't, it doesn't matter how good your working relationship with the sub is — what matters is what you have in writing, how closely you were monitoring, and how fast you escalate. The government judges the prime. Only the prime.

Privity of Contract: Why the Prime Is Always on the Hook

Privity of contract means there is a direct legal relationship between two parties who signed an agreement together. In federal contracting, the government has privity with the prime contractor only.

When your subcontractor performs poorly — misses a milestone, delivers a deficient product, fails a security check — the government's remedy is against you. They can issue a cure notice to you. They can assess liquidated damages against you. They can document your performance in CPARS — which reflects on you, not the sub. They can terminate for default — your contract, not the sub's subcontract.

Some PMs think: "We'll just put the blame on the sub." That doesn't work. Your CPARS narrative might say "performance challenges were attributable to a subcontractor," but the adjectival rating — Satisfactory, Marginal, Unsatisfactory — goes on your record. The source selection board at your next competition doesn't see "the sub was at fault." They see your rating.

Flowdown Clauses: The Legal Bridge from Prime to Sub

Even though the government can't directly enforce contract terms against your sub, they can require you to push those obligations down through what are called flowdown clauses.

A flowdown clause is a provision from your prime contract that you're required to include — word for word, or appropriately adapted — in your subcontracts. The purpose is to ensure your subs are operating under the same legal and regulatory framework as you are.

Some clauses must be flowed down to all subs regardless of dollar value or contract type. Others are triggered by specific thresholds or circumstances. Here's a simplified breakdown:

Clause Category Common Examples Flowdown Requirement
Mandatory (all subcontracts) FAR 52.222-26 (Equal Opportunity), FAR 52.222-21 (Prohibition on Segregated Facilities), Drug-Free Workplace Must be included regardless of dollar value
Mandatory above thresholds FAR 52.204-21 (Basic Safeguarding of CUI), DFARS 252.204-7012 (Cyber — CMMC requirements), Davis-Bacon (construction over $2K), Service Contract Act Required when the dollar value or work type meets the threshold
Situational / program-specific Export control requirements (ITAR, EAR), security classification requirements, government property clauses, quality system requirements Required when the work involves the covered area (classified, export-controlled, GFP, etc.)
CPARS-related obligations Performance reporting requirements, right to audit, access to records Required when the subcontract value and type trigger FAR 42.15 reporting thresholds

Failing to flow down required clauses is a contract compliance issue. In a DCMA review or CPARS evaluation, missing flowdowns can be cited as a management deficiency — which can drag down your rating. More practically: if your sub violates an obligation you were supposed to flow down, you may be held responsible for the violation.

The right practice: have a contract administrator review every new subcontract to confirm required flowdowns are included. Don't leave it to judgment calls in the field.

How to Actually Monitor Subcontractor Performance

Monitoring a sub isn't the same as checking in once a month to see how things are going. Effective subcontractor oversight means you have visibility into their performance before the government asks you about it.

Establish Reporting Requirements Early

Your subcontract should specify what the sub is required to report, how often, and in what format. At minimum, this typically means: schedule status against major milestones, cost performance (on cost-type subcontracts), deliverable status, and any known risks or issues. If your sub's subcontract doesn't require them to report, they won't volunteer bad news.

Track Deliverables Against a Baseline

Create a deliverable tracking log for each sub. Every CDRL (Contract Data Requirements List item) or key milestone that the sub owns should be on that log with its due date, current status, and any slippage notes. This is not optional paperwork — it's your evidence trail. If DCMA or the CO asks you how your sub is performing, you should be able to produce this log in five minutes.

Hold Regular Performance Reviews

Monthly at minimum for complex or high-value subcontracts. Agenda items should include: deliverable status, technical progress, budget status, upcoming milestones, and open issues. Document these reviews in writing — even a brief email summary of what was discussed counts. You want a record that you were actively managing.

Keep the Sub's Qualifications File Current

Verify that the sub's key personnel are still on the team. If they proposed a particular engineer with a specific clearance or certification, and that person left six months in, you have a problem — especially if those qualifications were cited in your proposal's staffing plan. Monitor staffing changes proactively, not reactively.

Common Sub Failure Modes — and How to Catch Them Early

The Silent Sub

You haven't heard from them in a few weeks. They're not raising issues. Everything seems fine. This is often a warning sign, not a good sign. Subs who go quiet are usually either in over their heads or managing a problem they're hoping will resolve itself before they have to tell you. Proactive outreach beats reactive fire drills every time.

Deliverable Slip Without Warning

The sub misses a milestone and mentions it in passing on the status call. If you don't have formal reporting requirements and a tracking log, you may not have caught this early enough to buffer your own delivery to the government. By the time you know, you're already late. The fix: don't wait for the government to ask. Flag it yourself and manage it upward proactively.

Qualifications Don't Match the Proposal

The sub proposed a cleared, certified team. What showed up was a team of newer employees learning on the job. This happens more often than people want to admit. If your proposal cited specific personnel qualifications, the government will hold you to them — and if there's a compliance review, they'll ask for proof. Verify sub staffing at kickoff and monitor it throughout performance.

Cybersecurity and CMMC Compliance Gaps

CMMC requirements flow down to subs who handle Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI). A sub who isn't compliant isn't just a compliance risk for them — it's a risk to your contract and potentially a disqualifying issue for re-competition. Know your subs' compliance status. Don't assume they've handled it.

Protecting Your CPARS When Sub Performance Is a Factor

Your CPARS rating reflects your performance — including how well you managed your subcontractors. If a sub contributes to a schedule slip, a quality deficiency, or a compliance issue, it will appear in your CPARS narrative. Here's how to protect your record when sub performance becomes a factor.

Document everything in real time. Every time you identify a sub performance issue, write it down. Dates, descriptions, what you directed the sub to do, and how they responded. If the issue surfaces in a CPARS evaluation, you need a documented record that shows you managed it actively — not that you let it slide.

Escalate proactively to the COR. Don't wait for the COR to find out about a sub issue through a deliverable review. Tell them yourself — with your mitigation plan already in hand. A PM who surfaces problems early with a solution is a very different CPARS narrative than one who gets caught hiding them.

Take corrective action and document it. When a sub misses a milestone, document the cure notice or written direction you issued. Show that you enforced your subcontract, not just that you complained about the sub. The government's evaluators want to see that you had management control over your team.

Respond to the draft CPARS if sub performance is cited. You have 14 days to respond to a draft CPARS evaluation before it's finalized. If the narrative attributes performance failures to a sub, respond with specific evidence of how you managed the situation, what you directed the sub to do, and what corrective actions were taken. Your response becomes part of the permanent record.

The Small Business Subcontracting Plan Obligation

Many contracts over the simplified acquisition threshold ($250K) — and virtually all large contracts — require a Small Business Subcontracting Plan under FAR 19.704. This plan commits you to specific percentage goals for small business utilization across categories: small business, small disadvantaged business, woman-owned, veteran-owned, HUBZone, and service-disabled veteran-owned.

The CPARS evaluation area for "Small Business Utilization" checks whether you hit these goals. If you don't, you need to explain why. A consistent pattern of missing SB goals without a good explanation is a CPARS mark that follows you into your next competition — particularly on contracts where the government is evaluating small business commitment as a factor.

As the PM, you should know your plan goals and track them quarterly. Don't find out you missed them at the end of the year when it's too late to do anything about it.
